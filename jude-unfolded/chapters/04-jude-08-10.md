## B. Plain-English Orientation

Having set out three examples of judgment, Jude now applies them. The intruders behave "in like manner." Relying on their dreams, they do three things: they defile the flesh, they reject authority, and they slander heavenly beings.

To expose the arrogance of the third charge, Jude tells a story his readers evidently knew. When Michael, the greatest of the archangels, disputed with the Devil over Moses' body, he did not presume to pronounce judgment on his adversary on his own authority. He left that to God: "The LORD rebuke you!"

The intruders show no such restraint. They mock what they do not understand. Meanwhile, the things they do understand, their instincts and appetites, are understood the way animals understand them, and those very things are destroying them.

The passage turns on a contrast between **presumption** and **reverence**. The intruders claim special insight through dreams, yet they are ignorant of spiritual realities and governed by animal instinct. Michael, who stands in the presence of God, knows the limits of his authority.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### Ὁμοίως μέντοι (*Homoiōs mentoi*) — ‘yet in the same way’

The opening Ὁμοίως μέντοι καὶ οὗτοι (*Homoiōs mentoi kai houtoi*), literally "In the same way, however, these also," ties the intruders to the examples of vv. 5–7. The particle μέντοι (*mentoi*), "nevertheless, yet," adds a note of surprise or protest. *In spite of* these well-known examples of judgment, the intruders do the same things. This is the first of the letter's "these" applications.

### καὶ οὗτοι ἐνυπνιαζόμενοι (*kai houtoi enypniazomenoi*) — ‘these too, dreaming’

The participle ἐνυπνιαζόμενοι (*enypniazomenoi*), "dreaming," stands as the description of the intruders that governs all three of the following verbs. The LSV's "those dreaming indeed defile the flesh" preserves this: the dreaming is the source of the behavior.

What does Jude mean? Some interpreters take it figuratively, as if the intruders lived in a dream world of self-delusion or fantasized about immorality. The background of the word points elsewhere. In the Greek Old Testament, the same verb describes the false prophet of Deuteronomy 13: "When a prophet or a dreamer of a dream arises in your midst … saying, Let us go after other gods … you do not listen to the words of that prophet or to that dreamer of the dream" (Deut 13:1–3, LSV). The Septuagint uses ἐνυπνιαζόμενος, Jude's word, for that "dreamer of a dream."[^c4-1] Jeremiah denounces prophets "who prophesy falsehood in My Name, saying, I have dreamed, I have dreamed" (Jer 23:25, LSV), and God says, "I [am] against the prophets of false dreams" (Jer 23:32, LSV).

Dreams were not suspect in themselves. Joel promised that in the last days "Your old men dream dreams" (Joel 2:28, LSV), and Peter announced the fulfillment of that promise at Pentecost (Acts 2:17). The problem was dreams claimed as divine revelation that led people away from God's revealed will. Jude's intruders seem to have claimed visions or revelations that authorized their conduct. That would explain why Jude stresses that the faith was delivered "once" (v. 3). No new revelation can overturn it.

### σάρκα μὲν μιαίνουσιν (*sarka men miainousin*) — ‘defile the flesh’

The first of three charges, σάρκα μὲν μιαίνουσιν (*sarka men miainousin*), uses the verb μιαίνω (*miainō*), "to stain, defile, soil," in a moral sense.[^c4-2] "The flesh" here means the body. The charge corresponds to Sodom's sexual immorality (v. 7) and to the intruders' turning grace into "licentiousness" (v. 4).

The three charges are linked by the Greek particles μέν … δέ … δέ (*men … de … de*), "on the one hand … on the other … and." The LSV's "indeed … and … and" reflects this. The construction holds the three together as aspects of a single pattern of life.

### κυριότητα δὲ ἀθετοῦσιν (*kyriotēta de athetousin*) — ‘reject lordship’

The second charge, κυριότητα δὲ ἀθετοῦσιν (*kyriotēta de athetousin*), uses ἀθετέω (*atheteō*), "to set aside, reject, nullify,"[^c4-3] with the object κυριότης (*kyriotēs*), "lordship, dominion." What lordship do they reject?

1. **The lordship of the Lord.** The word is derived from κύριος (*kyrios*, "lord"), and Jude has just said that the intruders deny "our only Master and Lord—Jesus Christ" (v. 4). Rejecting "lordship" would then mean rejecting the authority of Christ. Clement of Alexandria read it this way: "despise dominion … that is, the only Lord, who is truly our Lord, Jesus Christ."[^c4-4]
2. **Angelic powers.** In Eph 1:21 and Col 1:16, "lordship" (κυριότης) is one of a list of angelic ranks: "principality, and authority, and might, and lordship" (Eph 1:21, LSV). Since the next charge concerns "glorious [ones]," Jude might mean angelic authorities here too.
3. **Human church leaders.** Some have suggested that the intruders despised the leadership of the community.

The first interpretation fits the immediate context best, since it echoes v. 4. It also fits the contrast with the angels of v. 6, who left their place under God's authority. The singular "lordship," without an article, may also suggest an abstract principle: the intruders reject the very idea of being under authority.

### δόξας δὲ βλασφημοῦσιν (*doxas de blasphēmousin*) — ‘and slander glorious ones’

The third charge is the most puzzling. The verb βλασφημέω (*blasphēmeō*) means "to slander, revile, speak abusively of." The object δόξας (*doxas*), literally "glories," refers to glorious beings, almost certainly angels. The LSV's bracketed "[ones]" signals that the Greek has only the plural noun. Second Peter's parallel confirms the angelic reference: "they are not afraid to speak evil of glorious ones, whereas messengers, being greater in strength and power, do not bear a slanderous judgment against them before the LORD" (2 Pet 2:10–11, LSV).

Why would anyone slander angels? Jude does not say, and scholars have proposed several explanations:

- **Disparaging the angels as guardians of the moral law.** Jewish and early Christian tradition held that the Law of Moses was given "through messengers" (Gal 3:19; Acts 7:53; Heb 2:2). If the intruders taught that grace freed them from the moral law, they might have belittled the angels associated with that law. This is a suggestion drawn from the biblical evidence just cited, not a fact recorded in Jude.[^c4-5]
- **Mocking evil powers.** If the "glorious ones" include fallen angels (some argue this from the Michael example, where the slander concerns the Devil), the intruders may have treated spiritual evil with contempt, claiming that their spiritual status put them beyond its reach.
- **General irreverence** toward the whole unseen order of God's creation.

The example that follows, Michael's restraint toward the Devil, suggests that Jude's main point is the intruders' presumption. Whatever beings they slandered, they spoke with an authority they did not possess.

### ὁ δὲ Μιχαὴλ ὁ ἀρχάγγελος (*ho de Michaēl ho archangelos*) — ‘but Michael the archangel’

Michael is one of only two angels named in the Protestant canon of Scripture; the other is Gabriel. In Daniel he is "Michael, first of the chief heads" and "the great head, who is standing up for the sons of your people" (Dan 10:13; 12:1, LSV), the guardian of Israel. In Revelation, "Michael and his messengers warred against the dragon" (Rev 12:7, LSV). Jude calls him ὁ ἀρχάγγελος (*ho archangelos*), "the archangel," literally "the chief messenger," as the LSV renders it. The only other New Testament use of the word is in 1 Thess 4:16, "with the voice of a chief-messenger" (LSV).

The choice of Michael heightens the contrast. If anyone had the standing to condemn the Devil, it was the commander of heaven's armies.

### ὅτε τῷ διαβόλῳ διακρινόμενος διελέγετο περὶ τοῦ Μωϋσέως σώματος (*hote tō diabolō diakrinomenos dielegeto peri tou Mōuseōs sōmatos*) — ‘when, contending with the devil, he argued about the body of Moses’

Two legal or debating verbs describe the encounter. διακρινόμενος (*diakrinomenos*) means "contending, disputing," often in a judicial sense. διελέγετο (*dielegeto*) means "was arguing, debating." The scene is a dispute, perhaps a kind of court case, "about the body of Moses" (περὶ τοῦ Μωϋσέως σώματος, *peri tou Mōyseōs sōmatos*), over who had rights to it.

The Old Testament says only this about Moses' death: "Moses, servant of the Lord, dies there in the land of Moab, according to the command of YHWH; and He buries him in a valley in the land of Moab, opposite Beth-Peor, and no man has known his burying place to this day" (Deut 34:5–6, LSV). The mysterious burial, done by God himself in an unknown place, invited speculation, and a tradition grew up that angels were involved. Jude's version, the only surviving ancient form of the story that names a dispute between Michael and the Devil, is explored in the deep dive below.

### οὐκ ἐτόλμησεν κρίσιν ἐπενεγκεῖν βλασφημίας (*ouk etolmēsen krisin epenenkein blasphēmias*) — ‘did not dare to bring a slanderous judgment’

The key phrase οὐκ ἐτόλμησεν κρίσιν ἐπενεγκεῖν βλασφημίας (*ouk etolmēsen krisin epenenkein blasphēmias*) can be read in two ways.

1. **"He did not dare to pronounce a slanderous (or abusive) judgment against him."** The genitive βλασφημίας (*blasphēmias*) describes the kind of judgment: an insulting, reviling condemnation. Michael did not abuse the Devil, even though the Devil deserved condemnation. He left judgment to God. This is the traditional reading ("durst not bring against him a railing accusation," WBS) and the one most translations follow.
2. **"He did not presume to condemn him for slander."** The genitive names the charge: Michael did not pronounce a verdict of "slander" against the Devil. On this reading, which draws on an ancient Greek fragment preserved in a catena (a chain of excerpts from early commentators) and on the lost ending of the *Testament of Moses* (see the deep dive), the Devil had slandered Moses by calling him a murderer. Michael, though in the right, did not presume to judge the Devil for this slander and appealed to God instead.[^c4-6]

Both readings reach the same point. Michael, though an archangel and in the right, recognized that judgment over a fellow spiritual being belonged to God alone. The intruders, mere humans, pass judgment freely on beings far above them.

The verb ἐτόλμησεν (*etolmēsen*), "he dared," is important. Jude does not say Michael was unable to condemn the Devil, or that the Devil deserved no condemnation. He says Michael *did not dare*. The restraint was reverence, not weakness.

### ἀλλὰ εἶπεν· Ἐπιτιμήσαι σοι κύριος (*alla eipen: Epitimēsai soi kyrios*) — ‘but said: may the Lord rebuke you’

Michael's words, Ἐπιτιμήσαι σοι κύριος (*Epitimēsai soi kyrios*), use the **optative** of wish: "May the Lord rebuke you!" This is the second optative in Jude, after "be multiplied" in v. 2. The LSV prints "LORD" in capitals, indicating that the reference is to the God of Israel.

The words echo Zechariah's vision of Joshua the high priest, accused by Satan before the angel of the LORD: "And YHWH says to Satan: 'YHWH pushes against you, O Satan, / Indeed, YHWH pushes against you'" (Zech 3:2, LSV). The Greek Septuagint renders the same words as Ἐπιτιμήσαι Κύριος ἐν σοί, διάβολε, "May the Lord rebuke you, O Devil," nearly identical to Jude.[^c4-7] In both scenes, a heavenly figure facing the accuser leaves the rebuke to God.

Zechariah 3 will return in Jude 23: the high priest Joshua is "a brand delivered from fire" and is "clothed with filthy garments" (Zech 3:2–3, LSV), images that reappear in Jude's call to snatch others "out of fire" and to hate the "coat having been stained from the flesh."

### οὗτοι δὲ ὅσα μὲν οὐκ οἴδασιν βλασφημοῦσιν (*houtoi de hosa men ouk oidasin blasphēmousin*) — ‘but these people slander whatever they do not understand’

Verse 10 returns to the intruders, "these" (οὗτοι, *houtoi*), with a pointed contrast built on two verbs of knowing:

- ὅσα μὲν οὐκ οἴδασιν βλασφημοῦσιν (*hosa men ouk oidasin blasphēmousin*), "whatever they do not know, they slander." The verb οἶδα (*oida*) here refers to knowledge of spiritual realities, the unseen world of angels and authority. Those who claimed revelatory dreams are, in Jude's judgment, ignorant of the very realm they speak about.

### ὅσα δὲ φυσικῶς ὡς τὰ ἄλογα ζῷα ἐπίστανται, ἐν τούτοις φθείρονται (*hosa de physikōs hōs ta aloga zōa epistantai, en toutois phtheirontai*) — ‘and what they know by instinct, like irrational animals, is what destroys them’

- ὅσα δὲ φυσικῶς ὡς τὰ ἄλογα ζῷα ἐπίστανται (*hosa de physikōs hōs ta aloga zōa epistantai*), "whatever they understand by instinct, like unreasoning animals." The second verb, ἐπίσταμαι (*epistamai*), means "to understand, know how." The adverb φυσικῶς (*physikōs*), "naturally, by nature,"[^c4-8] found only here in the New Testament, describes knowledge that comes from instinct, not reason or revelation. The phrase τὰ ἄλογα ζῷα (*ta aloga zōa*), "the irrational animals," literally "the creatures without *logos*," without reason or speech, was a stock expression for brute beasts.

Then the verdict: ἐν τούτοις φθείρονται (*en toutois phtheirontai*), "by these things they are destroyed" or "in these they are corrupted." The verb φθείρω (*phtheirō*) means both "to corrupt" and "to destroy, ruin," and Jude may intend both: the appetites they follow corrupt them morally and are leading them to destruction.

The irony is sharp. The intruders claim higher knowledge through dreams. In reality they are ignorant of spiritual things, and the only knowledge they truly possess is the instinctive knowledge of appetite, which they share with animals and which is ruining them. Clement of Alexandria paraphrased: "He means that they eat, and drink, and indulge in uncleanness, and says that they do other things that are common to them with animals, devoid of reason."[^c4-9] Paul's description of those whose "god [is] the belly, and whose glory [is] in their shame" (Phil 3:19, LSV) is similar.

### Deep dive: Michael the archangel and the body of Moses

Jude's reference to a dispute over Moses' body is one of the most intriguing puzzles in the New Testament. The Old Testament contains no such story. Where did it come from?

**What the Bible says.** Deuteronomy 34:5–6 records that Moses died "according to the command of YHWH" (literally "at the mouth of YHWH") and that "He buries him … and no man has known his burying place to this day" (LSV). God himself buried Moses, and the grave was unknown. That combination of divine burial and hidden grave naturally invited questions. Who carried out the burial? Why was the grave hidden? Was there opposition?

**What early Christians said about Jude's source.** Several early Christian writers connected Jude 9 to a specific Jewish book:

- **Clement of Alexandria** (c. 200), commenting on Jude 9, writes: "Here he confirms the assumption of Moses."[^c4-10] The Latin translation preserves the phrase *assumptionem Moysi*, which may refer either to a book (the *Assumption of Moses*) or to the event of Moses' being "taken up."
- **Origen** (c. 230), in *On First Principles*, refers to "the work entitled The Ascension of Moses (a little treatise, of which the Apostle Jude makes mention in his Epistle)," in which "the archangel Michael, when disputing with the devil regarding the body of Moses, says that the serpent, being inspired by the devil, was the cause of Adam and Eve's transgression."[^c4-11]
- Later writers also refer to this work. Didymus the Blind (fourth century), in his exposition of Jude, says that some doubted the canonicity of the letter because of this quotation from an apocryphal book. Gelasius of Cyzicus quotes the *Assumption* in a way that later enabled scholars to identify a surviving manuscript.[^c4-12]

**What survives.** In the mid-nineteenth century Antonio Ceriani discovered in the Ambrosian Library in Milan a single, incomplete sixth-century Latin manuscript of a Jewish work in which Moses, before his death, prophesies Israel's history to Joshua. Ceriani published it in 1861 and identified it, through its agreement with a quotation in Gelasius, as the *Assumption of Moses*. R. H. Charles later argued that the surviving text is better called the *Testament of Moses*, since it contains Moses' farewell speech but no account of his being taken up, and that the testament and the assumption may originally have been distinct works or parts of a combined one. The manuscript breaks off before Moses' death. The dispute between Michael and the Devil does not appear in what survives, and it may have stood in the lost ending.[^c4-13]

**Reconstructing the story.** The most important clue survives in Greek. In a *catena*, a medieval chain of excerpts from early commentators, the story is told this way: when Moses had died on the mountain, "Michael is sent to remove the body; then, when the devil slandered Moses and proclaimed him a murderer because he struck the Egyptian, the angel, not bearing the slander against him, said to the devil, 'May God rebuke you.'" (The Greek reads ὁ Μιχαὴλ ἀποστέλλεται μεταθήσων τὸ σῶμα, εἶτα τοῦ διαβόλου κατὰ τοῦ Μωυσέως βλασφημοῦντος καὶ φονέα ἀναγορεύοντος διὰ τὸ πατάξαι τὸν Αἰγύπτιον, οὐκ ἐνεγκὼν τὴν κατ' αὐτοῦ βλασφημίαν ὁ ἄγγελος, Ἐπιτιμήσαι σοι ὁ Θεός, πρὸς τὸν διάβολον ἔφη.) R. H. Charles summarized the surviving fragments: Michael is commissioned to bury Moses, and Satan opposes the burial on two grounds. First, he claims to be the lord of matter, so the body belongs to him; Michael answers, "The Lord rebuke thee, for it was God's spirit which created the world and all mankind." Second, he brings the charge of murder against Moses, who had killed an Egyptian (Exod 2:11–12).[^c4-14] The tenth-century commentator Oecumenius reports a similar tradition.[^c4-14b] Because the original ending is lost, every reconstruction is provisional. Even the old Cambridge commentary cautioned that such later traditions "may have grown out of the words of St Jude instead of being the foundation on which they rested."

**How did Jude use the story?** Jude uses it as he uses the examples of vv. 5–7: as a known story with a clear moral point. He assumes his readers know it, mentions only what he needs, and draws a single lesson. He does not quote a book by name or introduce the story as Scripture.

**Did Jude believe the event happened?** Interpreters answer this differently.

- Some hold that Jude, writing under the guidance of the Spirit, affirmed a true event preserved in Jewish tradition. Calvin, for example, judged that since "the Jews at that time had many things from the traditions of the fathers," it was not unreasonable to say "that Jude referred to what had already been handed down for many ages."[^c4-15]
- Others hold that Jude used a familiar story as an illustration, much as a modern preacher might cite a well-known parable or a classic novel, without endorsing its historicity. On this view the authority lies in the moral point, not in the narrative details.
- Still others think the question cannot be settled and is not essential to understanding Jude's argument.

All agree on the point Jude draws: *if the archangel Michael did not presume to pronounce judgment on the Devil, how much less should mere humans slander heavenly beings?* Readers may hold different views about the story's origin and still receive its lesson.

**Why did the story matter in Jude's world?** A story about angels disputing over Moses' body would have resonated with Jewish readers for whom Moses was the supreme prophet and lawgiver, and for whom the hidden grave was a sign of his unique relationship with God. In a community debating authority, revelation, and the moral law, a story in which even the great archangel deferred to God's authority over the body of the lawgiver was a pointed choice.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Angels and the law.** Jewish tradition held that angels were present at Sinai and mediated the giving of the Law (Deut 33:2 LXX; Jubilees 1:27–29; Acts 7:53; Gal 3:19; Heb 2:2). Stephen speaks of Israel as those "who received the Law by arrangement of messengers" (Acts 7:53, LSV). If the intruders rejected the moral authority of the law of God, it would be natural for them to speak disparagingly of the angels associated with it.

**Dreams and revelation in the ancient world.** Throughout the ancient Mediterranean, dreams were widely regarded as a channel of divine communication. Jewish tradition accepted that God could speak through dreams, as with Joseph and Daniel, but also warned against dreamers who led people astray (Deut 13; Jer 23). Early Christianity valued prophecy and visions while insisting on testing them (1 Cor 14:29; 1 Thess 5:19–21; 1 John 4:1). The *Didache* warned that a prophet's claims were to be judged by his "ways," his conduct.[^c4-16] Jude's "dreamers" fall under this kind of judgment.

**Testaments and "assumptions."** Jewish literature of the period included many "testaments," farewell discourses of patriarchs before death (the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, the *Testament of Moses*), and stories of figures "taken up" to heaven (Enoch, Elijah, and in some traditions Moses). Jude's references to Moses' body and to Enoch (vv. 14–15) show that he and his readers were at home in this literature.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Deuteronomy 13:1–5; Jeremiah 23:25–32 (background for "dreaming").** The "dreamer of a dream" who leads people to "go after other gods" (Deut 13:1–2, LSV) is the clearest Old Testament background for Jude's term. The verbal link is strongest in the Greek Old Testament, where Jude's word appears. This is probably a deliberate echo, though Jude does not quote the passage.

**Zechariah 3:1–4 (direct allusion).** Michael's words in Jude 9 match the Septuagint of Zech 3:2 almost exactly. Since Zechariah 3 also supplies the imagery of Jude 23, Jude, or his source, seems to have known the whole scene well.

**2 Peter 2:10–12 (literary parallel).** Second Peter generalizes Jude's example: "messengers, being greater in strength and power, do not bear a slanderous judgment against them before the LORD" (2 Pet 2:11, LSV). It omits the names of Michael and Moses and the specific story, perhaps because the story came from a non-canonical source. It also expands Jude's comparison to animals: "as irrational natural beasts, made to be caught and destroyed" (2 Pet 2:12, LSV).

**Daniel 10:13, 21; 12:1; Revelation 12:7–9 (Michael).** These passages establish Michael as Israel's guardian and the leader of the heavenly forces against the dragon. Jude's portrait of a Michael who refuses to presume is all the more striking against that background.

**Matthew 4:10 (shared theme).** When Jesus dismisses Satan in the wilderness, he does so by appealing to Scripture: "it has been written" (Matt 4:10, LSV). Like Michael, he grounds his response in God's authority rather than his own.

**Romans 1:22–24; Philippians 3:19 (shared theme).** Paul's description of those who, "professing to be wise, … were made fools" (Rom 1:22, LSV) and were given up to their desires parallels Jude's portrait of people who claim higher knowledge yet live by animal appetite.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. What is the source of the Michael story, and what does its use imply?** Discussed in the deep dive. The source is most likely a lost ending of the *Testament* or *Assumption of Moses*. Christians have drawn different conclusions about what Jude's use of the story implies about its historical truth.

**2. What is "lordship," and who are the "glorious ones"?** Discussed above. The most likely readings are the Lord's authority and angels, respectively. Alternatives are noted.

**3. What does "a judgment of slander" mean?** "A slanderous judgment" (traditional) or "a condemnation for slander" (reconstructed setting). Both lead to the same point.

**4. Should Christians "rebuke the Devil"?** Some modern Christian practices encourage believers to address and rebuke Satan directly in prayer. Jude 9 has been cited on both sides of this question. Some read it as a model, since Michael's words are a form of rebuke spoken in God's name. Others read it as a caution, since Michael refused to pronounce judgment himself and appealed to God. Jude's own point concerns *slander and presumption*, not techniques of spiritual warfare. The verse most clearly teaches humility: whatever believers say about spiritual evil should be said in dependence on God's authority, not their own.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verse 8 is the first application in the letter's alternating pattern of text and application. Verses 5–7 supplied the text, and v. 8 says "In like manner … those dreaming." Verse 9 then introduces a second "text," the Michael story, and v. 10 applies it: "But these…"

The passage also prepares for what follows. The animal comparison of v. 10 anticipates the woe on those who follow Cain, Balaam, and Korah (v. 11). The theme of slanderous speech anticipates the "harsh [words]" of v. 15 and the "swollen words" of v. 16. The phrase "they understand naturally" anticipates the description of the intruders in v. 19 as ψυχικοί (*psychikoi*), "natural" people "not having the Spirit." In Jude's view, the intruders' claimed spirituality is in fact the opposite of spirituality.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Testing claimed revelations.** Movements in every generation claim new revelations, special dreams, or private words from God that set aside the received faith or its moral teaching. Jude, like Deuteronomy and the *Didache*, suggests a test. Does the claimed revelation lead toward the faith "once delivered" and toward holiness, or away from them? Christian traditions differ on the place of ongoing prophecy in the church. They can agree that no experience authorizes a believer to "defile the flesh" or reject the lordship of Christ.

**Reverence in speech about spiritual realities.** Michael's restraint may challenge both careless mockery of spiritual realities and presumptuous bravado toward them. Christians may speak about evil truthfully and confidently, yet without contempt or self-reliance, leaving final judgment to God.

**The danger of contempt.** Jude's charge is that the intruders "speak evil of" what they do not understand. Contempt for what one has not understood is a common failing in religious and political disputes today. Jude's example invites believers to examine their own speech, including their speech about the people Jude warns against.

**Appetite and identity.** Jude's picture of people who know their appetites "like the irrational beasts" may prompt reflection on how far modern life is shaped by instinctive desire, often deliberately cultivated by commerce and media, and on how Christian discipleship offers a different kind of knowledge and freedom.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Jude describes the intruders as "dreaming," probably meaning that they claimed revelations that justified their behavior. How can individuals and communities test claims to spiritual experience today?
2. Why do you think Jude chose a story about Michael and the Devil to make his point about slander? What would be lost if he had simply said, "Do not slander angels"?
3. Michael "did not dare" to pronounce judgment, even on the Devil. What does this suggest about the attitude believers should have toward those they consider enemies of God?
4. Jude contrasts two kinds of knowledge: ignorance of spiritual realities and instinctive knowledge of appetite. Where do you see these two kinds of "knowing" in your own life or culture?
5. How do you respond to the fact that Jude draws on a story not found in the Old Testament? Does it affect how you read the letter? Why or why not?
