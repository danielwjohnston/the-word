## B. Plain-English Orientation

Jude now speaks as a prophet. He pronounces a "woe" over the intruders, an announcement of coming judgment mixed with lament. He gives the reason by comparing them to three notorious figures from Israel's scriptures:

- **Cain**, the first murderer, who killed his brother out of envy;
- **Balaam**, the prophet-for-hire, who led Israel into immorality and idolatry for profit;
- **Korah**, the Levite who rebelled against the leadership God had appointed and was swallowed by the earth.

Each figure stands for a kind of sin that Jude sees in the intruders. The three verbs also trace a progression: they *went* in Cain's way, they *abandoned themselves* to Balaam's error, and they *perished* in Korah's rebellion. Jude speaks of their destruction as if it had already happened, because in his view it is as certain as Korah's was.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### οὐαὶ αὐτοῖς (*ouai autois*) — ‘woe to them’

The interjection οὐαί (*ouai*), "woe!" or "alas!", is the classic opening of a prophetic oracle of judgment. Isaiah pronounces a series of them: "Woe [to] those saying to evil 'good,' and to good 'evil'" (Isa 5:20, LSV). Habakkuk, Amos, and other prophets use the same form. Jesus pronounced woes on the rich and on the scribes and Pharisees (Luke 6:24–26; Matt 23:13–36). The Epistle of Enoch, part of 1 Enoch that Jude knew, contains long series of woes against sinners: "Woe to those who build unrighteousness and oppression / And lay deceit as a foundation; / For they shall be suddenly overthrown" (1 Enoch 94:6, trans. Charles).[^c5-1]

Outside the Gospels and Revelation, the word "woe" appears in the New Testament only twice: in Paul's "woe is to me if I may not proclaim good news" (1 Cor 9:16, LSV) and here.[^c5-2] Jude's is the only woe pronounced against others in the New Testament letters. It marks this verse as the emotional and prophetic climax of the first half of his argument.

A woe is not a curse that the speaker wishes on someone. It is a solemn announcement that judgment is coming. It often carries a note of grief. The English "alas" catches some of that tone.

### ὅτι τῇ ὁδῷ τοῦ Κάϊν ἐπορεύθησαν (*hoti tē hodō tou Kain eporeuthēsan*) — ‘for they went the way of Cain’

The first verb, ἐπορεύθησαν (*eporeuthēsan*), "they went, walked," pictures a course of life, a "way" (ὁδός, *hodos*) followed by choice. To walk in someone's way is to follow that person's example. The phrase τῇ ὁδῷ τοῦ Κάϊν (*tē hodō tou Kain*), "the way of Cain," does not refer only to one act of murder. It refers to the kind of life Cain represents.

What did Cain represent? Genesis tells the bare story: Cain's offering was not accepted, he became angry, and he killed his brother Abel. When God asked where Abel was, Cain replied, "I have not known; am I my brother's keeper?" (Gen 4:9, LSV). The deep dive below shows how Jewish and Christian interpreters filled out the picture. For Jude's purpose, three features are most likely in view:

- **Envy and hatred of a brother.** First John draws exactly this lesson: "not as Cain—he was of the evil one, and he slew his brother. And for [what] reason did he slay him? Because his works were evil, and those of his brother [were] righteous" (1 John 3:12, LSV). Jude's intruders are divisive (v. 19), and they sit at the love feasts while betraying their meaning (v. 12).
- **Cynicism about judgment.** Jewish tradition remembered Cain as the first person to deny that God judges. In the Targum's retelling, Cain says to Abel, "There is neither judgment nor Judge, nor another world; nor will good reward be given to the righteous, nor vengeance be taken of the wicked."[^c5-3] Jude's intruders, the "scoffers" of v. 18, live as if judgment were not real.
- **Corrupting others.** Josephus describes Cain after his exile as one who "excited his acquaintance to procure pleasures and spoils by robbery, and became a great leader of men into wicked courses."[^c5-4] Jude's intruders lead others astray.

Jude does not say which aspect he means. He may intend the whole tradition. Cain is the first person in Scripture to turn against God's order and against his brother, the father of a "way" that others follow.

### καὶ τῇ πλάνῃ τοῦ Βαλαὰμ μισθοῦ ἐξεχύθησαν (*kai tē planē tou Balaam misthou exechythēsan*) — ‘and for pay they poured themselves into the error of Balaam’

The second verb is vivid. ἐξεχύθησαν (*exechythēsan*) is the passive of ἐκχέω (*ekcheō*), "to pour out." Used of people it means "they were poured out," that is, they gave themselves up wholly or abandoned themselves.[^c5-5] The LSV's "rushed" catches the headlong motion. The ASV has "ran riotously" (ASV), Webster "run greedily" (WBS), and the BSB "rushed headlong" (BSB). The intruders did not drift into Balaam's error. They threw themselves into it.

"The deceit of Balaam" translates τῇ πλάνῃ τοῦ Βαλαάμ (*tē planē tou Balaam*). The noun πλάνη (*planē*) means "error, wandering, deception," the state of going astray and leading others astray. It belongs to the same word family as the "stars going astray" (ἀστέρες πλανῆται, *asteres planētai*) of v. 13.

The phrase μισθοῦ (*misthou*), "for reward" or "for pay," is a genitive of price: they abandoned themselves to error *for wages*. Balaam was famous for being hired. Moab's king Balak sent elders "with divinations in their hand," payment for the seer's services (Num 22:7, LSV), and promised to honor him greatly (Num 22:17). Deuteronomy remembers that Moab "hired against you Balaam son of Beor … to revile you" (Deut 23:4, LSV). Second Peter summarizes: Balaam "loved a reward of unrighteousness" (2 Pet 2:15, LSV).

Balaam's "error" was more than greed. According to Numbers, after he failed to curse Israel, it was "through the word of Balaam" that the Israelites were led "to cause a trespass against YHWH in the matter of Peor" (Num 31:16, LSV), the episode in which Israelite men engaged in sexual immorality with Moabite and Midianite women and joined in the worship of their gods (Num 25:1–3). Revelation puts the tradition plainly: Balaam "taught Balak to cast a stumbling-block before the sons of Israel, to eat idol-sacrifices, and to commit whoredom" (Rev 2:14, LSV). For Jude's readers, Balaam was the prophet who, for profit, taught God's people that immorality was permissible. The parallel with the intruders, who turn grace into licentiousness (v. 4) and flatter people "for the sake of profit" (v. 16), is exact.

### καὶ τῇ ἀντιλογίᾳ τοῦ Κόρε ἀπώλοντο (*kai tē antilogia tou Kore apōlonto*) — ‘and perished in the rebellion of Korah’

The third clause turns to rebellion. "Controversy" translates ἀντιλογίᾳ (*antilogia*), literally "speaking against," hence "contradiction, dispute, rebellion."[^c5-6] The LSV's "controversy" is literal. Most translations have "rebellion" (WEB, BSB) or "gainsaying" (ASV, WBS).

The word carries an echo that Jude's Greek-reading audience may have heard. In the Septuagint, ἀντιλογία is the word used for the "water of contention" at Meribah, where Israel quarreled with Moses and God: Τοῦτο τὸ ὕδωρ Ἀντιλογίας, ‘This is the water of Contention’ (Num 20:13 LXX; also Num 27:14; Deut 32:51; 33:8). It is also used for legal disputes brought before judges (Exod 18:16; Deut 17:8; 19:17).[^c5-7] The word suggests a challenge to God-given authority.

Korah's story is told in Numbers 16. A Levite, already privileged to serve at the tabernacle, he led two hundred and fifty leaders of the congregation against Moses and Aaron. His slogan sounded spiritual and egalitarian: "Enough of you! For all the congregation—all of them [are] holy, and YHWH [is] in their midst; and why do you lift yourselves up above the assembly of YHWH?" (Num 16:3, LSV). Moses saw through it: "is it little to you that the God of Israel has separated you … and you have also sought the priesthood! Therefore, you and all your congregation are meeting against YHWH" (Num 16:9–11, LSV). The judgment was spectacular: "the earth opens her mouth and swallows them … and they go down—they and all that they have—alive to Sheol … and they perish from the midst of the assembly" (Num 16:32–33, LSV), while fire consumed the two hundred and fifty who offered incense (16:35).

The verb ἀπώλοντο (*apōlonto*), "they perished," is in the aorist, a past tense. Jude speaks of the intruders as if they had *already* perished. Grammarians sometimes call this a "prophetic" use of the past tense: a future event is described as already accomplished because it is so certain. In a sense they perished when they joined Korah's rebellion, as Korah's company was doomed the moment it set itself against God. The word ἀπόλλυμι (*apollymi*) also links back to v. 5, where Jesus "destroyed" (ἀπώλεσεν, *apōlesen*) the unbelieving wilderness generation.

### The progression of the three verbs

Read in sequence, the three verbs describe a downward path:

| Verb | Figure | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| ἐπορεύθησαν, "they went" | Cain | Setting out on a way of life |
| ἐξεχύθησαν, "they rushed / abandoned themselves" | Balaam | Throwing oneself into it wholeheartedly |
| ἀπώλοντο, "they perished" | Korah | Reaching its end |

The sins also escalate. Cain's sin was personal: hatred, murder, and contempt for judgment. Balaam's sin led others into sin for profit. Korah's sin openly defied God's appointed order. The intruders, Jude suggests, embody all three.

**A caution about "prophetic aorist."** Not every past tense in prophecy is a "prophetic aorist." Here, though, the context makes the category apt. Jude has just said the intruders' judgment is "written beforehand" (v. 4) and is about to quote Enoch's prophecy of judgment in the past tense: "Behold, the LORD has come" (v. 14). The past tense expresses certainty.

### Deep dive: Cain, Balaam, and Korah in Scripture and tradition

The three figures had rich afterlives in Jewish and Christian interpretation, and Jude's readers would have heard his brief allusions against that background.

**Cain.**

- *In Genesis* (4:1–16), Cain is the firstborn of Adam and Eve and a tiller of the ground. His offering is not accepted. God warns him that sin is crouching at the door but that he must rule over it (4:7). He kills Abel, denies responsibility, and is sentenced to be "a wanderer, even a trembling one" (4:12, LSV), though God marks him for protection.
- *In the New Testament*, Cain is the archetype of hatred: "not as Cain—he was of the evil one" (1 John 3:12, LSV). Hebrews contrasts Abel's faith with Cain's lack of it: "By faith Abel offered a better sacrifice to God than Cain" (Heb 11:4, LSV). Jesus speaks of "all the righteous blood … from the blood of Abel the righteous" (Matt 23:35, LSV).
- *In Jewish tradition*, Cain became the prototype of several vices. Josephus portrays him as "wholly intent upon getting," the inventor of "measures and weights," who "changed the world into cunning craftiness" and "became a great leader of men into wicked courses."[^c5-8] The Palestinian Targums turn the brothers' conversation in the field into a theological argument, with Cain denying that there is "judgment" or a "Judge" or "another world," and Abel affirming them, before Cain kills him.[^c5-9] (The Targums as we have them were written down later than Jude, though they preserve older traditions. Their witness here is to a line of interpretation, not proof of what Jude had read.)

Any of these portraits could stand behind Jude's "way of Cain." Jealous hatred fits the divisions in v. 19. Leading others into sin and greed fit vv. 11–12 and 16. Denial of judgment fits the "scoffers" of v. 18. Jude's brevity leaves the allusion open, and that openness allows it to cover the full range of the intruders' behavior.

**Balaam.**

- *In Numbers 22–24*, Balaam is a non-Israelite seer from Pethor on the Euphrates whom Balak, king of Moab, hires to curse Israel. Repeatedly, he insists, "If Balak gives to me the fullness of his house of silver and gold, I am not able to pass over the command of YHWH" (Num 24:13, LSV), and he ends up blessing Israel. His donkey sees the angel that he cannot see and speaks to him (22:21–35). Read by itself, the story is ambiguous. Balaam obeys God's word in the end, but his eagerness to go, and God's anger at his going (22:22), suggest mixed motives.
- *The rest of Scripture* resolves the ambiguity negatively. Numbers 31:16 blames Balaam's counsel for the apostasy at Peor. Deuteronomy, Nehemiah, and Joshua remember him as hired to curse Israel and as "the diviner" killed among Israel's enemies (Deut 23:4–5; Neh 13:2; Josh 13:22). Micah calls Israel to "remember … What Balaam son of Beor answered" Balak (Mic 6:5, LSV).
- *In Jewish tradition*, Josephus has Balaam advise the Midianites to send their most beautiful daughters to the Israelite camp to seduce the young men into abandoning their law and worshipping other gods, "for by this means God will be angry at them."[^c5-10]
- *In the New Testament*, Balaam becomes the type of the false teacher who is motivated by money and leads believers into immorality and idolatry (2 Pet 2:15–16; Rev 2:14).

Jude's two words, "error" and "reward," compress this whole tradition. The intruders are teachers who, for personal gain, mislead God's people into sexual immorality while claiming spiritual authority.

**Korah.**

- *In Numbers 16*, Korah, a Levite of the clan of Kohath, joined with the Reubenites Dathan, Abiram, and On to challenge Moses' leadership and Aaron's priesthood. God vindicated Moses: the earth swallowed the rebels, and fire consumed the two hundred and fifty incense-offerers. Afterward, God confirmed Aaron's priesthood by making his rod blossom, so that "the murmurings of the sons of Israel" would cease (Num 17:5, LSV).
- *Elsewhere in Scripture*, Korah's rebellion is remembered as "striving against YHWH" (Num 26:9, LSV) and as envy: "they are envious of Moses in the camp, / Of Aaron, YHWH's holy one" (Ps 106:16, LSV). Ben Sira recalls how "the congregation of Core" maligned Aaron "with fury and wrath" and were "consumed … with the fiery flame" (Sir 45:18–19, Brenton). Remarkably, "the sons of Korah did not die" (Num 26:11, LSV). Their descendants became temple singers, and several psalms bear their name.
- *In Jewish tradition*, Korah became the archetype of the schismatic who challenges legitimate authority under the guise of spiritual equality.

Korah's slogan, "all the congregation—all of them [are] holy," sounds like something the New Testament itself might say, and in a sense it does (1 Pet 2:9). That is what makes him a fitting type for the intruders. Their rejection of authority (v. 8) may have been dressed in the language of spiritual freedom and equality. Korah shows that true statements can be used to justify rebellion against God's order.

**Why these three?** All three are figures who opposed God's purposes while associated in some way with God's people or God's word: a son of Adam, a prophet, and a Levite. All three led others, or tried to lead others, with them. All three met judgment. Together they show that the intruders' danger lies not only in their own sin but in their influence on others.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Prophetic speech in the early church.** Early Christian communities recognized prophets alongside apostles and teachers (Acts 13:1; 1 Cor 12:28; Eph 4:11). Jude's woe oracle shows him speaking with prophetic authority, in the tradition of Israel's prophets and of Jesus. His opponents, the "dreamers," may also have claimed prophetic gifts. Jude answers prophecy with prophecy, but his prophecy is anchored in Scripture.

**Paid religious teachers.** In the Greco-Roman world, philosophers and religious specialists often taught for fees, and critics of the time routinely mocked them for greed. Early Christians debated how traveling ministers should be supported. Paul defended the right to support but often declined it so as not to burden or mislead (1 Cor 9:3–18; 1 Thess 2:5–9). The *Didache* warns that a traveling apostle who "ask[s] money" is a false prophet.[^c5-11] Balaam was a fitting type for teachers whose message bent to their income.

**Interpreting Israel's stories as types.** Jewish and early Christian teachers commonly read figures from Israel's history as patterns (Greek τύποι, *typoi*, "types") for the present. Paul says of the wilderness stories, "these things happened to those persons as types, and they were written for our admonition" (1 Cor 10:11, LSV). Jude's three figures are used in exactly this way.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**1 John 3:11–12 (shared tradition).** John's use of Cain as the antithesis of brotherly love is the closest New Testament parallel to Jude's reference. Both writers assume that their readers know Cain as a type of the person who belongs to the evil one.

**2 Peter 2:15–16 (literary parallel).** Peter expands Jude's Balaam reference, mentioning Balaam's love of "a reward of unrighteousness" and the "mute donkey" that "forbid the madness of the prophet" (LSV). He omits Cain and Korah.

**Revelation 2:14 (shared tradition).** The letter to Pergamum names "those holding the teaching of Balaam," which Revelation defines as eating idol-sacrifices and committing sexual immorality. The parallel shows that Balaam had become a standard type of the antinomian teacher in early Christianity.

**Numbers 16; Psalm 106:16–18; Sirach 45:18–19 (Korah).** The core narrative and its later echoes. The theme of envy in Ps 106:16 parallels the grumbling of Jude 16.

**Isaiah 5; Luke 6:24–26; Matthew 23 (woes).** The prophetic and dominical woes provide the form of Jude's oracle.

**1 Corinthians 10:1–11 (method).** Paul's typological reading of the wilderness narratives, including the warning "neither murmur, as also some of them murmured, and perished by the destroyer" (1 Cor 10:10, LSV), parallels Jude's method and some of his material.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Which aspect of Cain does Jude intend?** Interpreters differ. Some emphasize hatred and murder (following 1 John), some the corruption of others (following Josephus), and some the denial of judgment (following the Targums). J. B. Mayor noted that for Philo, Cain is "the type of selfishness," and that 1 John names him "as a type of jealous hate."[^c5-12] The allusion may be deliberately broad.

**2. Is the aorist "they perished" prophetic?** Most commentators take it so. An alternative view holds that Jude regards the intruders' fate as already sealed by their choice, in the way Korah's company was doomed as soon as it set itself against Moses.

**3. How does Jude's use of Balaam relate to Numbers 22–24?** Numbers 22–24 portrays Balaam ambiguously, and other passages (Num 31; Deut 23; Neh 13; Josh 13) portray him negatively. Jude relies on the latter. This shows that the New Testament writers read individual passages in light of the whole canon and of traditional interpretation.

**4. Is authority itself sacred?** Jude's use of Korah could be misused to defend any human authority against criticism. Moses and Aaron's authority in Numbers 16 was explicitly God-given and confirmed by God. Korah's sin was rebellion against *God's* appointment, not criticism of human leaders as such. The Bible elsewhere honors prophets who challenged corrupt leaders (Nathan, Elijah, Jeremiah). Applying Jude 11 requires discernment about whether authority is truly from God and exercised under God.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verse 11 is the climax of the section that began in v. 8. The "dreamers" of v. 8, who slander what they do not understand and are ruled by instinct (v. 10), are now placed in a line of biblical rebels. The woe also prepares for the dense metaphors of vv. 12–13, which open "These are…" and describe the intruders' character and fate. The three verbs of v. 11, *went*, *rushed*, and *perished*, recall the movement of the earlier examples, from salvation to destruction (v. 5).

The three figures also connect with later verses. Balaam's "reward" anticipates the intruders' flattery "for the sake of profit" (v. 16). Korah's "murmuring" (Num 16:11; 17:5) anticipates the "discontented grumblers" of v. 16. Cain's "way" anticipates their "walking according to their own lusts" (vv. 16, 18).

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Three enduring temptations.** Cain, Balaam, and Korah represent temptations that recur in every religious community: resentment and hatred toward fellow believers; using spiritual influence for personal gain; and rejecting God's order while using spiritual language to justify it. Jude's woe may invite communities to examine themselves before they examine others.

**Religion and money.** Balaam's "reward" remains a live issue. Churches and ministries today face questions about how leaders are supported, how money shapes messages, and how marketing can bend teaching. Jude does not condemn all payment for ministry; other New Testament writers defend it. He does condemn error embraced for profit.

**Equality as a cover for rebellion.** Korah's slogan, "all the congregation—all of them [are] holy," was true and yet was used to justify defiance of God's appointment. Believers may need discernment to recognize when good principles are being used for self-promotion, while remaining open to legitimate challenges to leaders who abuse their position.

**The weight of influence.** All three figures harmed others. Teachers, parents, leaders, and anyone with influence may consider that their "way" becomes a path others walk.
