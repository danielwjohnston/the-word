# Chapter 3. Three Ancient Warnings: The Exodus, the Angels, and Sodom (Jude 5–7)

## A. The Text

> **5** Now I intend to remind you, you once having known all this, that Jesus, having saved a people out of the land of Egypt, He secondly destroyed those not having believed; **6** messengers also, those who did not keep their own principality, but left their proper dwelling, He has kept in eternal bonds under darkness until [the] judgment of [the] great day, **7** as Sodom and Gomorrah, and the cities around them, in like manner to these [messengers], having given themselves to whoredom, and having gone after other flesh, have been set before [as] an example, undergoing the justice of continuous fire.
>
> — Jude 5–7, LSV

## B. Plain-English Orientation

Jude now begins to show why the intruders' judgment was "written beforehand" (v. 4). He reminds his readers of three well-known examples of God's judgment:

1. **The wilderness generation.** The people whom the Lord saved from Egypt were later destroyed when they refused to trust him (v. 5).
2. **The rebellious angels.** Heavenly beings who abandoned their assigned place are now held in darkness until the final judgment (v. 6).
3. **Sodom and Gomorrah.** The cities that pursued sexual immorality and "other flesh" now stand as a warning example of fiery judgment (v. 7).

Each example concerns people or beings who had a privileged position. Israel had been redeemed, the angels had a heavenly station, and Sodom and its neighbors lived in a fertile, well-watered land (Gen 13:10). Each abandoned that position through unbelief, rebellion, or immorality, and each came under judgment. The implication for the intruders, and for anyone tempted to follow them, is plain. Belonging to God's people does not guarantee safety for those who turn grace into license.

Jude's opening, "I intend to remind you," matters too. He is not telling his readers anything new. He is calling to mind what they already know, in a way that makes its present relevance unmistakable.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### "Now I intend to remind you, you once having known all this"

The verb ὑπομνῆσαι (*hypomnēsai*), "to remind," places Jude in the role of a teacher who refreshes memory rather than introducing novelty. That role fits his claim that the faith was delivered "once" (v. 3). The readers already "know all this," literally "having known all things once" (εἰδότας ὑμᾶς ἅπαξ πάντα, *eidotas hymas hapax panta*). The participle εἰδότας (*eidotas*), "knowing," is concessive: *although* you know. Second Peter uses the same courtesy, "I will always be ready to remind you about these things, although you have known [them]" (2 Pet 1:12, LSV).

The word ἅπαξ (*hapax*), "once" or "once for all," has wandered in the manuscripts. In some it stands with "knowing" ("you who once for all knew all things"). In others it moves into the next clause ("the Lord, having *once* saved a people"). The SBLGNT and NA28 place it with "knowing," and the LSV follows them: "you once having known all this." The echo of v. 3 is deliberate: the faith was delivered *once for all*, and the readers *once for all* came to know it. What remains is to remember.

### "that Jesus, having saved a people out of the land of Egypt"

This is one of the most remarkable phrases in the New Testament, and one of the most debated. The LSV reads **"Jesus."** Many other translations read **"the Lord"** (WEB, ASV, KJV), and the BSB reads "Jesus" with a note. The difference is not a translation choice. It reflects a difference in the Greek manuscripts, explored in the deep dive below.

If "Jesus" is original, Jude says that Jesus himself, before his human birth, was the one who delivered Israel from Egypt. That is a striking claim, but not an isolated one. Paul says that the Israelites in the wilderness "were drinking of a spiritual rock following them, and the rock was the Christ" (1 Cor 10:4, LSV), and he warns, "neither may we tempt the Christ, as also certain of them tempted" (1 Cor 10:9, LSV). John says that Isaiah "saw His glory, and spoke of Him" (John 12:41, LSV), referring to Jesus and to Isaiah's vision of the LORD in the temple. Jude's reading belongs to the same early Christian conviction that the one revealed in Jesus was already active in Israel's history.

The verb σώσας (*sōsas*), "having saved," is the ordinary New Testament word for salvation. Its use here recalls the language of the exodus itself: "YHWH saves Israel out of the hand of the Egyptians in that day" (Exod 14:30, LSV). The exodus was Israel's defining experience of salvation. Jude's point depends on this. The people who were later destroyed were not outsiders; they had been *saved*.

### "He secondly destroyed those not having believed"

The phrase τὸ δεύτερον (*to deuteron*) literally means "the second time" or "secondly." It can simply mean "afterward" (WEB: "afterward destroyed"), but its natural sense sets two acts side by side: first salvation, then destruction. The LSV keeps the literal "secondly."

The reference is to the wilderness generation, which refused to enter the promised land after the report of the spies. God says to Moses, "Until when do they not believe in Me, for all the signs which I have done in its midst?" (Num 14:11, LSV). The sentence follows: "Your carcasses fall in this wilderness … who have murmured against Me" (Num 14:29, LSV). The aorist participle "those not having believed" (τοὺς μὴ πιστεύσαντας, *tous mē pisteusantas*) sums up the whole episode.

The warning in Jude's first example is the most pointed of the three, because it concerns *God's own redeemed people*. The intruders are inside the church. They share its meals and claim its grace. Jude's readers might suppose that being among the saved is protection enough. The wilderness generation shows otherwise. The early church used this example often: Paul in 1 Corinthians 10 and the author of Hebrews ("we see that they were not able to enter in because of unbelief," Heb 3:19, LSV). Jude uses it first in his series, perhaps for that reason.

### "messengers also, those who did not keep their own principality"

The LSV's "messengers" translates ἀγγέλους (*angelous*), the ordinary word for "messengers" that, in a heavenly context, means "angels." The LSV renders it consistently as "messengers," but the context makes clear that heavenly beings are meant.

These angels "did not keep" (μὴ τηρήσαντας, *mē tērēsantas*) "their own principality" (τὴν ἑαυτῶν ἀρχήν, *tēn heautōn archēn*). The word ἀρχή (*archē*) means "beginning," "rule," or "domain." Here it most likely means the position of authority and the sphere of responsibility God had assigned them. The New Testament can call angelic powers "principalities" (ἀρχαί, *archai*; e.g., Rom 8:38; Eph 6:12). The LSV's "principality" preserves that link. The WEB has "their first domain," the ASV "their own principality," and the BSB "their positions of authority."

Note the first play on "keep." The readers have been "kept" (v. 1). These angels did not "keep" what God had given them.

### "but left their proper dwelling"

The angels also "left" or "abandoned" (ἀπολιπόντας, *apolipontas*) "their proper dwelling" (τὸ ἴδιον οἰκητήριον, *to idion oikētērion*). An οἰκητήριον is a habitation, a dwelling place.[^c3-1] Paul uses the same rare word for the resurrection body: "our dwelling that is from Heaven" (2 Cor 5:2, LSV). The angels' proper home was heaven. They left it.

Which angels, and what did they do? Jude assumes his readers know the story. As the deep dive below shows, they almost certainly did: it is the story of the "sons of God" who took human wives in Genesis 6:1–4, as expanded in the Jewish tradition preserved in 1 Enoch.

### "He has kept in eternal bonds under darkness until [the] judgment of [the] great day"

Now comes the second, ironic "keep." The angels who did not *keep* their position, God "has kept" (τετήρηκεν, *tetērēken*, perfect tense: has kept and still keeps) under guard. Jude's wordplay states a principle: those who refuse to keep their God-given place are themselves kept, for judgment.

- **"Eternal bonds"** (δεσμοῖς ἀϊδίοις, *desmois aidiois*). The adjective ἀΐδιος (*aidios*), "everlasting," appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Rom 1:20, of God's "eternal power." The "bonds" are chains of imprisonment.
- **"Under darkness"** (ὑπὸ ζόφον, *hypo zophon*). The word ζόφος (*zophos*) means deep gloom. In Homer it was used of the gloom of the underworld.[^c3-2] It appears again in v. 13 ("the gloom of the darkness") and in the parallel passages of 2 Peter (2:4, 17).
- **"Until [the] judgment of [the] great day"** (εἰς κρίσιν μεγάλης ἡμέρας, *eis krisin megalēs hēmeras*). The imprisonment is not the final penalty. It is custody awaiting trial. The "great day" is the final judgment, the "day of the LORD" of the prophets.

These phrases match the language of 1 Enoch closely, as the deep dive shows.

### "as Sodom and Gomorrah, and the cities around them"

The third example, Σόδομα καὶ Γόμορρα (*Sodoma kai Gomorra*), is the most famous. Genesis 19 tells how God "rained on Sodom and on Gomorrah brimstone and fire from YHWH, from the heavens; and He overthrows these cities, and all the circuit" (Gen 19:24–25, LSV). The "cities around them" (αἱ περὶ αὐτὰς πόλεις, *hai peri autas poleis*) are Admah and Zeboim, named in Deut 29:23 and Hos 11:8 as destroyed with Sodom and Gomorrah. (Zoar, the fifth city of the plain, was spared at Lot's request.)

### "in like manner to these [messengers]"

The Greek τὸν ὅμοιον τρόπον τούτοις (*ton homoion tropon toutois*) means "in the same way as these." Grammatically, "these" (τούτοις, *toutois*) is masculine, so it cannot refer to the cities (πόλεις, *poleis*), which is feminine. It most naturally refers to the nearest masculine plural, the angels of v. 6. The LSV makes this explicit by supplying "[messengers]" in brackets. (Some interpreters have suggested that "these" looks ahead to the intruders of v. 8, but the immediate sense is a comparison between Sodom and the angels.)

So the comparison is this: *the cities sinned in the same way as the angels*. In what way? The next phrases answer.

### "having given themselves to whoredom"

The participle ἐκπορνεύσασαι (*ekporneusasai*) is a strengthened form of the common verb for sexual immorality, πορνεύω (*porneuō*). Its prefix ἐκ- (*ek-*) suggests going to excess, "giving oneself up to fornication."[^c3-3] The word occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. In the Septuagint it often describes Israel's spiritual unfaithfulness, pictured as prostitution, but here, as the following phrase shows, the sense is literal.

### "and having gone after other flesh"

The phrase ἀπελθοῦσαι ὀπίσω σαρκὸς ἑτέρας (*apelthousai opisō sarkos heteras*) means literally "having gone away after other (or different) flesh." The KJV's famous "strange flesh" and the WEB's "strange flesh" translate it. The BSB has "perversion." The LSV keeps the literal "other flesh."

What is "other flesh"? This phrase has become a flashpoint in modern debates about sexuality, and it needs careful handling. The deep dive below sets out the evidence. In brief, the parallel Jude has just drawn with the angels ("in like manner to these") strongly suggests the following: as the angels crossed the boundary between heaven and earth to unite with human women, so the men of Sodom sought sexual relations with beings of a different order, namely the angels who visited Lot (Gen 19:1–5). On this reading, "other flesh" refers above all to the attempted violation of the angelic visitors. Jude's emphasis falls on the transgression of the created order.

### "have been set before [as] an example"

The verb πρόκεινται (*prokeintai*), "they are set forth, displayed," is in the present tense: the cities *are* still set forth. The noun δεῖγμα (*deigma*), another New Testament hapax, means "a thing shown," a specimen or example, and here a warning example.[^c3-4] Second Peter uses the related ὑπόδειγμα (*hypodeigma*): "having set [them as] an example to those about to be impious" (2 Pet 2:6, LSV).

Ancient readers believed they could still see the evidence. The region around the southern end of the Dead Sea, desolate, salty, and sulfurous, was identified with the ruined cities. The Wisdom of Solomon says of them: "even to this day the waste land that smoketh is a testimony, and plants bearing fruit that never come to ripeness: and a standing pillar of salt is a monument of an unbelieving soul" (Wis 10:7, Brenton).[^c3-5] Third Maccabees says that God made the Sodomites "an example to after generations" (3 Macc 2:5, Brenton).

### "undergoing the justice of continuous fire"

The present participle ὑπέχουσαι (*hypechousai*), "undergoing," also unique in the New Testament, means "to undergo, suffer" a penalty.[^c3-6] The noun δίκη (*dikē*), "justice," here means the execution of a sentence, punishment. The phrase πυρὸς αἰωνίου (*pyros aiōniou*) is "eternal fire," which the LSV renders "continuous fire," in keeping with its consistent treatment of αἰώνιος (*aiōnios*).

Two senses may be combined. The visible, smoking desolation of the region was understood as an ongoing display of the fire that destroyed the cities. That fire also pointed beyond itself to the "continuous fire that has been prepared for the Devil and his messengers" (Matt 25:41, LSV). The cities are an example *because* their temporal destruction prefigures final judgment. Jesus made the same connection: "it will be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city" (Matt 10:15, LSV).

**On "eternal" and "continuous."** The LSV's "continuous" for αἰώνιος reflects a decision to translate the word by its root, αἰών (*aiōn*, "age"), rather than by the traditional "eternal." Whether αἰώνιος in the New Testament means "unending" or "belonging to the age to come" has long been discussed, and the word can carry both ideas: the life and judgment of the coming age, which does not end. Readers should not build a doctrine of the duration of judgment on the LSV's English choice alone, any more than on "eternal." The theology must be built from the whole biblical witness.

### Deep dive: "Jesus … saved a people out of Egypt" — the text of Jude 5

The Greek manuscripts of Jude 5 are a tangle. The two main points of variation are (a) the name of the one who saved the people and (b) the position of "once."

**The readings.** According to the SBLGNT apparatus and its updated form citing NA28:[^c3-8]

| Edition | Reading of the key words |
|---|---|
| SBLGNT (2010), NA28 (2012) | εἰδότας ὑμᾶς ἅπαξ πάντα ὅτι **Ἰησοῦς** λαὸν… ("knowing all things once, that **Jesus**…") |
| NA27 (1993) | …πάντα ὅτι **ὁ κύριος ἅπαξ** λαὸν… ("that **the Lord**, having *once* saved…") |
| Westcott–Hort (1881), Tregelles | …ἅπαξ πάντα ὅτι **κύριος**… ("that **[the] Lord**…") |
| Robinson–Pierpont (Byzantine), TR | εἰδότας ὑμᾶς ἅπαξ **τοῦτο** ὅτι **ὁ κύριος**… ("once knowing **this**, that **the Lord**…") |

The great majority of later Byzantine manuscripts read "the Lord." According to the tallies in one modern edition, the Byzantine wording appears in about 69 percent of manuscripts and the wording with "Jesus" in a fraction of one percent.[^c3-9] The earliest manuscripts, however, divide differently. "Jesus" is the reading of two of the most important early codices, Alexandrinus (A, fifth century) and Vaticanus (B, fourth century), together with other early witnesses. The earliest surviving papyrus of Jude, P72 (third or fourth century), has the remarkable reading θεὸς Χριστός, "God Christ." Other manuscripts read "God."[^c3-10]

**How did the committee decide?** When the United Bible Societies committee prepared the text that became NA27, the majority chose "the Lord." Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* reports that two members of the committee, Metzger himself and Allen Wikgren, dissented, arguing that critical principles favored "Jesus" as the best-attested reading.[^c3-11] By the time of NA28 (2012), which followed the *Editio Critica Maior* for the Catholic Letters, the editors had adopted "Jesus," as the SBLGNT (2010) had already done independently.

**The arguments.**

*For "Jesus":*

1. **Strong early attestation** in important manuscripts and early translations.
2. **It is the harder reading.** Scribes were more likely to change a surprising "Jesus" into an unproblematic "the Lord" than the reverse. A scribe copying "Jesus saved a people out of Egypt" might suppose that the historical Jesus was meant, find the statement impossible, and "correct" it.
3. **It explains the other readings.** "Lord," "God," and "God Christ" can all be understood as attempts to resolve the difficulty of "Jesus."
4. **A possible scribal mechanism.** In manuscripts, sacred names were abbreviated: ΙΣ for "Jesus" and ΚΣ for "Lord." These differ by one letter, so confusion in either direction was possible. This cuts both ways, though, and does not decide the question.

*For "the Lord":*

1. **"Lord" is Jude's usual word for God and Christ** (vv. 4, 9, 14, 17, 21, 25). A reading that uses "Jesus" for the pre-existent Christ would be unusual.
2. Some scholars judge that "Jesus" acting in the exodus is so unexpected that it is more likely a scribal slip (ΚΣ to ΙΣ) than an original reading.

Scholarly discussion continues. In 2007 Timo Flink argued that the reading "Jesus" has no parallel that a scribe could have imitated and is therefore hard to explain as an invention.[^c3-12] Others have defended "the Lord." What can be said with confidence is that "Jesus" is now the reading of the most widely used critical editions, of the SBLGNT on which this book is based, and of the LSV, and that it is very well attested.

**What does it mean?** If Jude wrote "Jesus," he identifies the one who saved Israel from Egypt with Jesus Christ. He does not imply that the man from Nazareth lived in the second millennium BC. Rather, the one who became incarnate as Jesus already existed and acted as Israel's deliverer. Christian theology calls this the **pre-existence** of Christ. It is affirmed elsewhere in the New Testament (John 1:1–3; 8:58; Phil 2:6–7; Col 1:15–17; Heb 1:2), and 1 Cor 10:4, 9 apply it specifically to the wilderness. Some interpreters connect it further with the mysterious "Messenger" of Exodus who leads Israel and of whom God says, "My Name [is] in His heart" (Exod 23:21, LSV). Jude does not develop the idea. He simply states it, as if it were already familiar to his readers.

If Jude wrote "the Lord," he almost certainly still meant to evoke the same God whose lordship is now exercised by Jesus. Note that Jude calls Jesus "our only Master and Lord" in the previous verse (v. 4). On either reading, the warning is the same: the Lord who saves can also judge those who, having been saved, refuse to believe.

### Deep dive: angels and the judgment traditions

Who were the angels "who did not keep their own principality, but left their proper dwelling"? The Old Testament offers only one story that fits: Genesis 6:1–4.

> **1** And it comes to pass that mankind has begun to multiply on the face of the ground, and daughters have been born to them, **2** and the sons of God see that they, the daughters of men, [are] beautiful, and they take women for themselves of all whom they have chosen. … **4** The giants were in the earth in those days, and even afterward, when sons of God come in to the daughters of men, and they have borne to them those who [were] the mighty from of old, the men of renown.
>
> — Gen 6:1–4, LSV

Genesis does not say that the "sons of God" were punished, or even that they were angels. By the time of Jude, however, an elaborate Jewish tradition had grown up around these verses. Its fullest surviving form is the "Book of the Watchers," 1 Enoch 1–36, usually dated to the third century BC or earlier. In it, two hundred angels called "Watchers" descend to earth, take human wives, father the giants, and teach humanity forbidden arts. God then sends his archangels to bind them:

> And again the Lord said to Raphael: 'Bind Azâzêl hand and foot, and cast him into the darkness: and make an opening in the desert … and cast him therein. And place upon him rough and jagged rocks, and cover him with darkness, and let him abide there for ever, and cover his face that he may not see light. And on the day of the great judgement he shall be cast into the fire.'
>
> — 1 Enoch 10:4–6 (trans. R. H. Charles)[^c3-13]

And to Michael:

> 'Go, bind Semjâzâ and his associates who have united themselves with women so as to have defiled themselves with them in all their uncleanness. And when their sons have slain one another … bind them fast for seventy generations in the valleys of the earth, till the day of their judgement and of their consummation …'
>
> — 1 Enoch 10:11–12 (trans. R. H. Charles)

The correspondences with Jude 6 are close:

| Jude 6 | 1 Enoch |
|---|---|
| "did not keep their own principality, but left their proper dwelling" | The Watchers abandon heaven for earth (1 Enoch 6–7; 12:4; 15:3, 7) |
| "in eternal bonds" | "Bind … hand and foot"; "bind them fast" (10:4, 12) |
| "under darkness" | "cast him into the darkness … cover him with darkness" (10:4–5) |
| "until [the] judgment of [the] great day" | "on the day of the great judgement" (10:6); "till the day of their judgement" (10:12) |

Jude quotes 1 Enoch explicitly in vv. 14–15, so there is little doubt that he knew this tradition and expected his readers to know it. Second Peter's parallel adds the term "Tartarus," the Greek name for the deepest prison of the underworld: "God did not spare messengers having sinned, but having cast [them] down to Tartarus with chains of deepest gloom, delivered [them], having been reserved to judgment" (2 Pet 2:4, LSV). First Peter's mysterious reference to "the spirits in prison, who sometime [ago] disobeyed … in [the] days of Noah" (1 Pet 3:19–20, LSV) probably belongs to the same circle of ideas.

The tradition also appears in other Jewish writings of the period. The *Testament of Naphtali*, discussed below, pairs the Watchers with Sodom exactly as Jude does. Sirach says that God "was not pacified toward the old giants" (Sir 16:7, Brenton). Third Maccabees recalls that God destroyed "the giants, who trusted in their strength and hardihood" (3 Macc 2:4, Brenton). The fall of the Watchers was a standard example of judgment in Second Temple Judaism, and Jude uses it the way his contemporaries did.

**Other interpretations.** Later Christian interpreters moved away from the angelic reading of Genesis 6. Augustine argued that the "sons of God" were "according to the flesh the sons of Seth" who intermarried with the ungodly line of Cain, and he insisted that angels, being spiritual, could not father children.[^c3-14] Many readers have followed him, and others have read Jude 6 as a reference to an original fall of Satan and his angels before creation (cf. Rev 12:7–9), unconnected with Genesis 6. Both views are defensible as readings of Genesis in its own right. They fit Jude less well, because Jude's vocabulary tracks 1 Enoch's account of the Watchers, his next verse compares the angels' sin to Sodom's sexual sin, and he quotes 1 Enoch a few lines later. Clement of Alexandria, an early commentator, read Jude 6 of angels who "left their own habitation, meaning, that is, the heaven and the stars," and took the "chains" as "the loss of the honour in which they had stood."[^c3-15]

**What Jude affirms, and what he does not.** Jude affirms that some heavenly beings rebelled, that they are under restraint, and that they await final judgment. He does not provide a full angelology, a description of their prison, or a timetable. He uses a story his readers knew to make a single point: *even angels who abandoned their God-given place did not escape judgment.* How much of the surrounding Enochic narrative Jude believed to be historical is a separate question, taken up in Chapter 8.

### Deep dive: Sodom and Gomorrah

Sodom and Gomorrah became the Bible's standard example of total destruction. Moses warns Israel that disobedience would leave the land "like the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah, Admah and Zeboim" (Deut 29:23, LSV). Isaiah calls Jerusalem's rulers "you rulers of Sodom" (Isa 1:10, LSV). Jesus says the coming of the Son of Man will be like the days of Lot, when "He rained fire and brimstone from the sky, and destroyed all" (Luke 17:29, LSV). Paul and Peter both cite the cities. But what, according to the Bible and early Jewish tradition, was Sodom's sin?

**The biblical evidence.**

- **Genesis 18–19.** The outcry against Sodom was already great before the angels arrived: "The cry of Sodom and Gomorrah—because great; and their sin—because exceedingly grievous" (Gen 18:20, LSV). The episode in Gen 19:4–5 shows the city's character in action. The men of the city, "from young even to aged," surround Lot's house and demand that the visitors be brought out "and we know them" (LSV), that is, for sexual assault. The scene combines violent sexual aggression, the violation of guests whom hospitality required Lot to protect, and the fact that the guests were angels.
- **Ezekiel 16:49–50.** Ezekiel names the sins of "your sister Sodom" as "Arrogancy, fullness of bread, and quiet ease," the failure to strengthen "the hand of the afflicted and needy," and adds, "they are haughty and do abomination before Me" (LSV). Ezekiel's emphasis is on pride, complacent wealth, and neglect of the poor, with "abomination" as a further charge.
- **Isaiah 1:10** and **Jeremiah 23:14** use Sodom as a byword for injustice and moral corruption in Jerusalem.

**The Second Temple evidence.** Jewish writers of Jude's era gave the story different emphases:

- **Pride and insolence:** God "abhorred them for their pride" (Sir 16:8, Brenton). God destroyed "the Sodomites, those workers of exceeding iniquity" for their pride and insolence (3 Macc 2:5).
- **Hostility to strangers and sexual practices:** Josephus writes that "the Sodomites grew proud, on account of their riches and great wealth; they became unjust towards men, and impious towards God … they hated strangers, and abused themselves with Sodomitical practices."[^c3-16]
- **Gluttony, debauchery, and same-sex relations:** Philo of Alexandria describes Sodom as "full of innumerable iniquities, and especially of gluttony and debauchery," and says that its men "not only … went mad after women, and defile[d] the marriage bed of others, but also those who were men lusted after one another."[^c3-17]
- **Changing the order of nature, paired with the Watchers:** The *Testament of Naphtali* urges its readers to recognize the Lord in creation, "that you become not as Sodom, which changed the order of its nature, in like manner also the Watchers changed the order of their nature, whom also the Lord cursed at the flood."[^c3-18]

The last text is especially important for Jude, because it makes exactly Jude's pairing. **Sodom and the Watchers are linked as two instances of transgressing the order of nature God established.** The Watchers crossed from heaven to earth to unite with human women. Sodom's men sought to violate heavenly visitors. Jude's "in like manner to these … having gone after other flesh" fits this pattern precisely.

**What "other flesh" means in Jude.** Three readings are found among interpreters:

1. **Angelic flesh.** "Other" (ἑτέρα, *hetera*) means "of a different kind." The men of Sodom pursued sexual union with beings who were not human, the counterpart of the angels' pursuit of human women. This is the reading most modern critical commentators favor, because it makes the best sense of "in like manner to these" and of the Testament of Naphtali parallel.
2. **Same-sex relations.** "Other" means "contrary to nature," and Jude refers to homosexual acts, which Philo and Josephus associated with Sodom. This reading is traditional in much Christian interpretation.
3. **Sexual immorality in general**, with the angelic and same-sex dimensions both included in the description of the city's excess.

The first reading best explains Jude's comparison, and this book adopts it as the primary sense. Two cautions follow from it. First, Jude 7 should not be made to carry more than it says. The phrase's main reference is to the attempted violation of angels, and Jude does not address modern questions about committed same-sex relationships. Those questions are usually argued from other texts (for example Lev 18:22 and Rom 1:26–27), and readers should weigh those passages on their own terms. Second, the opposite overcorrection is equally mistaken: Jude clearly condemns *sexual immorality* ("having given themselves to whoredom"). Sodom in Jude is not merely a symbol of inhospitality, even though inhospitality and violence were part of its sin. Honest reading lets Jude say what he says and no more.

**Why Sodom fits Jude's argument.** Jude's three examples move from God's people (Israel), to heavenly beings (angels), to pagan cities (Sodom). The circle widens to show that judgment reaches every order of creation. All three share one feature: *they left the place God gave them*. Israel left trust for unbelief, the angels left heaven for earth, and Sodom left the order of creation for the pursuit of "other flesh." The intruders, Jude will say, do "in like manner" (v. 8).

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**The exodus in Jewish memory.** The exodus from Egypt was the central saving event of Israel's story, recited at Passover, celebrated in the Psalms, and used by the prophets as the pattern for future deliverance. The wilderness generation's failure was equally remembered, as a warning. Psalm 95, sung in synagogue and church, ends with God's oath: "Forty years I am weary of the generation, … Where I swore in My anger" that they would not enter his rest (Ps 95:10–11, LSV). Jude's readers would have known both sides of the story.

**Angels in Second Temple Judaism.** Jewish thought between the Testaments developed a rich angelology: named archangels (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Uriel), angelic hierarchies, guardian angels of nations, and fallen angels. The Dead Sea Scrolls, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and other writings show how widely these ideas were held. Jude's casual reference to rebellious angels and, in v. 9, to Michael the archangel shows that he and his readers shared this world of thought.

**The Dead Sea region.** The southern end of the Dead Sea, with its salt formations, bitumen, and barren landscape, was identified in antiquity with the destroyed cities of the plain. Wisdom 10:7, quoted above, speaks of its "waste land that smoketh" and its pillar of salt. Josephus also describes the region.[^c3-19] For Jude's readers, the "example" was not only a story but a place.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**1 Corinthians 10:1–11 (close thematic parallel).** Paul's use of the wilderness generation is the closest parallel to Jude 5. All Israel "passed through the sea … and all ate the same spiritual food," yet "in the most of them God was not well pleased, for they were strewn in the wilderness" (1 Cor 10:1, 3, 5, LSV). Paul identifies the rock as "the Christ" (10:4) and warns against tempting "the Christ" (10:9). His conclusion, "these things happened to those persons as types, and they were written for our admonition" (10:11, LSV), is exactly Jude's use of the story. The texts share a tradition of interpretation, but neither is likely quoting the other.

**Hebrews 3:16–19 (close thematic parallel).** "Was it not with those who sinned, whose carcasses fell in the wilderness? … they were not able to enter in because of unbelief" (Heb 3:17, 19, LSV). Hebrews stresses unbelief just as Jude does ("those not having believed").

**2 Peter 2:4–9 (literary parallel).** Peter's version lists angels, the flood, and Sodom, in biblical order. It adds Noah and Lot as examples of the righteous rescued and concludes: "the LORD has known to rescue [the] pious out of temptation, and to keep [the] unrighteous being punished to [the] day of judgment" (2 Pet 2:9, LSV). The relationship to Jude is discussed in the Introduction and Appendix C.

**Matthew 25:41; Revelation 20:10 (shared theme).** The "continuous fire that has been prepared for the Devil and his messengers" (Matt 25:41, LSV) connects the fate of rebellious angels and the fire of judgment, the same two ideas that Jude 6–7 places side by side.

**Exodus 23:20–21 (possible background).** The Messenger in whom God's Name dwells and who leads Israel through the wilderness has been connected by some interpreters with Jude's "Jesus" in v. 5. This is a suggestive connection, not a demonstrated allusion.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. "Jesus" or "the Lord" in v. 5?** Discussed in the deep dive. The reading "Jesus" is strongly attested and is now printed in the main critical editions, but a minority of scholars still prefers "the Lord."

**2. Can the redeemed be destroyed?** Jude's first example concerns people who were genuinely saved from Egypt and later destroyed for unbelief. Christian traditions read this differently. Some see in it a warning that those who were once part of the redeemed community can fall away. Others see it as a type: physical deliverance from Egypt was real, but not every member of the delivered people had saving faith, and so the warning targets those whose membership in the church is not matched by faith. Jude's purpose is practical rather than systematic. He warns the readers not to trust in privilege, and he assures them that God is able to keep them (v. 24).

**3. Does Jude endorse the Watcher story as history?** Jude certainly uses the tradition, and his argument assumes its point. Whether he regarded every detail of 1 Enoch's narrative as historical cannot be determined from his brief allusion. The question is discussed further in Chapter 8.

**4. What was Sodom's sin?** Discussed in the deep dive. Jude's own emphasis is on sexual immorality and the pursuit of "other flesh," understood in light of the angel parallel. Other biblical texts emphasize pride, wealth, injustice, and inhospitality. A full biblical portrait includes all of these.

**5. "Eternal" or "continuous" fire?** The question of what αἰώνιος means, and of the nature and duration of final judgment, is a larger theological discussion that Jude's verse alone cannot settle. Jude's own point is that Sodom's destruction is a visible, lasting example of judgment that should give the reader pause.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verses 5–7 are the first of Jude's "texts," the evidence that the intruders' condemnation was "written beforehand" (v. 4). The next verse begins "In like manner" (ὁμοίως, *homoiōs*) and applies the examples: "those dreaming indeed defile the flesh, and they disregard lordship, and they speak evil of glorious [ones]" (v. 8). The three sins of v. 8 correspond loosely to the three examples. Defiling the flesh corresponds to Sodom. Disregarding lordship corresponds to the angels who left their place and to Israel's rebellion. Slandering glorious ones prepares for the contrast with Michael in v. 9.

The passage also develops the letter's key word: "keep." The angels "did not keep" and are "kept" (v. 6). The readers, "kept" by God (v. 1), will be urged to "keep" themselves (v. 21). The examples of vv. 5–7 are not only threats against the intruders. They are also mirrors for the readers, showing what it would mean to leave the place God has given.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*These reflections are suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Privilege is not protection.** Jude's first example is directed at people who belong to the community of the saved. Church membership, a dramatic conversion story, or a heritage of faith do not by themselves guarantee faithfulness. Jude's warning may be most needed in communities that feel most secure.

**The goodness of boundaries.** All three examples involve leaving a God-given place. Modern culture often treats limits as obstacles to self-realization. Jude's examples suggest that boundaries such as the place of trust, of creaturely station, and of the created order can be gifts that protect and do not merely restrict. This idea can be misused to justify unjust social hierarchies, which Jude does not address. Applied to the believer's relationship with God, though, it invites reflection on the freedom found in remaining where God has placed us: in his love (v. 21).

**Reading hard texts honestly.** Jude 7 is often quoted in modern debates. Responsible readers will resist both the temptation to make it say more than it does and the temptation to make it say less. That discipline is itself a form of "contending for the faith."

**Christ in the whole story.** If Jude 5 identifies Jesus as Israel's deliverer, Christians may read the Old Testament as the story of the same Lord they know in the Gospels. That conviction has shaped Christian worship and interpretation from the beginning.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Why do you think Jude began his examples with God's own people rather than with pagan Sodom? What effect might that order have had on his readers?
2. What difference does it make to your understanding of Jesus if Jude 5 says that *Jesus* saved Israel from Egypt?
3. The angels "did not keep their own principality" and are now "kept" for judgment. How does this wordplay illuminate the relationship between faithfulness and security?
4. Jude, Ezekiel, Josephus, and Philo describe Sodom's sin differently. Why might different writers emphasize different aspects of the same story? What are the risks of focusing on only one?
5. Jude says his readers already know these things but need reminding. What truths do you know well but most need to be reminded of?
