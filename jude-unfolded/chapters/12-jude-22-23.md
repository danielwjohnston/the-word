## B. Plain-English Orientation

Jude's last instructions turn from the readers' own spiritual life (vv. 20–21) to their responsibility toward others. Those who have been kept in God's love and who wait for Christ's mercy must now show mercy themselves. Jude names three kinds of people (on the most widely accepted reading of the text):

1. **Those who are wavering**, unsettled by the intruders' influence. Show them mercy.
2. **Those in immediate danger**, already near the fire of judgment. Snatch them out.
3. **Others who are deeply compromised.** Show them mercy too, but with fear, hating even the clothing stained by their sin.

These two verses transform the letter. After so much warning and condemnation, Jude's last practical word is mercy. The readers are not to write off everyone touched by the intruders. They are to distinguish between people in different situations and to act toward each with compassion, urgency, and wisdom. Contending for the faith (v. 3) ends in rescue.

The Greek text of these verses is one of the most complicated in the New Testament. The manuscripts preserve two-clause and three-clause versions with different verbs. This chapter follows the three-clause text printed in the LSV, the SBLGNT, and NA28, and explains the alternatives in a deep dive.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### καὶ οὓς μὲν ἐλεᾶτε (*kai hous men eleate*) — ‘and have mercy on some’

The command ἐλεᾶτε (*eleate*), "show mercy, have mercy," is a present imperative of ἐλεάω (*eleaō*), a variant form of the more common ἐλεέω (*eleeō*). The Byzantine manuscripts read ἐλεεῖτε, the more familiar form.[^c12-1] The word picks up the theme of mercy that frames the letter. Jude wished his readers mercy (v. 2), and they wait for Christ's mercy (v. 21). Now they are to show mercy. Mercy received becomes mercy given.

The construction καὶ οὓς μὲν … οὓς δὲ … οὓς δὲ (*kai hous men … hous de … hous de*), "and some … others … others," divides the people in view into groups. The LSV's "and, indeed, … others … others" reflects it.

### διακρινομένους (*diakrinomenous*) — ‘who are doubting’

The participle διακρινομένους (*diakrinomenous*) comes from διακρίνω (*diakrinō*), a verb with a wide range: "to separate, distinguish, judge," and in the middle voice "to dispute" or "to doubt, waver." Jude used the same verb in v. 9 for Michael "disputing" with the Devil. Here two meanings are possible:

1. **"Those who doubt" or "waver."** This is the sense the verb usually has in the New Testament when it describes a person's inner state. Jesus says, "if you may have faith, and may not doubt" (Matt 21:21, LSV). James writes, "let him ask in faith, doubting nothing, for he who is doubting has been like a wave of the sea, driven by wind and tossed" (Jas 1:6, LSV). On this reading, Jude refers to believers who have been unsettled by the intruders and are uncertain whom to believe. They need mercy, patient and compassionate help, not condemnation.
2. **"Those who dispute."** This sense matches Jude's own use in v. 9. It would refer to people who argue, perhaps those who have taken the intruders' side. Some manuscripts read "convict" or "refute" (ἐλέγχετε, *elenchete*) instead of "show mercy" in this clause, which would give "refute those who dispute."

Most translations and commentators prefer "doubt" or "waver." The image in James of the doubter as "a wave of the sea" offers an interesting echo of Jude's "wild waves" (v. 13). The intruders are wild waves, and those they unsettle are tossed by them. The wavering are not the enemy. They are the ones at risk.

The Byzantine text reads the nominative διακρινόμενοι (*diakrinomenoi*), which makes the participle describe the readers rather than the people they help. Webster's revision of the traditional English Bible translates it "And of some have compassion, making a difference" (WBS), meaning that the readers should show compassion while discerning between cases. That reading also has a good sense, but the accusative form, referring to those who doubt, has stronger early support and is printed in the critical editions.[^c12-2]

### οὓς δὲ σῴζετε ἐκ πυρὸς ἁρπάζοντες (*hous de sōzete ek pyros harpazontes*) — ‘save others, snatching them out of the fire’

The second group is in more urgent danger. The command σῴζετε (*sōzete*), "save, rescue," is accompanied by the participle ἁρπάζοντες (*harpazontes*), "snatching, seizing." The verb ἁρπάζω (*harpazō*) means "to seize by force, to grab." Jesus uses it when he says that "no one will snatch them out of My hand" (John 10:28, LSV). Here it describes a rescuer pulling someone out of a burning building, an act of urgent, physical intervention.

The "fire" (πῦρ, *pyr*) recalls the "continuous fire" of Sodom (v. 7), the fire of judgment. These people are already, so to speak, in the flames, close to the fate of the intruders. They cannot be helped by gentle persuasion alone. They need decisive action.

The image comes from the prophets. In Zechariah's vision, the LORD says of Joshua the high priest: "Is this not a brand delivered from fire?" (Zech 3:2, LSV), a burning stick pulled out of the fire before it is consumed. The Septuagint's wording, δαλὸς ἐξεσπασμένος ἐκ πυρός (*dalos exespasmenos ek pyros*), "a brand snatched out of fire," is close to Jude's ἐκ πυρὸς ἁρπάζοντες (*ek pyros harpazontes*).[^c12-3] Amos uses the same image in a passage that also recalls Sodom and Gomorrah: "I have overturned among you, / Like the overturn by God of Sodom and Gomorrah, / And you are as a brand delivered from a burning" (Amos 4:11, LSV). Jude's readers, who have just heard about Sodom's fire (v. 7), are called to be the hands that pull people from it.

Notice where this allusion comes from. Zechariah 3 also stood behind Michael's words in v. 9: "The LORD rebuke you!" In that chapter, the accuser stands against Joshua, the LORD rebukes the accuser, Joshua is called "a brand delivered from fire," and his filthy garments are removed (Zech 3:1–4). Jude draws on the same scene twice. In v. 9 it shows the restraint of the heavenly defender. In v. 23 it shows the rescue of the one accused.

### οὓς δὲ ἐλεᾶτε ἐν φόβῳ (*hous de eleate en phobō*) — ‘and on others have mercy with fear’

The third group also receives mercy, ἐλεᾶτε again, but now ἐν φόβῳ (*en phobō*), "in fear" or "with fear." What kind of fear?

1. **Fear of God.** Mercy must be shown with reverent awareness of God's holiness and judgment. The readers must not treat sin lightly while showing mercy to sinners. Paul writes, "having known, therefore, the fear of the LORD, we persuade men" (2 Cor 5:11, LSV), and Hebrews calls believers to serve God "with reverence and fear, for our God [is] also a consuming fire" (Heb 12:28–29, LSV).
2. **Caution about contamination.** Those who help people deeply involved in sin must take care not to be drawn into it themselves. Paul gives the same caution: "you who [are] spiritual restore such a one in a spirit of meekness, considering yourself—lest you also may be tempted" (Gal 6:1, LSV).

The two senses go together, and the next phrase explains the second.

### μισοῦντες καὶ τὸν ἀπὸ τῆς σαρκὸς ἐσπιλωμένον χιτῶνα (*misountes kai ton apo tēs sarkos espilōmenon chitōna*) — ‘hating even the tunic stained by the flesh’

The final phrase is vivid: μισοῦντες καὶ τὸν ἀπὸ τῆς σαρκὸς ἐσπιλωμένον χιτῶνα (*misountes kai ton apo tēs sarkos espilōmenon chitōna*), "hating even the tunic stained by the flesh."

- The **χιτών** (*chitōn*) was the basic garment of the ancient world, a tunic worn next to the skin under the outer cloak.
- **ἐσπιλωμένον** (*espilōmenon*), "stained, spotted," is the perfect passive participle of σπιλόω (*spiloō*), "to stain, defile."[^c12-4] It belongs to the same family as σπίλος, "stain," and, on one reading, as the σπιλάδες of v. 12. If Jude intended the link, the intruders who were "stains" at the love feasts (LSV) have stained the garments of those they influenced.
- **"From the flesh"** (ἀπὸ τῆς σαρκός, *apo tēs sarkos*) points to the source of the stain: sinful human nature, and especially the sexual sins Jude has repeatedly mentioned (vv. 4, 7–8).

The image is deliberately strong. The undergarment touched the body directly. To hate "even the coat" is to want nothing to do with the sin, not even with what has been in contact with it, while still reaching out to the person. Again Zechariah 3 is in the background: "Joshua was clothed with filthy garments," and the LORD says, "Turn aside the filthy garments from off him. … See, I have caused your iniquity to pass away from off you, so as to clothe you with costly apparel" (Zech 3:3–4, LSV). The garment is not the person. The garment can be removed, and the person can be clothed afresh.

The phrase holds together two attitudes that are easily separated: mercy toward people and hatred of sin. Christians have often summarized the balance in the slogan "love the sinner, hate the sin." Jude gives a more demanding picture. The mercy is real, but it is shown "in fear," and the hatred of sin is strong enough to recoil from its slightest trace.

### Deep dive: Showing mercy to those who doubt

Jude's instruction to "show mercy to those who are doubting" is one of the most pastorally significant sentences in the letter, and it deserves reflection.

**Who are the doubters?** In Jude's situation, they were probably believers who had been exposed to the intruders' teaching and conduct and had become unsure. Perhaps the intruders' visions impressed them, or their freedom seemed attractive. Perhaps they could not see why Jude and the other leaders objected. Such people were not yet committed to the intruders' way of life, but they were wavering.

**Why mercy?** Jude could have told his readers to rebuke or exclude the doubters. Instead he tells them to show mercy. Several reasons emerge from the letter:

- **They have received mercy themselves** (vv. 2, 21). Those who wait for Christ's mercy cannot withhold mercy from others.
- **Doubt is not the same as rebellion.** The intruders deliberately turn grace into license and reject authority. The doubters are uncertain. To treat uncertainty as rebellion would push the wavering toward the very people Jude warns against.
- **The goal is restoration.** Jude's whole letter aims to keep people in God's love. Mercy toward the wavering serves that goal.

**What does mercy look like?** Jude does not spell it out, but the New Testament offers many models. Paul urges, "receive him who is weak in the faith—not to determinations of reasonings" (Rom 14:1, LSV). The Lord's servant "must not quarrel, but to be gentle to all, apt to teach, patient under evil, instructing those opposing in meekness—if perhaps God may give to them conversion" (2 Tim 2:24–25, LSV). James promises that "he who turned back a sinner from the straying of his way will save a soul from death" (Jas 5:20, LSV). Jesus' parable of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to seek the one gone astray ends, "it is not [the] will in [the] presence of your Father who is in the heavens that one of these little ones may perish" (Matt 18:14, LSV). Mercy to the doubting likely includes patience, gentle instruction, prayer, presence, and the steady testimony of a community that is building itself up in the faith (v. 20).

**Discernment.** Jude's threefold distinction shows that mercy is not uniform. The wavering need patient compassion. Those near the fire need urgent rescue. The deeply compromised need mercy mixed with caution. Wise pastoral care pays attention to the person's situation.

**Doubt today.** The word "doubt" covers many experiences: intellectual questions, emotional struggles, confusion caused by conflicting teachers, disappointment with the church. Not all of them are what Jude had in mind. His instruction, though, suggests a general principle. Communities that hold firmly to "the faith once delivered" should be the most merciful toward those who struggle to hold it. Firmness and mercy are not opposites in Jude. Both flow from being kept in God's love.

Early Christian writers knew different forms too. Clement of Alexandria paraphrased a two-part text: "But some, he says, save, plucking them from the fire; but of some have compassion in fear."[^c12-6]

**Why so many forms?** The repetition of similar words (two forms of "show mercy," and the series οὓς μὲν … οὓς δέ) made the passage easy to miscopy. Scribes may have dropped a clause by accident when the eye skipped from one οὓς δέ to the next, or they may have smoothed a difficult text. The verb διακρίνω, which can mean "doubt" or "dispute," also invited different interpretations and therefore different verbs ("show mercy" or "refute").

**Which is original?** Interpreters are divided. The three-clause text printed in the SBLGNT and NA28 is supported by Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus (with "convict"), Ψ, and 1739, and it can explain the two-clause forms as accidental omissions. The two-clause forms are supported by the oldest witness, P72, and by Vaticanus, in different shapes. A good case can therefore be made that a shorter text is original and that the three-clause text grew by expansion. Nineteenth-century commentators already struggled with the passage. The Cambridge commentary observed that "the MSS. present a strange variety of readings" and that those "of most authority give, Some rebuke (or convict) … when they debate with you." Plummer, in Ellicott's commentary, thought "the evidence is very strong in favour of" the reading "some indeed convict." J. B. Mayor gave the problem one of the longest notes in his commentary.[^c12-7]

**Does it matter?** The core of the passage is the same in all forms: mercy toward some, urgent rescue of others from the fire, and caution about the defilement of sin. The differences affect how many groups Jude distinguishes and whether the first group receives mercy or rebuke. This book follows the three-clause text of the LSV and SBLGNT, while recognizing that certainty is not possible.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Clothing and purity.** In the Jewish world, garments could become ritually impure through contact with bodily discharges or with unclean things (Lev 13–15), and purification involved washing clothes. The image of a defiled garment would have carried both ritual and moral overtones. Zechariah's vision of the high priest's filthy garments, which are removed and replaced with clean ones, provided a powerful picture of forgiveness and restoration.

**Fire as judgment.** In Jewish and early Christian thought, fire was the most common image of divine judgment: the fire of Sodom, the fire of Gehenna, the "continuous fire" of the final judgment. To snatch someone from the fire was to rescue them from judgment, by persuasion, intervention, or prayer, before it was too late.

**Church discipline and restoration.** Early Christian communities practiced various forms of discipline toward members who fell into serious sin (Matt 18:15–17; 1 Cor 5; 2 Thess 3:14–15). The goal was always restoration: "do not count as an enemy, but admonish as a brother" (2 Thess 3:15, LSV). Jude's instructions belong to this pastoral tradition, though they do not describe formal procedures.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Zechariah 3:1–5 (allusion).** The brand snatched from the fire and the filthy garments removed. This is the most important background for both images in v. 23, and the same chapter stands behind Michael's words in v. 9.

**Amos 4:11 (shared image).** "A brand delivered from a burning," in a passage that also mentions Sodom and Gomorrah.

**James 1:6; 5:19–20 (shared themes).** The doubter as a wave of the sea, and the saving of a sinner from death. James's closing verses resemble Jude's in spirit: both letters end by urging readers to restore those who wander.

**Galatians 6:1; 2 Timothy 2:24–26; Romans 14:1 (pastoral care).** Gentle restoration with awareness of one's own vulnerability.

**Matthew 18:12–14 (the lost sheep).** God's will that none of the little ones perish.

**Revelation 3:4; 2 Corinthians 7:1 (defiled garments; cleansing).** "a few names even in Sardis who did not defile their garments" (Rev 3:4, LSV); "may we cleanse ourselves from every defilement of flesh and spirit, perfecting sanctification in the fear of God" (2 Cor 7:1, LSV). Paul joins cleansing from defilement of the flesh with "the fear of God," as Jude joins mercy "in fear" with hatred of the garment "stained from the flesh."

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Two groups or three?** See the deep dive.

**2. "Doubt" or "dispute"?** Most interpreters prefer "doubt" or "waver," following New Testament usage and the logic of showing mercy. The alternative ("dispute," with "refute" or "convict") has some manuscript support and matches Jude's use of the verb in v. 9.

**3. Who is in the fire?** Probably people who have gone further than the doubters, adopting the intruders' practices. Some interpreters think the intruders themselves might be included among those who could still be snatched from the fire. Jude's harsh descriptions in vv. 4–19 do not encourage that reading, but his structure leaves the door open in a way that is worth noting.

**4. What does "in fear" mean?** Fear of God, caution about contamination, or both.

**5. How do vv. 22–23 relate to the rest of the letter?** Some readers find the shift from condemnation to mercy abrupt. It is better understood as the goal of the letter. Jude exposes the intruders in order to protect the community and rescue those at risk. The warnings serve the mercy.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verses 22–23 complete the exhortation that began in v. 20. The readers who keep themselves in God's love (v. 21) now extend that love to others. The letter's three great themes come together here:

- **Mercy:** wished for the readers (v. 2), awaited from Christ (v. 21), shown to others (vv. 22–23).
- **Fire and judgment:** the fire of Sodom (v. 7), the coming judgment (vv. 14–15), and the fire from which people must be snatched (v. 23).
- **Defilement:** the flesh defiled by the intruders (v. 8), the stains at the love feasts (v. 12), and the garment stained by the flesh (v. 23).

The passage also prepares for the doxology. The readers who rescue others from stumbling into the fire will themselves be kept "without stumbling" by the God who is able (v. 24).

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Mercy as the end of contending.** Jude's letter shows that contending for the faith does not end in triumph over opponents but in mercy toward the wavering and rescue of the endangered. Christians who are passionate about truth might ask whether their passion leads to the same place.

**Differentiated care.** Jude distinguishes among people in different situations. Pastoral care today might likewise distinguish between honest questioners, people in crisis, and people deeply entangled in harmful patterns, responding to each with appropriate compassion, urgency, and wisdom.

**Mercy without naïveté.** Showing mercy "in fear" and "hating even the coat" suggests that mercy does not mean ignoring danger. Those who help others out of destructive situations, whether addiction, abuse, or spiritual manipulation, often need to set boundaries and protect themselves. That is not a failure of mercy but part of it.

**The garment can be changed.** Zechariah's vision, behind Jude's words, ends with new clothes. Those who have been stained can be cleansed. Christians who show mercy do so in the hope that God will "turn aside the filthy garments" and clothe people afresh.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Why do you think Jude ends his practical instructions with mercy rather than with further warnings? How does this change your reading of the whole letter?
2. Think of people you know who are "wavering" in faith. What would it look like to show them mercy, in practical terms?
3. Jude speaks of "snatching" some from the fire. Are there situations in which urgent intervention is more loving than patient waiting? How can one tell?
4. What does it mean to show mercy "in fear"? How can believers help others without being drawn into the same patterns?
5. The garment imagery comes from Zechariah 3, where filthy garments are removed and replaced. How does this picture of cleansing shape the way you think about people who have fallen into sin?

**1** Then the LORD said to Moses and Aaron, **2** “When someone has a swelling or rash or bright spot on his skin that could become an infectious skin disease, he must be brought to Aaron the priest or to one of his sons who is a priest. **3** The priest is to examine the infection on his skin, and if the hair in the infection has turned white and the sore appears to be deeper than the skin, it is a skin disease. נִרְפָּא נֶגַע־הַצָּרַעַת מִן־הַצָּרוּעַ **4** וְצִוָּה הַכֹּהֵן וְלָקַח לַמִּטַּהֵר שְׁתֵּי־צִפֳּרִים חַיּוֹת טְהֹרוֹת וְעֵץ אֶרֶז וּשְׁנִי תוֹלַעַת וְאֵזֹב **5** וְצִוָּה הַכֹּהֵן וְשָׁחַט אֶת־הַצִּפּוֹר הָאֶחָת אֶל־כְּלִי־חֶרֶשׂ עַל־מַיִם חַיִּים **6** אֶת־הַצִּפֹּר הַחַיָּה יִקַּח אֹתָהּ וְאֶת־עֵץ הָאֶרֶז וְאֶת־שְׁנִי הַתּוֹלַעַת וְאֶת־הָאֵזֹב וְטָבַל אוֹתָם וְאֵת הַצִּפֹּר הַחַיָּה בְּדַם הַצִּפֹּר הַשְּׁחֻטָה עַל הַמַּיִם הַחַיִּים **7** וְהִזָּה עַל הַמִּטַּהֵר מִן־הַצָּרַעַת שֶׁבַע פְּעָמִים וְטִהֲרוֹ וְשִׁלַּח אֶת־הַצִּפֹּר הַחַיָּה עַל־פְּנֵי הַשָּׂדֶה **8** וְכִבֶּס
