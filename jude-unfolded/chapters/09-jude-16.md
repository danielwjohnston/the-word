# Chapter 9. Grumblers, Fault-Finders, and Flatterers (Jude 16)

## A. The Text

> **16** These are discontented grumblers, walking according to their own lusts; and their mouth speaks swollen words, giving admiration to persons for the sake of profit;
>
> — Jude 16, LSV

## B. Plain-English Orientation

After Enoch's prophecy, Jude applies it once more: "These are…" Enoch's prophecy ended with "the harsh [words] that impious sinners spoke against Him." Jude now shows how the intruders' speech fits that description. They **grumble**, complaining against God and their circumstances. They **blame their lot**, always dissatisfied. They **follow their own desires**. They **speak arrogant words**, boastful and grandiose. And they **flatter people** when there is something to be gained.

The portrait is psychologically sharp. The same people who complain about God and boast about themselves are servile toward anyone who can benefit them. Their speech moves between resentment, arrogance, and flattery, and self-interest drives all three.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### "These are discontented grumblers"

The verse opens with the formula Οὗτοί εἰσιν (*Houtoi eisin*), "These are," which marks the third application in the letter (vv. 8, 10, 12, 16, 19). The LSV's "discontented grumblers" combines two Greek words, each found nowhere else in the New Testament.

**γογγυσταί (*gongystai*), "grumblers, murmurers."** The noun comes from the verb γογγύζω (*gongyzō*), "to grumble, mutter," whose sound imitates low, discontented muttering. The noun itself is rare, and in the Greek Old Testament it appears only in later revisions.[^c9-1] The verb and its related noun γογγυσμός (*gongysmos*), however, are the standard Septuagint terms for **Israel's murmuring in the wilderness**. "In YHWH's hearing your murmurings, which you are murmuring against Him … Your murmurings [are] not against us, but against YHWH" (Exod 16:8, LSV). After the spies' report, "all the sons of Israel murmur against Moses and against Aaron" (Num 14:2, LSV), and God says, "The murmurings of the sons of Israel, which they are murmuring against Me, I have heard" (Num 14:27, LSV). The murmuring led to the death of that generation in the wilderness, the very event Jude recalled in v. 5. Korah's rebellion, too, was a "murmuring" against Aaron (Num 16:11; 17:5).

Paul draws the same lesson for the church: "neither murmur, as also some of them murmured, and perished by the destroyer" (1 Cor 10:10, LSV). He urges the Philippians, "Do all things without murmurings and deliberations" (Phil 2:14, LSV). For Jude's readers, "grumblers" would have called up the whole wilderness story: people delivered by God who complained against him and perished.

**μεμψίμοιροι (*mempsimoiroi*), "fault-finders, complaining of their lot."** This word combines μέμφομαι (*memphomai*, "to blame, find fault") and μοῖρα (*moira*, "lot, portion, fate"). Its sense is "blaming one's lot," "querulous," and it describes a person perpetually dissatisfied with what life, or God, has given.[^c9-2] It was a recognized character type in Greek moral writing. The philosopher Theophrastus (fourth century BC) included "the Grumbler" (Μεμψιμοιρία, *Mempsimoiria*) among his thirty sketches of human types. His grumbler receives a gift of food from a friend's table and complains, "He grudged me a dish of soup and a cup of wine, I suppose, and so couldn't invite me to dinner."[^c9-3] Jude's readers would have recognized the type, a person whose ingratitude turns every blessing into a grievance.

Put together, the two words describe people whose fundamental posture toward God is complaint. They are the opposite of the thankful, contented trust that the faith produces (Phil 4:11–12; 1 Tim 6:6–8).

### "walking according to their own lusts"

The phrase κατὰ τὰς ἐπιθυμίας αὐτῶν πορευόμενοι (*kata tas epithymias autōn poreuomenoi*), "going according to their desires," describes a whole way of life governed by craving. The verb πορεύομαι (*poreuomai*), "to go, walk," repeats the "went" of v. 11 ("they went on in the way of Cain"), and it will return in v. 18 ("following after their own lusts of impieties"). The noun ἐπιθυμία (*epithymia*), "desire, craving," is neutral in itself but in this context means disordered desire. The intruders' grumbling and their self-indulgence are two sides of the same thing. Those whose desires rule them will always complain that their desires are not met.

**A textual note.** The SBLGNT and the Byzantine text read αὐτῶν (*autōn*), "their," while NA28 reads ἑαυτῶν (*heautōn*), "their own." The difference is minimal. The LSV's "their own" matches the sense of either.[^c9-4]

### "and their mouth speaks swollen words"

The Greek τὸ στόμα αὐτῶν λαλεῖ ὑπέρογκα (*to stoma autōn lalei hyperonka*) means literally "their mouth speaks swollen things." The adjective ὑπέρογκος (*hyperonkos*), from ὑπέρ (*hyper*, "over") and ὄγκος (*onkos*, "bulk, mass"), means "excessively large, immoderate," and in later Greek it was used of arrogant, bombastic speech.[^c9-5] The LSV's "swollen words" is vivid and accurate. The ASV's "great swelling words" and the BSB's "arrogantly" capture the sense.

The phrase has a striking biblical precedent. In the Greek version of Daniel, the arrogant king of the end time "shall speak swollen things" (λαλήσει ὑπέρογκα, *lalēsei hyperonka*) against the God of gods (Dan 11:36, Theodotion).[^c9-6] The Hebrew behind it says he "speaks wonderful things against the God of gods" (Dan 11:36, LSV). Daniel also describes the little horn with "a mouth speaking great things" (Dan 7:8, LSV). 1 Enoch, which Jude has just quoted, accuses sinners of speaking "proud and hard words / With your impure mouths against His greatness" (1 Enoch 5:4) and "proud and insolent words against His righteousness" (101:3).[^c9-7] Jude's phrase places the intruders' boasting in this line of arrogant speech against God.

What did their "swollen words" sound like? Perhaps grand claims about their visions (v. 8), their freedom (v. 4), or their spiritual superiority (v. 19). Second Peter's parallel adds content: "speaking swollen words of vanity, they entice in desires of the flesh" (2 Pet 2:18, LSV). Their impressive talk was a means of luring others into their way of life.

### "giving admiration to persons"

The phrase θαυμάζοντες πρόσωπα (*thaumazontes prosōpa*) means literally "admiring faces." It is not natural Greek. It is a Hebrew idiom rendered word for word in the Greek Old Testament. The Hebrew expression "to lift up the face" of someone (*nāśāʾ pānîm*) means to show favor or partiality, especially to the powerful. The Septuagint often translated it as θαυμάζειν πρόσωπον, "to admire a face."[^c9-8] For example:

- "you do not lift up the face of the poor, nor honor the face of the great" (Lev 19:15, LSV), where the Septuagint has "you shall not admire the face of the powerful";
- "your God YHWH … who does not accept by appearances, nor takes a bribe" (Deut 10:17, LSV), where the Septuagint has "who does not admire a face";
- "[It] is not good to discern faces" (Prov 28:21, LSV);
- and, strikingly, in the Sodom narrative itself, the angel's words to Lot, "Behold, I have also accepted your face for this thing" (Gen 19:21, LSV), where the Septuagint has ἐθαύμασά σου τὸ πρόσωπον, "I have admired your face," meaning "I have granted your request."

The New Testament uses a related compound for the same vice: "do not hold the faith of the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ in favor by appearance" (Jas 2:1, LSV); "if you favor by appearance, you work sin" (Jas 2:9, LSV). Peter at Cornelius's house declares that "God is not favoring by appearance" (Acts 10:34, LSV).

The intruders, then, show favoritism. They flatter and court people of influence.

### "for the sake of profit"

The reason for their flattery is ὠφελείας χάριν (*ōpheleias charin*), "for the sake of advantage" or "for profit." The noun ὠφέλεια (*ōpheleia*) means "help, benefit, advantage."[^c9-9] The word χάριν (*charin*), used here as a preposition meaning "for the sake of," is in origin the accusative of χάρις (*charis*), "grace." It is tempting to see an irony: the intruders, who turned the *grace* (χάρις) of God into license (v. 4), now flatter people *for the sake of* (χάριν) profit. Whether Jude intended the echo is uncertain, since χάριν was an everyday preposition, but attentive readers may have heard it.

The combination is telling. Those who grumble against God and speak arrogantly against him are servile toward people who can reward them. Paul describes similar figures: "such do not serve our Lord Jesus Christ, but their own belly; and through the good word and fair speech they deceive the hearts of the harmless" (Rom 16:18, LSV). Paul contrasts his own ministry: "at no time did we come with speech of flattery … nor in a pretext for covetousness" (1 Thess 2:5, LSV). Balaam's "reward" (v. 11) and the self-feeding shepherds (v. 12) find their counterpart here. The intruders' speech, like their leadership, serves their own advantage.

**A caution about the "admiring faces" idiom.** Because θαυμάζω normally means "to marvel, wonder at," a reader without the Septuagint background might think the intruders were simply impressed by people. The phrase is a fixed idiom for partiality and favoritism. Here, as often, the Greek of the New Testament makes sense only against the Greek of the Old Testament.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Patronage and flattery.** The Greco-Roman world was structured by patronage. Wealthy patrons supported clients, who repaid them with loyalty, public praise, and services. Flattery was the lubricant of the system, and moralists from Theophrastus to Plutarch wrote about how to tell a flatterer from a friend. Early Christian communities, which met in the homes of wealthier members and depended on their hospitality, were not immune to these dynamics. James warns against honoring the rich man in fine clothes and dishonoring the poor man (Jas 2:1–9). Jude's intruders seem to have courted influential members of the community, perhaps the hosts of the love feasts, for their own advantage.

**Moral character types.** Greek moral writers catalogued human vices in sketches of recognizable types, including the flatterer, the grumbler, the boaster, and the toady. Jude's string of vivid descriptors resembles such sketches, though his categories are shaped by Scripture: the murmurers of the wilderness, the arrogant speakers of Daniel and Enoch, the partial judges condemned by the Law.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Exodus 16; Numbers 14; 1 Corinthians 10:10 (background).** The wilderness murmuring is the main background for "grumblers." Jude's readers would connect v. 16 with v. 5: those who murmured perished.

**Numbers 16:11; 17:5 (Korah).** Korah's rebellion was a "murmuring." The grumblers of v. 16 walk in Korah's "controversy" (v. 11).

**Daniel 7:8; 11:36 (verbal parallel).** The arrogant king who "speaks wonderful things," Greek "swollen things," against God provides the closest biblical parallel to "swollen words."

**Leviticus 19:15; Deuteronomy 10:17; James 2:1–9; Acts 10:34 (idiom and theme).** Partiality is condemned in the Law, and God himself is the model of impartiality.

**Romans 16:17–18; 1 Thessalonians 2:5 (shared theme).** Paul's warnings against those who serve their own appetites with "fair speech," and his own refusal of flattery, parallel Jude's charge.

**2 Peter 2:18 (literary parallel).** "Swollen words of vanity" used to entice others.

**1 Enoch 5:4; 101:3 (source tradition).** Proud and hard words against God.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. How does v. 16 relate to the Enoch quotation?** Most interpreters see v. 16 as the application of the quotation, especially of its final phrase about "harsh [words] … against Him." The intruders' grumbling, boasting, and flattery are the "harsh words" of the prophecy in present form.

**2. Whom did the intruders flatter?** Jude does not say. Suggestions include wealthy members who hosted the community, people who might become followers and supporters, and church leaders whose approval they sought. The general point, that they cultivated people for gain, does not depend on a specific identification.

**3. Is all complaint sinful?** Scripture contains many complaints addressed to God, the laments of the Psalms, of Job, and of Jeremiah, which are not condemned. The difference lies in the posture. Lament brings pain *to* God in trust. Grumbling speaks *against* God in resentment and unbelief. The wilderness murmuring that Jude recalls was a refusal to trust the God who had delivered them (Num 14:11). Jude's word μεμψίμοιροι, "blaming one's lot," captures this spirit of grievance against God's providence.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verse 16 completes the third "text and application" unit (vv. 14–16), applying Enoch's prophecy to the intruders. It also gathers threads from earlier in the letter: the murmuring of the wilderness generation (v. 5), the slander of glorious ones (vv. 8, 10), Korah's rebellion (v. 11), and Balaam's greed (v. 11). And it prepares for v. 18, where the apostles' prediction of "scoffers, following after their own lusts" repeats the phrase about walking according to lusts.

Throughout this middle section, speech is a recurring concern: slander (vv. 8–10), harsh words (v. 15), grumbling and swollen words (v. 16), and scoffing (v. 18). By contrast, the readers' speech is to be prayer "in the Holy Spirit" (v. 20) and the letter ends in praise (vv. 24–25).

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Gratitude as resistance.** Grumbling can become a habit of mind that colors every blessing with grievance, as in Theophrastus's grumbler who received a gift and complained he had not been invited to dinner. Jude's portrait may invite believers to examine their own speech about God, their circumstances, and their communities, and to cultivate thankfulness as a spiritual discipline.

**Lament vs. grumbling.** Christians sometimes suppress honest lament out of fear that it is "grumbling." Scripture's laments show that bringing pain to God in faith is not the sin Jude describes. The question is whether complaint is addressed to God in trust or spoken against God in resentment.

**Flattery and influence.** In churches as in every institution, there are temptations to court those with money, status, or power. James and Jude both condemn partiality. Communities may consider whether they treat wealthy or influential members differently, and whether their leaders' speech is shaped by the desire for advantage.

**Inflated speech.** "Swollen words" are common in religious marketing, political rhetoric, and online self-presentation. Jude's phrase may prompt reflection on the difference between speech that serves truth and speech that serves the speaker's image.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Why do you think grumbling was treated so seriously in the wilderness narratives and in Jude? What does grumbling reveal about a person's relationship with God?
2. How would you distinguish biblical lament from the grumbling Jude condemns? Can you think of examples from Scripture or experience?
3. Jude describes people who are arrogant toward God and flattering toward people. Why do you think these two traits often go together?
4. Where do you see the temptation to "admire faces," to show favoritism for advantage, in your church, workplace, or community?
5. What practices might help cultivate contentment and gratitude in a culture that often encourages complaint?
