# Chapter 11. Building, Praying, Keeping, Waiting (Jude 20–21)

## A. The Text

> **20** But you, beloved, building yourselves up on your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Spirit, **21** keep yourselves in the love of God, waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ [resulting] in continuous life;
>
> — Jude 20–21, LSV

## B. Plain-English Orientation

After the long exposure of the intruders, Jude finally tells his readers what to do. He began by urging them to "fight for the faith" (v. 3). Here is what that fight consists of. It is not, first of all, an attack on the intruders. It is a way of life:

- **building** one another up on the foundation of the holy faith;
- **praying** in the power of the Holy Spirit;
- **keeping** themselves in God's love;
- **waiting** with hope for the mercy of Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life.

The grammar shows what matters most. There is one main command, "keep yourselves in the love of God," and three supporting activities expressed as participles: building, praying, and waiting. These are the means by which believers remain in the love that God has already shown them (v. 1).

The passage also has a striking shape. It mentions the Holy Spirit, God (the Father), and the Lord Jesus Christ, and it gathers faith, love, and hope, the three great Christian virtues. In two verses Jude sketches the whole Christian life.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### "But you, beloved"

For the third and last time, Jude addresses his readers as "beloved" (vv. 3, 17, 20). The emphatic Ὑμεῖς δέ (*Hymeis de*), "But you," repeats the turn of v. 17 and sets the readers against the intruders just described. The intruders are "not having the Spirit" (v. 19). The readers pray "in the Holy Spirit" (v. 20). The contrast could hardly be sharper.

### "building yourselves up on your most holy faith"

The participle ἐποικοδομοῦντες (*epoikodomountes*), "building up on," comes from the language of construction: οἰκοδομέω (*oikodomeō*), "to build a house," with the prefix ἐπί (*epi*), "upon." The New Testament uses this image often for the growth of believers and of the church. Paul writes: "I have laid a foundation, and another builds on [it], for no one is able to lay another foundation except that which is laid, which is Jesus the Christ" (1 Cor 3:10–11, LSV). Believers are "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ Himself being chief corner-[stone]" (Eph 2:20, LSV), and are to be "rooted and built up in Him, and confirmed in the faith" (Col 2:7, LSV).

**"Yourselves"** (ἑαυτούς, *heautous*) is a reflexive pronoun. In the plural it often has a reciprocal sense, "one another." Paul uses the same pronoun when he tells the Colossians to be "teaching and admonishing each other" (νουθετοῦντες ἑαυτούς, Col 3:16, LSV), and the Ephesians to be "forgiving one another" (χαριζόμενοι ἑαυτοῖς, Eph 4:32, LSV).[^c11-1] Jude is not describing private self-improvement. He is describing a community building each other up: "build up one another, as also you do" (1 Thess 5:11, LSV).

**"On your most holy faith"** (τῇ ἁγιωτάτῃ ὑμῶν πίστει, *tē hagiōtatē hymōn pistei*). The foundation is "the faith," the same faith "once delivered to the holy ones" that Jude urged his readers to contend for in v. 3. The phrase "your most holy faith" ties the end of the letter to its beginning. Contending for the faith (v. 3) and building on the faith (v. 20) are the same activity seen from two sides.

The adjective ἁγιωτάτῃ (*hagiōtatē*), "most holy," is the superlative of ἅγιος (*hagios*), "holy." It is the only superlative of that adjective in the New Testament.[^c11-2] Jude chooses the strongest possible word. The faith is not a human tradition to be adjusted to taste. It is "most holy," set apart by God and belonging to him. The intruders have turned grace into licentiousness (v. 4). The readers build on a faith that is holy through and through.

The Byzantine text puts the words in a different order, "on your most holy faith building yourselves up," but the meaning is the same.[^c11-3]

### "praying in the Holy Spirit"

The second participle, προσευχόμενοι (*proseuchomenoi*), "praying," is qualified by ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ (*en pneumati hagiō*), "in the Holy Spirit." See the deep dive below. In brief, it describes prayer that the Holy Spirit inspires, empowers, and guides. That is the opposite of the intruders, who are "not having the Spirit" (v. 19), and of their "dreaming" (v. 8), which Jude regards as false spirituality.

### "keep yourselves in the love of God"

Here is the main verb of the sentence and the central command of the letter: ἑαυτοὺς ἐν ἀγάπῃ θεοῦ τηρήσατε (*heautous en agapē theou tērēsate*), "keep yourselves in the love of God." The aorist imperative τηρήσατε (*tērēsate*) is the fifth and final use of τηρέω, "keep," the word that runs through the whole letter:

- the readers "having been kept in Jesus Christ" (v. 1);
- the angels who "did not keep" their place (v. 6);
- the angels whom God "has kept" in bonds (v. 6);
- the darkness that "has been kept" for the wandering stars (v. 13);
- and now, "keep yourselves" (v. 21).

The readers have been kept by God, and now they are to keep themselves. The angels did not keep their position. The readers must keep theirs. See the deep dive below.

**"The love of God"** (ἀγάπῃ θεοῦ, *agapē theou*) could grammatically mean either God's love for us or our love for God. In Jude, the first is clearly primary. The readers are "having been loved in God [the] Father" (v. 1). To "keep yourselves in the love of God" is to remain in the place where God's love has put you. Jesus said: "According as the Father loved Me, I also loved you, remain in My love; if you may keep My commands, you will remain in My love" (John 15:9–10, LSV). Responding love for God is implied, but the foundation is God's love.

### "waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ"

The third participle is προσδεχόμενοι (*prosdechomenoi*), "waiting for, expecting." It is the word used of Simeon, who was "looking for the comforting of Israel" (Luke 2:25, LSV), and of believers "waiting for the blessed hope and appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (Titus 2:13, LSV). It describes active, eager expectation, not passive delay.

What the readers wait for is "the mercy (ἔλεος, *eleos*) of our Lord Jesus Christ." Mercy was the first gift Jude wished his readers (v. 2). Here it is the mercy they will receive at Christ's coming, when he comes to judge (vv. 14–15). Paul prays for a friend, "may the LORD give to him to find kindness from the LORD in that day" (2 Tim 1:18, LSV), using the same Greek word, ἔλεος. The coming that means judgment for the ungodly (vv. 14–15) means mercy for those who wait for it.

### "[resulting] in continuous life"

The final phrase, εἰς ζωὴν αἰώνιον (*eis zōēn aiōnion*), "into eternal life," names the goal of the waiting. The LSV supplies "[resulting]" to show that the Greek preposition εἰς expresses direction or result. Christ's mercy leads to eternal life. The LSV's "continuous life" reflects its consistent rendering of αἰώνιος (*aiōnios*). In New Testament usage the phrase means both life without end and the life that belongs to the age to come.

The contrast with the intruders' destiny is deliberate. For them, "the gloom of the darkness throughout the age has been kept" (v. 13) and "continuous fire" is the example (v. 7). For the readers, the same word αἰώνιος describes life. Two destinies are set before the readers, and Jude's command is to remain in the love that leads to life.

### The shape of vv. 20–21

The sentence has a clear structure:

| Element | Greek | Relation |
|---|---|---|
| building yourselves up on your most holy faith | ἐποικοδομοῦντες | participle (means) |
| praying in the Holy Spirit | προσευχόμενοι | participle (means) |
| **keep yourselves in the love of God** | **τηρήσατε** | **main imperative** |
| waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ | προσδεχόμενοι | participle (accompanying attitude) |
| into eternal life | εἰς ζωὴν αἰώνιον | goal |

Two further patterns appear:

- **A Trinitarian pattern:** the Holy Spirit (v. 20), the love of God the Father (v. 21), and the mercy of the Lord Jesus Christ (v. 21). Jude does not offer a doctrine of the Trinity, but he speaks naturally of Spirit, Father, and Son as together the source of the Christian's life. Paul does the same in 2 Cor 13:14.
- **A pattern of faith, love, and hope:** "your most holy faith" (v. 20), "the love of God" (v. 21), and "waiting" for mercy and eternal life (v. 21), which is hope. Paul's famous triad is "faith, hope, love—these three" (1 Cor 13:13, LSV). He also speaks of "your work of faith, and the labor of the love, and the endurance of the hope" (1 Thess 1:3, LSV). Jude's version may reflect a shared early Christian pattern.

### Deep dive: Praying in the Holy Spirit

What does it mean to pray "in the Holy Spirit"? The phrase ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ can be read in several ways, and Christian traditions have emphasized different aspects.

**The New Testament background.** The closest parallel is Eph 6:18: "praying at all times in the Spirit, and in this, watching in all perseverance and supplication for all the holy ones" (LSV). Paul also describes the Spirit's role in prayer:

- The Spirit enables the most basic Christian prayer, the cry to God as Father: "you received [the] Spirit of adoption in which we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Rom 8:15, LSV); "God sent forth the Spirit of His Son into your hearts, crying, 'Abba! Father!'" (Gal 4:6, LSV).
- The Spirit helps believers in their weakness: "the Spirit helps our weaknesses; for what we may pray for, as it is necessary, we have not known, but [this] One—the Spirit—makes intercession for us with unutterable groanings" (Rom 8:26, LSV).
- In 1 Corinthians, Paul speaks of praying "in an [unknown] tongue" and says, "I will pray [in] the spirit, and I will also pray [with] understanding" (1 Cor 14:14–15, LSV).

Jesus speaks of worship "in spirit and truth" (John 4:23–24), and Paul of "serving God by the Spirit" (Phil 3:3, LSV).

**Interpretations.**

1. **Prayer inspired and guided by the Spirit.** Most interpreters understand Jude to mean prayer that is prompted, empowered, and shaped by the Holy Spirit, prayer that flows from the Spirit's presence in the believer and the community. On this reading, the phrase describes all genuine Christian prayer, including the "Abba, Father" of adoption and the Spirit's help in weakness.
2. **Charismatic or ecstatic prayer.** Some interpreters, especially in Pentecostal and charismatic traditions, see in Jude 20 a reference to Spirit-inspired prayer of the kind described in 1 Corinthians 14, including prayer in tongues. That is a possible reading, since Jude's communities, like Paul's, were probably familiar with such phenomena. Nothing in Jude's wording, however, requires it.
3. **Prayer within the sphere of the Spirit**, as opposed to the "natural" sphere of v. 19. This reading emphasizes the contrast with the intruders. Whatever the intruders' spiritual claims, true prayer happens in the realm of the Holy Spirit.

These readings are not exclusive. What is clear from the context is Jude's contrast. The intruders appealed to "dreams" and claimed spiritual superiority, but in Jude's judgment they were "not having the Spirit." The readers' answer is not to reject spiritual experience but to pray in the Spirit, inside the community, on the foundation of the faith, and in the love of God. In Jude's view, genuine spirituality is marked by prayer, holiness, love, and humility, not by private revelations that license sin.

**Prayer as part of contending.** It is significant that prayer is one of the means by which believers "fight for the faith" (v. 3). Jude does not imagine that false teaching can be met only with arguments. A praying community, dependent on the Spirit, is itself a defense of the faith.

### Deep dive: Keeping oneself in the love of God

Jude's central command, "keep yourselves in the love of God," raises a question that runs through the whole letter: how does the believer's keeping relate to God's keeping?

**God's keeping comes first.** The letter opens by describing the readers as "having been kept in Jesus Christ" (v. 1), or, as the Greek dative may also mean, kept *for* him. The perfect participle describes a settled state. It closes with praise to "Him who is able to guard you without stumbling" (v. 24). The readers' security rests on God's action, which frames the whole letter.

**The believer's keeping is real.** Between those two statements stands the command of v. 21: "keep yourselves." It is not a suggestion. The negative examples in the letter show what failure to keep looks like: the wilderness generation that did not believe (v. 5) and the angels who "did not keep their own principality" (v. 6).

**How the two fit together.** Jude does not explain the relationship theoretically, but the structure of vv. 20–21 suggests a practical answer. The believer keeps himself or herself in God's love not by heroic effort but by staying where God's love is found: building on the faith, praying in the Spirit, and waiting for Christ's mercy. These are not ways of earning God's love. They are ways of remaining in it. Jesus' words in John 15 offer a parallel: "remain in My love; if you may keep My commands, you will remain in My love" (John 15:9–10, LSV). The love comes first, and obedience is the way of abiding in it.

An image may help. The love of God is like a sheltered harbor. God has brought the readers into it (v. 1), and God is able to keep them there (v. 24). The command "keep yourselves in the love of God" means: do not sail out of the harbor. Build up the community, pray, wait for the Lord, and you will remain where you have been placed.

**Different traditions, one command.** As noted in Chapter 1, Christian traditions explain the relationship between divine preservation and human perseverance differently. All can agree on Jude's practical point: the command to keep oneself in God's love is meant seriously, and the means of obeying it are building, praying, and waiting.

**A grammatical note on the participles.** Greek participles that accompany an imperative often take on some of its force. Many translations therefore render vv. 20–21 as a series of commands: ‘build yourselves up … pray … keep yourselves … wait’ (compare the WEB and BSB). That is legitimate. It is still worth seeing that Jude subordinates building, praying, and waiting to the single command to keep oneself in God's love. They are not three extra tasks. They are how the one task is done.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Prayer in early Christian gatherings.** From the beginning, prayer was central to Christian community life. The Jerusalem believers were "continuing steadfastly in … the prayers" (Acts 2:42, LSV). Paul's letters are full of prayer reports and requests. The *Didache* provides texts of thanksgiving prayers and instructs believers to pray the Lord's Prayer three times a day.[^c11-4] Jude's call to pray in the Spirit belongs to this common practice. Since the love feasts were also occasions of prayer, the instruction may have had a communal setting in mind.

**Waiting for the Lord.** The early Christians lived in expectation of Christ's return. The Aramaic prayer *Maranatha*, usually understood as ‘Our Lord, come!’ (1 Cor 16:22; *Didache* 10.6), preserved this hope in the language of the earliest Palestinian believers. (The LSV renders it "The LORD has come!" in 1 Cor 16:22, reflecting a different way of dividing the Aramaic words.)[^c11-5] Jude's "waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ" expresses the same expectation, colored by the judgment scene he has just quoted.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**1 Corinthians 3:10–11; Ephesians 2:20–22; Colossians 2:7; Acts 20:32 (building imagery).** The church as a building on the foundation of Christ and the apostolic faith. Jude's "building yourselves up on your most holy faith" belongs to this family of images.

**Ephesians 6:18; Romans 8:15–16, 26–27; Galatians 4:6 (prayer in the Spirit).** The closest parallels to Jude's "praying in the Holy Spirit."

**John 15:9–10; 1 John 4:16 (remaining in love).** "God is love, and he who is remaining in love, remains in God, and God in him" (1 John 4:16, LSV). The Johannine language of "remaining" in God's love parallels Jude's "keep yourselves in the love of God."

**Romans 5:5; 8:39 (the love of God).** "The love of God has been poured forth in our hearts through the Holy Spirit" (Rom 5:5, LSV). Paul, like Jude, connects God's love and the Spirit.

**Titus 2:13; Luke 2:25 (waiting).** The same verb of eager expectation.

**1 Corinthians 13:13; 1 Thessalonians 1:3; 5:8 (faith, love, hope).** The triad that Jude's vv. 20–21 seem to echo.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Is the reference to the Holy Spirit Trinitarian?** Jude's mention of the Holy Spirit, God, and the Lord Jesus Christ in two verses is often noted as an early example of the threefold pattern that later Christian theology articulated as the doctrine of the Trinity. Jude does not reflect on the relationship among the three. His language reflects the experience of early Christians, who knew God as Father, Jesus as Lord, and the Spirit as present power.

**2. What kind of prayer is "in the Holy Spirit"?** See the deep dive. Interpretations range from all Spirit-led prayer to specifically charismatic forms.

**3. Is "the love of God" God's love or our love?** God's love is primary (v. 1), though a responding love is implied.

**4. Is the "mercy" of v. 21 present or future?** It is future, the mercy shown at Christ's coming and judgment. The waiting has present effects: it shapes how believers live now.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verses 20–21 are the positive counterpart to the whole central section. Each element answers something in the description of the intruders:

| The intruders | The readers |
|---|---|
| pervert grace into license (v. 4) | build on the most holy faith (v. 20) |
| "dreaming" (v. 8); "not having the Spirit" (v. 19) | praying in the Holy Spirit (v. 20) |
| stains (or reefs) at the love feasts (v. 12) | keep themselves in the love of God (v. 21) |
| darkness kept for them forever (v. 13) | waiting for mercy leading to eternal life (v. 21) |

The passage also completes the frame that began in vv. 1–2. Love, keeping, mercy, God, and Jesus Christ all return. Verse 3's call to contend for the faith is now explained: contending means building, praying, keeping, and waiting.

Finally, vv. 20–21 prepare for vv. 22–23. Those who are kept in God's love and wait for Christ's mercy are the ones who can show mercy to others. The movement is from receiving mercy to extending it.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**The positive shape of faithfulness.** When Christians face false teaching or moral confusion, it is easy to define faithfulness only by what one opposes. Jude's instructions are almost entirely positive: build, pray, keep, wait. Communities under pressure may find that their best defense is a deep, positive spiritual life rather than constant vigilance against enemies.

**Community, not isolation.** "Building yourselves up" is communal. Jude assumes that believers grow together, through shared teaching, worship, encouragement, and accountability. Modern individualism can make faith a private project. Jude's grammar resists that.

**Prayer as spiritual defense.** Prayer in the Spirit is part of contending for the faith. Churches facing division or confusion might give prayer, honest and dependent, a central place in their response, not merely a ceremonial one.

**Living from love toward hope.** The passage moves from the love of God, already given, to the mercy of Christ, still awaited. The Christian life is lived between those two points, secure in love and leaning toward hope. That orientation may help believers handle both complacency and anxiety.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Jude's response to false teaching is mostly positive: build, pray, keep, wait. Why might that be more effective than a purely defensive strategy?
2. How do you understand "praying in the Holy Spirit"? How has your tradition shaped your understanding?
3. What practices help you, or your community, "keep" yourselves in the love of God? Which of Jude's three activities (building, praying, waiting) is most neglected in your experience?
4. Jude speaks of the faith as "most holy." What does it mean to treat the faith as holy rather than as a human tradition to be adjusted?
5. How does waiting for "the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ" shape the way you think about the future and live in the present?
