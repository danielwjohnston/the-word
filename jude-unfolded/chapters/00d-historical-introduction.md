# Historical and Literary Introduction to Jude {.unnumbered}

Before walking through Jude verse by verse, it helps to ask the questions any careful reader asks of an ancient letter. Who wrote it? When, and to whom? What was happening that made it necessary? What kind of writing is it, and what world of ideas does it assume? How did the early church receive it? For a letter as short as Jude, the answers are often less certain than we would like. The evidence is still worth laying out, both for what it shows and for what it leaves open.

## 1. "Judas … brother of James": who wrote the letter?

The letter opens:

> Judas, a servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James, …
>
> — Jude 1, LSV

The Greek name Ἰούδας (*Ioudas*) is the Greek form of the Hebrew *Yehudah*, Judah, the name of Jacob's fourth son and of the tribe and land descended from him. English Bibles usually write "Jude" for the author of this letter. The convention distinguishes him from Judas Iscariot, but the Greek name is the same, and the LSV keeps "Judas." The name was very common among Jews of the period, so the author's identity depends on the clue he gives: he is the "brother of James."

### The candidates

Several men named Judas appear in the New Testament:

1. **Judas the brother of Jesus.** Mark lists him among Jesus' brothers: "Is this not the carpenter, the Son of Mary, and brother of James, and Joses, and Judas, and Simon?" (Mark 6:3, LSV; cf. Matt 13:55).
2. **Judas "of James,"** one of the Twelve, listed by Luke: "Judas of James, and Judas Iscariot, who also became betrayer" (Luke 6:16, LSV; cf. Acts 1:13). John calls him "Judas … (not the Iscariot)" (John 14:22, LSV). The Greek Ἰούδας Ἰακώβου most naturally means "Judas son of James," which is the usual way of using a genitive with a personal name. It does not mean "brother of James."
3. **Judas called Barsabbas**, a prophet and leader in the Jerusalem church who carried the apostolic letter to Antioch (Acts 15:22, 32).
4. **Judas Thomas**, a figure of later Syriac Christian tradition in which the apostle Thomas ("twin") is named Judas.

Among these, the first is the only one for whom "brother of James" is a natural self-description. Only one James in early Christianity was famous enough to identify someone simply by naming him: James of Jerusalem, "the brother of the LORD" (Gal 1:19, LSV). Paul names him as one of the "pillars" of the Jerusalem church (Gal 2:9). He leads the council in Acts 15 and receives Paul in Acts 21:18, and tradition takes him to be the author of the Letter of James, who also calls himself simply "a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ" (Jas 1:1, LSV). A Judas who introduces himself as "brother of James" is almost certainly claiming to be the brother of *that* James, and so a brother of Jesus.

### Why "brother of James" and not "brother of the Lord"?

If Jude was the brother of Jesus, why did he not say so? Ancient readers asked the same question. Clement of Alexandria, writing around AD 200, answered with a comment that later readers have found persuasive:

> Jude, who wrote the Catholic Epistle, the brother of the sons of Joseph, and very religious, while knowing the near relationship of the Lord, yet did not say that he himself was His brother. But what said he? Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ,—of Him as Lord; but the brother of James.[^i-1]

Several factors probably worked together. The family relationship was no longer the defining fact; after the resurrection, Jesus' brothers came to know him as Lord. The Gospels remember that during Jesus' ministry "not even His brothers were believing in Him" (John 7:5, LSV). Yet after Easter they appear among the praying disciples (Acts 1:14), and Paul knows that the risen Christ "appeared to James" (1 Cor 15:7, LSV). A brother by birth who had become a servant by faith might well prefer to call himself the second. Naming James also placed Jude within a recognized line of authority without claiming the title of apostle, which he appears to reserve for others (v. 17).

### The family of Jesus in early tradition

Two pieces of evidence outside the Gospels fill out the picture.

First, Paul mentions in passing that "the brothers of the LORD" traveled on missionary work with their wives (1 Cor 9:5, LSV). That remark, written in the mid-50s, shows that at least some of Jesus' brothers besides James were known as traveling preachers among the churches. Jude, who writes to readers he knows and who are at some distance from him, fits that profile.

Second, the second-century Jewish-Christian writer Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius, tells a story about Jude's grandsons. When the emperor Domitian (reigned AD 81–96) feared the descendants of David, "the grandchildren of Jude, who is said to have been the Lord's brother according to the flesh," were brought before him. They owned a small farm worth nine thousand denarii, showed him the calluses on their hands from working it, and explained that Christ's kingdom was "not a temporal nor an earthly kingdom, but a heavenly and angelic one." Domitian let them go as harmless. Afterward, Hegesippus says, they "ruled the churches because they were witnesses and were also relatives of the Lord," and they lived until the time of Trajan.[^i-2] The story probably includes some legendary color, but it preserves a memory of Jude's family as Galilean farmers, rooted in Jewish Christianity and respected in the churches.

### Deep dive: who were Jesus' "brothers"?

The New Testament names four brothers of Jesus (James, Joses or Joseph, Judas, and Simon) and mentions sisters (Mark 6:3). Christians have understood these relationships in three main ways, and the differences matter for how readers picture Jude.

- **Full brothers (the "Helvidian" view, after Helvidius, a fourth-century writer).** On this view they were children of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus. Most Protestant interpreters hold it, pointing to the plain sense of the word ἀδελφός (*adelphos*) and to texts such as Matt 1:25.
- **Older half-brothers, sons of Joseph by an earlier marriage (the "Epiphanian" view, after Epiphanius of Salamis).** This view is attested in second-century writings. It has been common in Eastern Orthodox tradition and fits Clement's description of Jude as "the brother of the sons of Joseph."
- **Cousins (the "Hieronymian" view, after Jerome).** This view takes *adelphos* in the broader sense of "kinsman," a sense it can carry in Jewish usage. It became standard in the Latin West and is common in Roman Catholic interpretation.

All three views agree on what matters most for this book: Jude belonged to Jesus' own family circle, grew up near him, and came to call him "Master and Lord" (v. 4). Readers who hold different views of Mary can read Jude in the same way.

### Did Jude himself write it?

Many scholars accept that the letter was written by Jude, the brother of Jesus. Many others think it was written later by someone using his name, a practice called pseudonymity that was known in the ancient world. The main arguments for a later writer are these:

- **The Greek is good.** Jude's vocabulary is rich, with sixteen words found nowhere else in the New Testament, and his style is vivid and carefully patterned. Could a Galilean villager write like this?
- **"The faith once delivered" (v. 3)** is said to treat Christianity as a fixed body of doctrine, which some regard as typical of a later, more institutional stage.
- **The apostles seem to belong to the past** (v. 17).

Defenders of authenticity reply:

- **Greek was widely used in Galilee**, and a family whose members traveled as missionaries in the Greek-speaking world (1 Cor 9:5) had every opportunity to learn it well. Ancient authors also commonly used secretaries, who could polish style. The quality of the Greek is a real question, but it does not settle authorship.
- **"The faith" as the content of the Christian message** already appears in Paul's earliest letters: "he who is persecuting us then, now proclaims good news—the faith that he was then destroying" (Gal 1:23, LSV). Language about "delivering" teaching is also early (1 Cor 11:2; 15:3).
- **Verse 17 asks readers to remember what the apostles told *them***. That requires only that the apostles had preached to these readers earlier, not that they were all long dead.
- **The choice of name is the strongest argument.** A later writer who wanted authority would hardly choose an obscure figure like Jude, and having chosen him, would likely have added the impressive title "brother of the Lord." A modest self-description fits the real Jude better than an imitator.

This book treats authorship by Jude the brother of Jesus as a responsible conclusion that fits the evidence well. It does not regard the question as closed, and very little of the letter's interpretation depends on the answer.[^i-3]

## 2. Language and style

Jude writes a compact, energetic, and artful Greek. Several features stand out, and they will recur throughout this book:

- **Rare words.** Of about 226 distinct words in Jude, sixteen occur nowhere else in the New Testament, among them the words meaning ‘contend for’ (v. 3), ‘slip in’ (v. 4), ‘reefs’ (v. 12), ‘late-autumn’ (v. 12), ‘foam up’ (v. 13), ‘wanderers’ (v. 13), ‘grumblers’ and ‘fault-finders’ (v. 16), ‘make divisions’ (v. 19), and ‘without stumbling’ (v. 24).[^i-4]
- **Triads.** Jude thinks in threes: called, loved, kept (v. 1); mercy, peace, love (v. 2); three examples of judgment (vv. 5–7); three sins of the ‘dreamers’ (v. 8); Cain, Balaam, Korah (v. 11); three groups to whom mercy is shown (vv. 22–23).
- **Catchwords.** Key words return in new settings and bind the letter together. The clearest is "keep" (τηρέω, *tēreō*): the readers are "kept" (v. 1), the angels did not "keep" their position and are "kept" for judgment (v. 6), darkness is "kept" for the wandering stars (v. 13), and the readers are to "keep" themselves in God's love (v. 21) as they trust the God who is able to "guard" them (v. 24).
- **The "these" pattern.** After each example or prophecy, Jude turns to the troublemakers with "these" (οὗτοι, *houtoi*): "those dreaming" (v. 8, where the LSV renders the word "those"), "these, indeed, whatever [things] they have not known" (v. 10), "These are stains" (v. 12), "These are … grumblers" (v. 16), "these are those causing divisions" (v. 19).
- **Vivid images drawn from nature and from Israel's story.**

## 3. Date and setting

The letter gives no explicit date. If Jude the brother of Jesus wrote it, it was written within his lifetime, probably before the 90s and possibly much earlier, since Hegesippus's story about Domitian concerns his grandsons. Some scholars date it to the 50s or early 60s, which would make it one of the earliest New Testament writings. Others place it between 70 and 100. The letter's horizon is strikingly Jewish and apocalyptic. It never mentions the destruction of Jerusalem's temple (AD 70), but arguments from silence are weak in so short a letter.

This uncertainty affects the interpretation less than one might expect. Jude's concerns are about holding to the received faith, moral integrity, authority, divisive teachers, and mercy toward the wavering. Those concerns fit the first Christian century at almost any point.

## 4. The recipients

Jude addresses "those called, having been loved … and having been kept" (v. 1), a description of all believers rather than of a particular place. Yet the letter is clearly written to particular people: he calls them "beloved" three times (vv. 3, 17, 20). He knows that intruders have slipped into their community (v. 4) and sit at their love feasts (v. 12), and he can remind them of what the apostles had told them (vv. 17–18).

He also assumes that they will understand compressed references to the exodus, to fallen angels, to Michael and Moses, to Cain, Balaam, and Korah, and to the prophecy of Enoch. Such readers were most likely Jewish Christians, or a mixed community deeply formed by Jewish scriptural interpretation. Suggested locations include Palestine, Syria, Egypt, and Asia Minor. None can be proven, and this book does not build on any of them.

## 5. The troublemakers

Jude does not name the people he opposes. He calls them "certain men" (v. 4), and everything we know about them comes from his description. Gathering his charges gives this profile:

- They are **inside** the community. They "came in … stealthily" (v. 4) and share its meals (v. 12).
- They turn **grace into license**, especially sexual license (vv. 4, 7–8, 16, 18).
- They **deny** Jesus Christ as Master and Lord, probably by the way they live rather than by explicit doctrinal denial (v. 4).
- They appeal to **dreams**, apparently claimed revelations (v. 8).
- They **reject authority** and **slander glorious beings** (vv. 8–10).
- They are **self-serving**, "shepherding themselves" (v. 12), "for the sake of profit" (v. 16), like Balaam "for reward" (v. 11).
- They are **divisive** and, in Jude's judgment, **"not having the Spirit"** (v. 19).

Older commentators often identified them as Gnostics, members of the second-century movements that taught salvation through secret knowledge and despised the material world. The resemblance is partial at best. Jude says nothing about a lower creator-god, a cosmic myth, or a doctrine of secret knowledge. The developed Gnostic systems also belong to a later period than most scholars assign to Jude. The *Pulpit Commentary* already judged that nothing in the letter warrants the supposition that Jude "was thinking of Simon Magus in particular, or of Carpocrates, or any of the early Gnostics," though it allowed that "the seeds which were to develop into the pronounced Gnosticism of a later time were already sown."[^i-5] Jude's own charges suggest something simpler: traveling teachers who claimed prophetic or charismatic authority and taught that grace freed believers from moral law. That picture is this book's reconstruction, built from Jude's words and from early Christian instructions for testing traveling prophets (Chapter 2), and the book treats it as a reconstruction.

## 6. What kind of writing is Jude?

Jude is a **letter**. It has the standard opening (sender, recipients, greeting) and an elaborate closing doxology in place of the usual farewell. In substance it is a **sermon or word of exhortation in letter form**, with a clear rhetorical aim: to move its hearers to "fight for the faith" (v. 3).

Two ways of describing its structure are especially illuminating:

- **A Jewish interpretive pattern.** The central section (vv. 5–19) alternates between *texts* (scriptural examples, a tradition, a prophecy, an apostolic prediction) and *interpretations* introduced by "these" (οὗτοι). J. B. Mayor, following F. H. Chase, noted that the formula οὗτοί εἰσιν ("these are") belongs to apocalyptic writing that explains visions. Zechariah's angel says, "These [are] they whom YHWH has sent" (Zech 1:10, LSV), and Revelation's elder says, "These are those who are coming out of the Great Tribulation" (Rev 7:14, LSV). Mayor suggested that Jude adopted the formula from such writings.[^i-6] The pattern also resembles the *pesher* commentaries among the Dead Sea Scrolls, which quote a prophetic text and then explain its "interpretation" in terms of the community's own time.[^i-6b] It suggests a writer trained in Jewish ways of reading Scripture as prophecy fulfilled in his own day.
- **Greco-Roman rhetoric.** Ancient teachers of speech described the parts of a persuasive address. Quintilian, writing in the first century AD, reports that "most authorities divide the forensic speech into five parts: the exordium, the statement of facts, the proof, the refutation, and the peroration."[^i-7] Read with these categories, Jude has an introduction stating its purpose (v. 3), a statement of the situation (v. 4), the proofs (vv. 5–16), and a concluding appeal (vv. 17–23), framed by the letter opening and the doxology. This is offered as a reading aid; Jude never names these categories.

The two analyses do not compete. A Jewish author writing in Greek could use both inherited Jewish methods and the persuasive patterns any educated speaker of Greek would know.

## 7. Jude's scriptural world

Jude is saturated with Israel's scriptures, yet he never introduces an Old Testament quotation with "it is written." His one formal quotation comes from 1 Enoch (vv. 14–15). His references to the Hebrew Bible are compressed allusions that assume the reader knows the story:

| Jude | Allusion |
|---|---|
| 5 | The exodus and the wilderness unbelief (Exod 12–14; Num 14) |
| 6 | The "sons of God" (Gen 6:1–4) as read in 1 Enoch 6–16 |
| 7 | Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 19) |
| 9 | Michael and Moses' burial (Deut 34:5–6) via later tradition; Michael’s words echo Zech 3:2 ("YHWH pushes against you, O Satan") |
| 11 | Cain (Gen 4), Balaam (Num 22–24; 31:16), Korah (Num 16) |
| 12 | Shepherds feeding themselves (Ezek 34:2); clouds and wind without rain (Prov 25:14) |
| 13 | The restless sea casting up mire (Isa 57:20) |
| 14–15 | Enoch (Gen 5:21–24); 1 Enoch 1:9 |
| 16 | The wilderness grumbling (Exod 16; Num 14) |
| 23 | A brand snatched from the fire; filthy garments (Zech 3:2–4; Amos 4:11) |

One detail suggests that Jude knew his scriptures in Hebrew or Aramaic rather than only in the Greek Septuagint. In Isa 57:20 and Prov 25:14, the passages behind his images of the foaming sea and the rainless clouds, the Hebrew text contains the very details Jude uses: the sea "casts out filth and mire," and there are "clouds and wind without rain" (LSV, translating the Hebrew). The Septuagint versions of both verses lack those details.[^i-8] This is not proof, but it fits a Palestinian Jewish author.

Jude also draws on **Jewish traditions outside the Hebrew Bible**: 1 Enoch, the fullest surviving source for the Watcher story and for the prophecy he quotes, and a tradition about Michael's dispute with the Devil over Moses' body, which early Christian writers associated with a work called the *Assumption* (or *Ascension*) *of Moses*. These references were the main reason some early Christians hesitated over Jude. They are discussed fully in Chapters 3, 4, and 8.

## 8. Jude and 2 Peter

Anyone who reads Jude and then 2 Peter 2 notices the resemblance. The same sequence of themes and images appears in both: intruders who deny the Master (Jude 4; 2 Pet 2:1), sinning angels kept in gloom for judgment (Jude 6; 2 Pet 2:4), Sodom and Gomorrah (Jude 7; 2 Pet 2:6), defiling the flesh and despising authority (Jude 8; 2 Pet 2:10), angels who do not bring a slanderous judgment (Jude 9; 2 Pet 2:11), creatures without reason (Jude 10; 2 Pet 2:12), Balaam's love of reward (Jude 11; 2 Pet 2:15), feasting together (Jude 12; 2 Pet 2:13), waterless clouds and gloom of darkness (Jude 12–13; 2 Pet 2:17), "swollen" words (Jude 16; 2 Pet 2:18), and scoffers foretold by the apostles (Jude 17–18; 2 Pet 3:2–3). Three Greek words occur in the New Testament only in these two letters.

There are three main explanations:

1. **2 Peter used Jude.** Most modern scholars hold this view.[^i-8b] Jude's version is usually the more compact and more Jewish, and it is easier to explain why 2 Peter would omit Jude's quotation from Enoch and his named Michael story than why Jude would add them. 2 Peter also arranges the examples in biblical order (angels, flood, Sodom), while Jude's order follows his own thematic logic.
2. **Jude used 2 Peter.** This was Martin Luther's opinion. He wrote that Jude "refers to the second Epistle of St. Peter, from which it has taken nearly all its words." Some later scholars agreed, among them F. Spitta, as Mayor reports.[^i-8c] This view points to 2 Peter's future tense ("there will be false teachers," 2 Pet 2:1) and Jude's past tense ("there came in," Jude 4), as if Jude were reporting the fulfillment of 2 Peter's prediction.
3. **Both used a common source**, perhaps a body of early Christian teaching about false teachers.

Alfred Plummer, who weighed the question carefully in 1891, admitted that he "remains still uncertain as to the true relation between the two, and that he has inclined sometimes to the one, and sometimes to the other."[^i-8d] That honest uncertainty is a good guide.

The question matters for the study of 2 Peter more than for Jude. This book reads Jude on its own terms and uses 2 Peter as an early interpreter of Jude's language. Appendix C gives the parallel texts side by side.

## 9. How the church received Jude

Jude's place in the New Testament was secure early in some regions and debated in others, and the debate centered on his use of Enoch.

- **Late second century:** the Muratorian Fragment, a Latin list of accepted books whose date is debated, includes "the Epistle of Jude."[^i-9] Clement of Alexandria commented on Jude in his *Hypotyposes*, and part of that commentary survives in Latin.[^i-10] Tertullian, around AD 200, used Jude to defend Enoch's authority: "Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude."[^i-11]
- **Third century:** Origen praised Jude as "a letter of few lines, it is true, but filled with the healthful words of heavenly grace."[^i-12]
- **Early fourth century:** Eusebius placed Jude among the "disputed writings, which are nevertheless recognized by many," noting that "not many of the ancients have mentioned it" but that it had been "read publicly in very many churches."[^i-13]
- **AD 367:** Athanasius's Easter letter listing the books of the New Testament includes "one of Jude" among the seven catholic epistles.[^i-14]
- **Late fourth century:** Jerome explained the hesitation directly: "because in it he quotes from the apocryphal Book of Enoch it is rejected by many. Nevertheless by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures."[^i-15]

At the Reformation, Martin Luther judged in his 1523 sermons on Jude that the letter "cannot be looked upon as being that of one who was truly an Apostle, for the author speaks in it of the Apostles, as being much their junior," and that it "refers to the second Epistle of St. Peter, from which it has taken nearly all its words."[^i-16] John Calvin defended its authority and suggested that Jude drew on ancient tradition, not necessarily on an apocryphal book.[^i-17]

One further fact helps modern readers understand the issue. **The Book of Enoch, which Jude quotes, is itself part of the Old Testament canon of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church and the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church**, though not of other churches or of Judaism.[^i-18] The question "What does it mean that Jude quotes Enoch?" therefore receives different answers in different Christian traditions. Chapter 8 explores it.

## 10. Why Jude matters

Jude has been called the most neglected book of the New Testament. Its neglect is understandable but costly. In twenty-five verses it offers:

- a picture of **Christian identity** as gift before task: called, loved, kept;
- a clear statement that the **gospel has content**, entrusted once for all and worth contending for;
- an unflinching moral seriousness about **grace twisted into license**;
- a demonstration of how the first Christians read **Israel's scriptures and traditions** as speaking to their present;
- a remarkably early and high **Christology**: Jesus is "our only Master and Lord," possibly the one who saved Israel from Egypt, and the coming Lord of Enoch's prophecy;
- a model of **contending without contempt**, defending the faith while showing mercy to the wavering and rescuing those in danger;
- one of the most beautiful **doxologies** in Scripture, which turns the whole letter toward the God who keeps his people.

The chapters that follow try to show how these pieces fit together.
