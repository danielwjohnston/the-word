## B. Plain-English Orientation

With "but you, beloved," Jude turns from the intruders to his readers. He has been building his case from ancient sources: Israel's history, angelic traditions, the stories of Cain, Balaam, and Korah, and Enoch's prophecy. Now he brings it close to home. The readers themselves had heard the apostles warn that in the last days there would be scoffers, driven by ungodly desires. The intruders fulfill that prediction. In Jude's final description of them, they are people who create divisions, who are merely "natural" in their outlook, and who do not have the Spirit.

This is the last "text and application" unit in the letter. Its text is not an ancient prophecy but the recent teaching of the apostles, and its application is the most theologically pointed charge of all: the people who claim spiritual revelation do not have the Spirit.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### Ὑμεῖς δέ, ἀγαπητοί (*Hymeis de, agapētoi*) — ‘but you, beloved’

The Greek Ὑμεῖς δέ, ἀγαπητοί (*Hymeis de, agapētoi*) places the pronoun "you" first for emphasis: "*But you*, beloved." It marks a sharp contrast with the repeated "these" (οὗτοι, *houtoi*) of the previous verses. This is the second of Jude's three addresses to the "beloved" (vv. 3, 17, 20), and it begins the turn toward the readers that will carry through to the end of the letter.

### μνήσθητε (*mnēsthēte*) — ‘remember’

The aorist imperative μνήσθητε (*mnēsthēte*), "remember," is Jude's first direct command to his readers. In the Bible, remembering is never merely recalling information. It means calling the past into the present so that it shapes action. Moses tells Israel, "you have remembered all the way which your God YHWH has caused you to go these forty years in the wilderness, in order to humble you" (Deut 8:2, LSV). Paul writes, "Remember Jesus Christ, raised out of the dead" (2 Tim 2:8, LSV). Jude began the main body of his letter with "I intend to remind you" (v. 5). Now he asks his readers to do their own remembering.

### τῶν ῥημάτων τῶν προειρημένων ὑπὸ τῶν ἀποστόλων τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ (*tōn rhēmatōn tōn proeirēmenōn hypo tōn apostolōn tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou*) — ‘the words spoken beforehand by the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ’

The readers are to remember τῶν ῥημάτων τῶν προειρημένων ὑπὸ τῶν ἀποστόλων (*tōn rhēmatōn tōn proeirēmenōn hypo tōn apostolōn*), "the words spoken beforehand by the apostles." The perfect participle προειρημένων (*proeirēmenōn*), "spoken before" or "foretold," stresses that the warning was given in advance and still stands.

Two observations follow:

1. **Jude distinguishes himself from "the apostles."** He does not say "we apostles." This fits the author's modest self-description in v. 1 and is one of the reasons for thinking that the author did not claim apostolic status. Some scholars have read the phrase as evidence that the apostles belonged to a past generation and that the letter is therefore late. The next verse suggests otherwise: the apostles had spoken "to you," to the readers themselves.
2. **The apostles' words are placed alongside the prophets.** Jude has cited Israel's history and Enoch's prophecy. Now he adds the apostles' teaching as a further authority. Second Peter's parallel makes the pairing explicit: "to be mindful of the sayings said before by the holy prophets, and of the command of us the apostles of the LORD and Savior" (2 Pet 3:2, LSV). For the early church, the prophets and the apostles together were the foundation of the faith (Eph 2:20).

### ὅτι ἔλεγον ὑμῖν (*hoti elegon hymin*) — ‘for they kept telling you’

The imperfect ἔλεγον (*elegon*), "they were saying" or "they used to say," suggests repeated teaching rather than a single statement. The warning about scoffers was a regular part of apostolic instruction. And it was said ὑμῖν (*hymin*), "to you." Jude's readers had heard it firsthand, probably from the missionaries who founded their community. That detail points to a date within the first generation or so of the church. It also makes Jude's appeal personal. He is not citing a distant authority. He is reminding them of what they themselves heard.

What did the apostles say? The New Testament preserves several such warnings. Paul told the Ephesian elders: "there will enter in, after my departing, grievous wolves to you, not sparing the flock, and there will arise men of your own selves, speaking perverse things, to draw away the disciples after them" (Acts 20:29–30, LSV). He told Timothy that "in latter times some will depart from the faith" (1 Tim 4:1, LSV) and that "in the last days there will come perilous times, for men will be lovers of themselves, lovers of money, boasters, proud, slanderous … unthankful … lovers of pleasure more than lovers of God, having a form of piety, but having denied its power" (2 Tim 3:1–5, LSV). Jesus himself had warned: "many false prophets will arise, and will lead many astray" (Matt 24:11, LSV); "behold, I have foretold you all things" (Mark 13:23, LSV). Jude's summary does not quote any of these passages directly, but it reflects the same tradition of prophetic warning.

### Ἐπ’ ἐσχάτου χρόνου ἔσονται ἐμπαῖκται (*Ep’ eschatou chronou esontai empaiktai*) — ‘in the last time there will be scoffers’

The phrase ἐπ' ἐσχάτου χρόνου (*ep' eschatou chronou*), "at the last time," reflects the conviction, shared by the whole New Testament, that the coming of Christ inaugurated the "last days." Hebrews says God has spoken "in these last days … in [His] Son" (Heb 1:2, LSV). Peter at Pentecost quoted Joel's promise of what would happen "in the last days" (Acts 2:17). John says, "Little youths, it is the last hour" (1 John 2:18, LSV). The "last time" is not a distant future. It is the period in which the church lives, between Christ's first coming and his return.

**A textual note.** The manuscripts differ in the wording of this phrase. The Byzantine text and TR read ἐν ἐσχάτῳ χρόνῳ (*en eschatō chronō*), "in the last time." NA27 read ἐπ' ἐσχάτου τοῦ χρόνου, with the article. SBLGNT and NA28 read ἐπ' ἐσχάτου χρόνου. Some manuscripts also add a second ὅτι ("that") before the quotation. The sense is unaffected.[^c10-1]

The **scoffers** (ἐμπαῖκται, *empaiktai*) are "mockers," people who ridicule. The word appears in the New Testament only here and in 2 Pet 3:3.[^c10-2] In 2 Peter the scoffers mock the promise of Christ's return: "Where is the promise of His coming? For since the fathers fell asleep, all things so remain from the beginning of the creation" (2 Pet 3:4, LSV). Jude does not specify the object of their mockery. In the context of his letter it is most naturally the moral demands of the faith and the warning of judgment. The intruders treat God's judgment as a joke, and their way of life follows from it. The "scorner" of Israel's wisdom literature is a similar figure: "O the blessedness of that one, … And has not sat in the seat of scorners" (Ps 1:1, LSV). Proverbs adds, "Do not reprove a scorner, lest he hate you" (Prov 9:8, LSV).

### κατὰ τὰς ἑαυτῶν ἐπιθυμίας πορευόμενοι τῶν ἀσεβειῶν (*kata tas heautōn epithymias poreuomenoi tōn asebeiōn*) — ‘living by their own impious desires’

The scoffers are κατὰ τὰς ἑαυτῶν ἐπιθυμίας πορευόμενοι τῶν ἀσεβειῶν (*kata tas heautōn epithymias poreuomenoi tōn asebeiōn*), literally "going according to their own desires of ungodlinesses." The genitive "of impieties" describes the kind of desires: ungodly desires. The phrase echoes v. 16 almost word for word ("walking according to their own lusts") and adds Jude's key word one last time: ἀσέβεια, "impiety." From v. 4, through the fourfold repetition in the Enoch quotation (v. 15), to here, the intruders are consistently the "ungodly."

### οὗτοί εἰσιν οἱ ἀποδιορίζοντες (*houtoi eisin hoi apodiorizontes*) — ‘these are the ones who cause divisions’

The final application begins with the familiar formula, Οὗτοί εἰσιν (*Houtoi eisin*), "These are." The first description is οἱ ἀποδιορίζοντες (*hoi apodiorizontes*). The verb, found only here in the New Testament, means "to mark off, separate, make distinctions."[^c10-3] It is built from ὅρος (*horos*, "boundary") with prefixes meaning "away" and "through." The intruders draw lines through the community.

Two senses are possible:

1. **"Those who cause divisions."** The intruders create factions, separating their followers from the rest of the community. Paul warns against "those who are causing the divisions and the stumbling-blocks, contrary to the teaching that you learned" (Rom 16:17, LSV), and lists "dissensions, sects" among the works of the flesh (Gal 5:20, LSV).
2. **"Those who make distinctions."** The intruders classify people into spiritual ranks, regarding themselves as the truly "spiritual" and others as inferior. This reading fits the next word, ψυχικοί, especially if that was a term the intruders used for others.

The two senses overlap. Drawing distinctions of spiritual rank is a way of dividing a community. The Textus Receptus adds ἑαυτούς (*heautous*), "themselves," which gives Webster's "These are they who separate themselves" (WBS), as though the intruders withdrew from the community. Most manuscripts and the critical editions lack the word.[^c10-4] Jude's picture is not of people who have left but of people who divide from within. They still sit at the love feasts (v. 12).

### ψυχικοί (*psychikoi*) — ‘unspiritual, merely natural people’

The adjective ψυχικοί (*psychikoi*) is difficult to translate. It derives from ψυχή (*psychē*), "soul, life," and describes what belongs to ordinary, natural human life as opposed to what belongs to the Spirit of God.[^c10-5] The LSV has "natural men," the WEB and Webster "sensual" (WEB, WBS), and the BSB "worldly" (BSB).

Paul uses the word in a similar contrast: "the natural man does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him … and he who is spiritual, indeed discerns all things" (1 Cor 2:14–15, LSV). He also contrasts the "natural body" with the "spiritual body" of the resurrection (1 Cor 15:44). James describes a false wisdom as "earthly, physical, demon-like" (Jas 3:15, LSV), where "physical" translates the same Greek word.

The term carries a sharp irony. If the intruders claimed spiritual superiority through their dreams and visions, and perhaps used "natural" (*psychikoi*) as a label for less enlightened believers, Jude turns their own category back on them. *They* are the merely natural ones. A century later, some Gnostic groups divided humanity into "spiritual," "natural" (*psychikoi*), and "material" classes. Jude's opponents were not second-century Gnostics, and it would be anachronistic to read that system into the letter. The Corinthian correspondence shows, however, that the contrast between "natural" and "spiritual" people was already being used in early Christian disputes about status.

### πνεῦμα μὴ ἔχοντες (*pneuma mē echontes*) — ‘not having the Spirit’

The final phrase, πνεῦμα μὴ ἔχοντες (*pneuma mē echontes*), "not having the Spirit," is the most serious charge in the letter. In the New Testament, possessing the Holy Spirit is the mark of belonging to Christ. Paul writes: "if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ—this one is not His" (Rom 8:9, LSV), and "in one Spirit we were all immersed into one body" (1 Cor 12:13, LSV).

The intruders, who appear to have claimed special spiritual experiences, are in Jude's judgment devoid of the Spirit. Their dreams (v. 8) were not the Spirit's gift. Their conduct, with its immorality, greed, grumbling, arrogance, and divisiveness, is the opposite of the fruit of the Spirit. The very next verse urges the readers to pray "in the Holy Spirit" (v. 20). Jude's contrast is complete: the intruders lack the Spirit, while the readers pray in the Spirit.

(The Greek word πνεῦμα has no article here and could in principle mean "spirit" in a general sense: "not having a spirit," that is, being merely soulish and lacking a spiritual dimension. Given the New Testament usage and the contrast with v. 20, most interpreters take it as the Holy Spirit.)

**A caution about ψυχικός.** It is tempting to build a doctrine of human nature, body, soul, and spirit, on words like ψυχικός. That is not Jude's purpose, nor Paul's in 1 Corinthians. The word describes an *orientation*: life lived on the level of ordinary human impulses, without the transforming presence of God's Spirit. It does not mean that the soul is bad or that the Spirit replaces human nature.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Apostolic teaching and its transmission.** In the first decades of the church, the apostles and their associates traveled among the churches teaching, and their teaching was passed on orally before and alongside written letters. Jude's readers had heard apostolic warnings "said to you." The phrase gives a glimpse of the early process by which communities received, remembered, and relied on the apostles' words, the same process that eventually led to the collection of the New Testament writings.

**Factions and spiritual elitism.** The churches of the first century were small communities in which differences of background, status, and spiritual experience could easily harden into factions. The Corinthian church divided around favored teachers ("I, indeed, am of Paul," 1 Cor 3:4, LSV) and around spiritual gifts (1 Cor 12–14). Jude's intruders seem to have created a similar division, with their followers presented as the truly spiritual.

**"Last days" expectation.** Jewish apocalyptic thought expected a period of intensified evil and apostasy before God's final intervention. Early Christians believed that this period had begun with Christ, and they interpreted false teaching and moral collapse within the church as signs of it (1 John 2:18–19; 2 Tim 3:1–5). Jude shares that outlook.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**2 Peter 3:1–4 (literary parallel).** Peter's version of this passage pairs the prophets and the apostles and specifies that the scoffers mock the promise of Christ's coming. The relationship between the two letters is especially close here.

**Acts 20:29–30; 1 Timothy 4:1; 2 Timothy 3:1–5; 4:3–4 (apostolic predictions).** These passages illustrate the kind of warning Jude's readers had heard. Note especially 2 Tim 4:3: people "will heap up teachers according to their own desires" (LSV).

**Matthew 24:11, 24; Mark 13:22–23 (dominical predictions).** Jesus' warnings about false prophets in the last days are the root of the apostolic tradition.

**1 John 2:18–19 (shared situation).** John's opponents "went forth out of us, but they were not of us" (1 John 2:19, LSV). Jude's intruders, by contrast, are still within the community, causing division from inside.

**1 Corinthians 2:14–15; 15:44–46; James 3:15 (vocabulary).** The contrast between the "natural" and the "spiritual."

**Romans 8:9; 1 Corinthians 12:13 (theology of the Spirit).** Having the Spirit is the mark of belonging to Christ.

**Romans 16:17; 1 Corinthians 1:10; 3:3–4; Galatians 5:20 (divisions).** Paul's warnings against division illuminate Jude's charge.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Does v. 17 show that Jude is late?** Some argue that the reference to "the apostles" as a group whose words are remembered implies a post-apostolic date. Others point out that v. 18 says the apostles spoke "to you," the readers, which places the letter within the lifetime of people who had heard the apostles. The verse is compatible with an early or a later date, but it does not require a late one.

**2. Which apostles?** Jude does not name them. The readers may have been evangelized by Paul, by Peter, or by other apostles and missionaries. Since the warning is generic, the question cannot be settled.

**3. Did the intruders use the term ψυχικοί?** It is an attractive hypothesis, especially given the parallel in 1 Corinthians, but it cannot be proven from Jude alone. What is certain is that Jude uses the term to deny the intruders' spiritual claims.

**4. Can someone in the church "not have the Spirit"?** Jude's judgment raises a pastoral and theological question. Christian traditions differ on how to understand people who are outwardly part of the church but show no evidence of the Spirit's work. Some understand them as never having been truly regenerate. Others see them as having fallen away, and others again allow for degrees of unfaithfulness. Jude does not offer a theory. His purpose is to warn his readers not to mistake the intruders' claims for spiritual reality. Notably, he still holds out hope of rescue for those influenced by them (vv. 22–23).

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verses 17–19 complete the long central section that began in v. 5. They form the fifth "text and application" unit, and the pattern of the whole section is now visible:

| Text | Application |
|---|---|
| Three examples of judgment (vv. 5–7) | "those dreaming…" (v. 8) |
| Michael and the Devil (v. 9) | "these … speak evil" (v. 10) |
| Cain, Balaam, Korah (v. 11) | "These are stains…" (vv. 12–13) |
| Enoch's prophecy (vv. 14–15) | "These are … grumblers" (v. 16) |
| The apostles' prediction (vv. 17–18) | "these are those causing divisions" (v. 19) |

The texts move from the distant past to the recent past, from the exodus to the readers' own memory of the apostles. The last application is the climax: the intruders lack the Spirit. That sets up the contrast with which the next section opens: "But you, beloved, … praying in the Holy Spirit" (v. 20).

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**The discipline of remembering.** Jude does not tell his readers to figure out new strategies. He tells them to remember. Christian communities today may find stability in recalling what they have received, in Scripture, creeds, and the teaching of faithful predecessors, rather than in perpetual novelty.

**Warnings as gifts.** The apostles warned their converts about future false teachers. Warning is an act of love. Leaders and parents who prepare others for difficulty are doing apostolic work.

**Spiritual elitism.** The intruders seem to have divided the community into the spiritual and the merely natural. Every era has its versions: groups that claim superior experience, knowledge, or holiness and look down on ordinary believers. Jude's verdict, that such dividers may themselves be "natural" and "not having the Spirit," is a warning against spiritual pride in every form, including in those who oppose the intruders.

**The evidence of the Spirit.** Jude's charge that the intruders lack the Spirit invites a question for every believer: what is the evidence of the Spirit's presence? The New Testament answers with love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control (Gal 5:22–23), unity (Eph 4:3), and prayer (Jude 20), rather than with dramatic experiences alone.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Jude tells his readers to "remember." What specific teachings or warnings do you think Christians today most need to remember?
2. The apostles warned that "scoffers" would come. What forms does scoffing at faith take today, both outside and inside the church?
3. How can a community distinguish between healthy diversity and divisive factionalism?
4. What does it mean to "have the Spirit"? How would you recognize the Spirit's presence in a person or community?
5. Spiritual elitism divided Jude's community. Where might spiritual pride show up in your own context, perhaps even among those who consider themselves defenders of the faith?
