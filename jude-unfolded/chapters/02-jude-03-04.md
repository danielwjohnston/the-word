## B. Plain-English Orientation

These two verses give the reason for the letter. Jude had been eager to write to his friends about the salvation they shared. Something forced a change of plan. People had slipped into the community unnoticed, people whose judgment was already on record. They were taking God's grace as permission for immoral living, and in doing so they were denying Jesus Christ, the only Master and Lord. So Jude writes urgently instead, appealing to his readers to contend for the faith that had been handed over to God's people once for all.

Verse 3 states the **purpose** of the letter: an appeal to contend for the faith. Verse 4 states the **occasion**, the intruders. Everything that follows explains and supports these two verses. Verses 5–19 show why the intruders deserve the judgment already "written beforehand," and verses 20–23 show what contending for the faith actually looks like.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### Ἀγαπητοί (*Agapētoi*) — ‘beloved’

The address ἀγαπητοί (*agapētoi*) echoes "having been loved" in v. 1 and the wish for "love" in v. 2. It is affectionate, and it is also strategic: Jude uses it three times (vv. 3, 17, 20), each at a point where he turns directly to the readers. Before he tells them about the intruders, he reminds them where they stand. They are beloved by God and by him.

### πᾶσαν σπουδὴν ποιούμενος γράφειν ὑμῖν περὶ τῆς κοινῆς ἡμῶν σωτηρίας (*pasan spoudēn poioumenos graphein hymin peri tēs koinēs hēmōn sōtērias*) — ‘making every effort to write to you about our common salvation’

The Greek phrase πᾶσαν σπουδὴν ποιούμενος (*pasan spoudēn poioumenos*) means "making every effort" or "being very eager." The word σπουδή (*spoudē*) means haste, diligence, or earnestness. Jude had been fully intent on writing a letter of a different kind, and perhaps had already begun one.

Its subject was to be "our common salvation" (περὶ τῆς κοινῆς ἡμῶν σωτηρίας). The adjective κοινός (*koinos*) means "shared, held in common." It is the word Luke uses of the first believers who "had all things common" (Acts 2:44, LSV), and Paul calls Titus his "true child according to a common faith" (Titus 1:4, LSV). It does not mean "ordinary." The salvation Jude wanted to celebrate belongs equally to him and his readers, and perhaps to Jewish and Gentile believers alike. It is the common possession of the whole people of God.

We do not know what that other letter would have contained, or whether Jude ever wrote it. Some readers wish he had. Yet the letter we have is not a departure from the subject of "our common salvation." It is a defense of it. The salvation he wanted to celebrate is the very thing the intruders threaten.

### ἀνάγκην ἔσχον γράψαι ὑμῖν (*anankēn eschon grapsai hymin*) — ‘I found it necessary to write to you’

The change of plan is expressed with strong language: ἀνάγκην ἔσχον (*anankēn eschon*), "I had necessity," or "I found it necessary," "I felt compelled." Paul uses the same noun when he says that "necessity is laid on me" to proclaim the good news (1 Cor 9:16, LSV). Something had happened that left Jude no choice.

There is also a quiet shift in the verb. The first "to write" (γράφειν, *graphein*) is in the present tense, describing the ongoing project he had in mind. The second (γράψαι, *grapsai*) is aorist, describing this particular letter as a single urgent act. The grammar fits the story: a long-planned general letter, interrupted by an immediate and specific one.

### παρακαλῶν (*parakalōn*) — ‘urging you’

The participle παρακαλῶν (*parakalōn*) means "urging, appealing, exhorting." It is a warm and pastoral word, from the same family as παράκλητος (*paraklētos*), the title of the Holy Spirit that the LSV renders "Comforter" (John 14:16), and that other versions render ‘Helper’ or ‘Advocate’. Jude does not command his readers like a superior issuing orders. He appeals to them as a brother. The LSV supplies "[you]" because the Greek leaves the object to be understood.

### ἐπαγωνίζεσθαι τῇ … πίστει (*epagōnizesthai tē … pistei*) — ‘to contend for the faith’

Here is the heart of the appeal. The verb ἐπαγωνίζεσθαι (*epagōnizesthai*) occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. It belongs to the family of ἀγών (*agōn*), the word for an athletic contest or struggle, from which English gets "agony." Paul uses related words for the Christian life: "I have striven the good strife" (2 Tim 4:7, LSV); "Be striving the good strife of the faith" (1 Tim 6:12, LSV). The prefix ἐπ- (*ep-*) may add a sense of "for" or "on behalf of," and the dative case of "the faith" means "contend *for* the faith." The faith is what the contest defends, not the weapon used in it.

The LSV's "fight" is vigorous and literal. Other translations have "contend earnestly" (WEB, ASV, BSB) or "earnestly contend" (WBS). The image is of exertion, of a struggle that costs effort. It is not an image of violence. The rest of the letter will show what this struggle involves. The intruders are not to be attacked; they are to be recognized for what they are and resisted. The readers' main task, spelled out in vv. 20–23, is to build themselves up in the faith, to pray, to keep themselves in God's love, and to show mercy to those who waver. Jude's contending is defined by his closing instructions, not by the heat of his warnings.

### τῇ ἅπαξ παραδοθείσῃ τοῖς ἁγίοις (*tē hapax paradotheisē tois hagiois*) — ‘handed down once for all to the saints’

Three words in this phrase carry great weight.

**"Once"** translates ἅπαξ (*hapax*), "once, once for all." The same word describes Christ's sacrifice: "now He has been revealed once, at the full end of the ages, for [the] annulling of sin through His sacrifice" (Heb 9:26, LSV); "Christ suffered once for sin—righteous for unrighteous—that He might lead us to God" (1 Pet 3:18, LSV). The faith was handed over on a particular occasion, completely. It does not need supplementing by later revelations, and that is exactly the claim the intruders, with their ‘dreams’ (v. 8), appear to have made.

**"Delivered"** translates παραδοθείσῃ (*paradotheisē*), from παραδίδωμι (*paradidōmi*), "to hand over, pass on." This is the technical language of tradition. Paul uses it for the most important things he taught: "For I delivered to you as most important what I also received: that Christ died for our sins according to the Writings" (1 Cor 15:3, LSV), and "For I received from the LORD that which I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus in the night in which He was delivered up, took bread" (1 Cor 11:23, LSV). Jewish teachers used the same pair of ideas, receiving and handing on, for the transmission of sacred teaching. The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, opens its tractate *Avot* with a chain of tradition from Moses through Joshua and the elders. Christianity understood itself from the beginning as a message received and passed on through faithful witnesses.

The aorist passive participle presents the delivering as a completed act, with God as the implied giver. The faith did not originate with the church; it was given to the church.

**"The holy ones"** translates τοῖς ἁγίοις (*tois hagiois*), "the saints." The LSV's "holy ones" helps modern readers avoid thinking of a special class of exceptionally virtuous Christians. In the Bible "the holy ones" are God's people as a whole, set apart for him. Daniel foresees that "the saints of the Most High receive the kingdom" (Dan 7:18, LSV), where the Aramaic word behind "saints" means "holy ones." The faith was entrusted to the whole community, not to a spiritual elite. That matters, because the intruders, as v. 19 will suggest, seem to have divided the community into ranks.

### παρεισέδυσαν γάρ τινες ἄνθρωποι (*pareisedysan gar tines anthrōpoi*) — ‘for certain people have slipped in’

Verse 4 explains why the appeal was necessary. The verb παρεισέδυσαν (*pareisedysan*) is another word found only here in the New Testament. It means "to slip in secretly, steal in."[^c2-1] It pictures someone entering through a side door, unnoticed. Paul uses related language of the "false brothers brought in unaware, who came in secretly to spy out our liberty" (Gal 2:4, LSV), and 2 Peter of false teachers "who will stealthily bring in destructive sects" (2 Pet 2:1, LSV).

The point is not necessarily that the intruders hid their identity. It is that their arrival was not recognized for what it was. They came as fellow believers, perhaps as traveling teachers welcomed according to the customs of Christian hospitality, and only later did their influence become clear. The phrase "certain men" (τινες ἄνθρωποι, *tines anthrōpoi*) is deliberately vague and somewhat dismissive. Jude never names them.

### οἱ πάλαι προγεγραμμένοι εἰς τοῦτο τὸ κρίμα (*hoi palai progegrammenoi eis touto to krima*) — ‘written up long ago for this judgment’

This is one of the most debated phrases in the letter. The Greek οἱ πάλαι προγεγραμμένοι εἰς τοῦτο τὸ κρίμα (*hoi palai progegrammenoi eis touto to krima*) means literally "those long ago written-beforehand for this judgment."

- **προγράφω** (*prographō*) means "to write before" or "to write publicly."[^c2-2] Paul uses it for the scriptures: "as many things as were written before, for our instruction were written before" (Rom 15:4, LSV).
- **πάλαι** (*palai*) means "long ago, of old."[^c2-3]
- **κρίμα** (*krima*) means "judgment" or "condemnation," the verdict and its sentence.

What does it mean that these people were "written beforehand" for judgment? Three main interpretations have been offered.

1. **Written about in earlier prophecies.** The intruders' judgment was described in advance in the scriptures and prophecies Jude is about to cite: the wilderness generation, the angels, Sodom, Cain, Balaam, Korah, and especially Enoch's prophecy against the "impious." "This judgment" points forward to the judgments Jude will describe. On this reading, Jude is saying: *people like these were written about long ago, and what was written about them is about to be read out.* The word "long ago" (πάλαι) fits a prophecy as ancient as Enoch's. This is the interpretation most modern commentators favor, and the structure of the letter supports it.
2. **Written in heavenly books.** Jewish writings of the period speak of heavenly records in which deeds and destinies are inscribed (for example, the "heavenly tablets" of 1 Enoch 81 and 93:2). Jude might mean that the intruders' condemnation was already recorded in heaven.
3. **Predestined to condemnation.** Some theological traditions have read the phrase as a statement of individual reprobation, a divine decree that these persons would be condemned. Clement of Alexandria already addressed this, insisting that the intruders were ordained to judgment "not that they might become impious, but that, being now impious, they were ordained to judgment."[^c2-4]

The first interpretation best fits the flow of the letter, which immediately sets out "written" examples of judgment. Nineteenth-century commentators reached the same conclusion. The *Pulpit Commentary* judged that "the Old Testament prophecies, therefore, are probably those referred to," noting "that mention is made by-and-by of Enoch as one of the prophets of old." Alfred Plummer insisted that the phrase "gives no support to extreme predestinarian theories," because "'of old' (παλαι) cannot refer to the eternal counsel and decree of Almighty God, but to something in human history."[^c2-4a] This book adopts it while acknowledging the others. The phrase is a statement about the certainty of judgment on a certain kind of behavior, not a statement that particular persons were created for damnation. It is also, in context, a word of reassurance. The intruders' arrival had not taken God by surprise.

### ἀσεβεῖς (*asebeis*) — ‘impious’

The single word ἀσεβεῖς (*asebeis*), "ungodly, impious," stands alone in the Greek like a verdict. It is Jude's key term for the intruders. The noun, verb, and adjective of this word family occur six times in the letter, four of them in the quotation from Enoch: "to convict all their impious ones, concerning all their works of impiety that they did impiously, and concerning all the harsh [words] that impious sinners spoke" (v. 15). By calling the intruders "impious" here, Jude labels them with the very word of the prophecy he will quote. The *asebeia* family describes a failure of reverence toward God, a life lived as though God were not to be feared.

### τὴν τοῦ θεοῦ ἡμῶν χάριτα μετατιθέντες εἰς ἀσέλγειαν (*tēn tou theou hēmōn charita metatithentes eis aselgeian*) — ‘turning the grace of our God into licentiousness’

This phrase names the intruders' central error. The participle μετατιθέντες (*metatithentes*), from μετατίθημι (*metatithēmi*), means "to transfer, change," and here "to make one thing a pretext for another."[^c2-5] The intruders take "the grace of our God" and turn it into **licentiousness**, ἀσέλγεια (*aselgeia*), a word for unrestrained, shameless behavior, often sexual.[^c2-6] It appears in the New Testament's vice lists: "adultery, whoredom, uncleanness, licentiousness" (Gal 5:19, LSV).

The error is ancient and recurring. Paul had to answer it: "Will we continue in sin that grace may abound? … Let it not be!" (Rom 6:1–2, LSV); "you were called to freedom, brothers, only the freedom [is] not for an occasion to the flesh" (Gal 5:13, LSV). Peter warns believers not to have "freedom as the cloak of evil" (1 Pet 2:16, LSV). Titus offers the positive counterpart: "the saving grace of God has appeared to all men, teaching us, that denying the impiety and the worldly desires, we may live soberly, and righteously, and piously in the present age" (Titus 2:11–12, LSV). In Titus, grace teaches the denial of impiety. In Jude, the impious deny the Master and use grace as license.

The intruders probably did not see themselves as opponents of grace. They likely saw themselves as its most consistent champions: if God forgives freely, why should conduct matter? Jude's verdict is that such a view does not exalt grace; it perverts it.

### καὶ τὸν μόνον δεσπότην καὶ κύριον ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦν Χριστὸν ἀρνούμενοι (*kai ton monon despotēn kai kyrion hēmōn Iēsoun Christon arnoumenoi*) — ‘and denying our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ’

The participle ἀρνούμενοι (*arnoumenoi*), "denying," does not necessarily mean the intruders publicly renounced Christ in words. The New Testament often speaks of denial by conduct: "They profess to know God, but they deny [Him] by their works" (Titus 1:16, LSV). Since the intruders were inside the church, it is likely that they confessed Jesus with their lips while denying his lordship with their lives. To use grace as license is to refuse the authority of the one who gave it.

The titles are striking. **"Master"** translates δεσπότην (*despotēn*), the accusative of δεσπότης (*despotēs*), the word for an owner of slaves and a ruler with absolute authority.[^c2-7] In the Septuagint and elsewhere in the New Testament it is used of God ("LORD, You [are] God, who made the heaven, and the earth," Acts 4:24, LSV; cf. Luke 2:29). Second Peter uses it of Christ, "denying the Master who bought them" (2 Pet 2:1, LSV). **"Only"** (μόνος, *monos*) is the language of Jewish monotheism. In v. 25, Jude will praise "the only … God our Savior." To call Jesus "our *only* Master and Lord" is to apply to him language that belongs to the God of Israel. Jude, who calls himself Jesus' *doulos* in v. 1, now calls him the *despotēs* of all believers.

The LSV's dash ("our only Master and Lord—Jesus Christ") makes clear that both titles refer to Jesus. In Greek this is signaled by a single article (τόν, *ton*) governing both nouns, a construction in which two singular personal nouns joined by "and" under one article normally refer to the same person.

**A textual note.** The Byzantine tradition and the Textus Receptus add θεόν (*theon*), "God": "the only Master God and our Lord Jesus Christ." Webster's revision, following the older English Bibles, translated this as two persons: "denying the only Lord God, and our Lord Jesus Christ" (WBS). The WEB, translating the same Majority Text, renders it as one person: "denying our only Master, God, and Lord, Jesus Christ." The critical editions omit θεόν, and the LSV follows them. On either reading, Jude's Christology is high.

**Two cautions.**

1. *Etymology and ἐπαγωνίζομαι.* Because the word is related to *agōn*, sermons sometimes describe Jude's readers as athletes "agonizing" in the arena. The word does belong to the language of contest, and the image is fair as far as it goes. But the meaning of a rare compound is best derived from its context, not from a picture assembled from its parts. Here the context says only that the readers should exert themselves on behalf of the faith. Jude defines how in vv. 20–23.
2. *"The faith" vs. "faith."* With the article and with "once delivered," *pistis* here means the body of belief, not personal trust. That is not a sign of a "later" Christianity: Paul already speaks this way in Gal 1:23. Equally, Jude does not imagine faith as a mere list of propositions. The faith is "most holy" (v. 20), something believers build themselves up on.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Traveling teachers and Christian hospitality.** The early churches depended on traveling apostles, prophets, and teachers, and they extended them hospitality. That openness made communities vulnerable. The *Didache*, an early Christian manual of church practice usually dated between the late first and early second century, gives practical tests for visitors. "Whosoever … comes and teaches you all these things that have been said before, receive him. But if the teacher himself turn and teach another doctrine to the destruction of this, hear him not." An apostle should stay no more than a day or two, and "if he ask money, he is a false prophet." "Not every one that speaks in the Spirit is a prophet; but only if he hold the ways of the Lord. Therefore from their ways shall the false prophet and the prophet be known."[^c2-8] These instructions match Jude's concerns closely: teaching measured against what was handed down, character as the test of a claimed spiritual gift, and suspicion of teachers who seek profit (vv. 11, 16). The *Didache* was not written to Jude's readers, and the two documents are independent. It does show that the problem Jude faced was a recognized one in the early churches.

**Contests and the *agōn*.** Athletic games were a major feature of Greco-Roman civic life, and the language of contest (*agōn*, "struggle, competition") was widely used metaphorically by philosophers and moralists for the moral life. Hellenistic Jewish writers also used it of faithfulness under persecution. Jude's word draws on this widely understood world of strenuous, disciplined effort.

**Tradition in Judaism and early Christianity.** Jewish teachers carefully transmitted the interpretation of the Law from teacher to student. Early Christians, many of them Jews, used the same vocabulary of "receiving" and "handing on" for the gospel and for the teachings of Jesus. When Jude speaks of "the faith once delivered," he speaks the language of a community conscious of being entrusted with something it did not invent.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Contending for the faith (Phil 1:27; 1 Tim 6:12; 2 Tim 4:7).** Paul urges the Philippians to stand "with one soul, striving together for the faith of the good news" (Phil 1:27, LSV), using a related compound (συναθλέω, "strive together"). Timothy is told, "Be striving the good strife of the faith" (1 Tim 6:12, LSV), and Paul says of himself, "I have striven the good strife, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith" (2 Tim 4:7, LSV). These are shared themes and vocabulary rather than quotations. Together they show that "contending for the faith" was part of the common moral language of early Christianity.

**Guarding a deposit (1 Tim 6:20; 2 Tim 1:14).** Paul tells Timothy, "guard the thing entrusted" (1 Tim 6:20, LSV) and "guard the good thing committed through the Holy Spirit that is dwelling in us" (2 Tim 1:14, LSV). The image of a deposit held in trust parallels Jude's faith "once delivered."

**Received and delivered (1 Cor 11:23; 15:1–3).** The closest verbal parallels are Paul's statements of tradition. Note especially 1 Cor 15:1–2: "the good news that I proclaimed to you, which you also received, in which you also have stood, through which you are also being saved" (LSV). The gospel is received, stood in, and saving, like Jude's "common salvation."

**Infiltrators (Gal 2:4; 2 Pet 2:1).** Paul's "false brothers brought in unaware, who came in secretly" (Gal 2:4, LSV) offers the nearest Pauline parallel to Jude's "came in … stealthily." The parallel with 2 Pet 2:1 ("stealthily bring in destructive sects, even denying the Master who bought them") is one of the strongest verbal links between Jude and 2 Peter (see Appendix C).

**Grace and license (Rom 6:1–2, 15; Gal 5:13; 1 Pet 2:16; Titus 2:11–12).** Discussed above. These passages show that the abuse of grace was a danger the apostles recognized and resisted in several places.

**Denial by conduct (Titus 1:16; Matt 10:33; 2 Tim 2:12).** Jesus warned, "whoever will deny Me before men, I also will deny him before My Father who is in the heavens" (Matt 10:33, LSV). Jude's charge stands in this line.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Did Jude write the other letter?** Some have suggested that the planned letter about "our common salvation" might survive elsewhere, for example as 2 Peter. There is no evidence for this, and most scholars regard the question as unanswerable.

**2. Is "the faith once delivered" a sign of late authorship?** As noted in the Historical Introduction, some scholars see in "the faith" a fixed body of doctrine typical of later "early catholic" Christianity. Others point out that Paul spoke of "the faith" as content (Gal 1:23) and of delivering traditions (1 Cor 11:2, 23; 15:3) in the 50s. The phrase is compatible with an early date.

**3. What exactly is "the faith"?** Jude does not define it. From the letter itself we can infer that it includes the lordship of Jesus Christ (v. 4), the reality of judgment (vv. 5–15), the grace of God that does not license immorality (v. 4), the apostles' teaching (v. 17), the work of the Holy Spirit (vv. 19–20), the love of God and the mercy of Christ (v. 21), and the hope of eternal life (v. 21). That is, it includes both belief and the shape of life that belief requires. Christian traditions differ on where this "faith once delivered" is now authoritatively found. Many Protestants locate it in Scripture alone. Catholic and Orthodox Christians locate it in Scripture together with the church's living tradition. All agree that it was given, not invented.

**4. What does "written beforehand" imply about predestination?** See the discussion in section C above. The phrase has been read in support of divine reprobation, but the context points most naturally to *recorded prophecies of judgment*, which Jude proceeds to cite.

**5. Does "only Master" imply that Jesus is God?** Jude does not use the word "God" of Jesus (unless the Byzantine θεόν is original). He does apply to Jesus titles and monotheistic language ("only Master," "Lord") that Jewish usage reserved for God. If the reading "Jesus" in v. 5 is original (see Chapter 3), Jude also attributes to Jesus the exodus deliverance, the defining saving act of Israel's God. Many scholars see Jude as evidence of a very early and very high Christology emerging within Jewish Christianity.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verses 3 and 4 are the hinge on which the letter turns. Verse 3 states the **appeal**: contend for the faith. Verse 4 states the **reason**: intruders have come in. The rest of the letter unfolds both:

- Verses 5–19 expand v. 4. They show from scripture, tradition, and prophecy that the intruders' judgment was indeed "written beforehand," and they describe the intruders' "impious" character in detail.
- Verses 20–23 expand v. 3. They show what "contending for the faith" means in practice: building up, praying, keeping, waiting, and showing mercy.

Jude's frequent use of "impious" (vv. 4, 15, 18) and the word "faith" at both ends of this structure (vv. 3, 20) mark these connections.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*These are suggested applications, distinct from the text's original meaning.*

**Contending without contention.** Jude's call to "fight for the faith" has often been used to justify combativeness, sometimes over matters far removed from the core of the gospel. Jude's own letter shows a different picture. His concern was the lordship of Christ and the moral meaning of grace, not secondary disputes. His instructions for contending (vv. 20–23) are about spiritual formation and mercy, not argument and exclusion. Christians today might ask whether their "contending" looks like Jude's: urgent about what matters most, and practiced through prayer, love, and rescue.

**The faith as gift and trust.** "Once delivered to the holy ones" means the faith belongs to the whole people of God and was entrusted, not invented. For modern believers this may be both humbling and freeing. They do not need to create a faith that fits their moment, and they are responsible for passing on what they received.

**Grace that teaches.** Every generation meets the temptation to treat grace as permission. Jude may help believers see that grace and holiness are not rivals. The grace of God that forgives is the same grace that "teaches" a transformed life (Titus 2:11–12).

**Recognizing infiltration without paranoia.** Jude's intruders "came in … stealthily," and their influence was recognized only over time. Churches today might learn from the early Christian practice of testing teaching by its fidelity to the received faith and testing teachers by their conduct, as the *Didache* advised. They should do this without turning community life into suspicion of every newcomer. Jude's own ending (vv. 22–23) shows that vigilance and mercy belong together.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Jude intended to write about "our common salvation" but felt compelled to write a warning instead. Have you experienced a situation in which encouragement had to give way to correction? How did you decide?
2. What do you think belongs to "the faith once delivered to the holy ones," and what is secondary? How might different Christian traditions answer this question?
3. The intruders likely saw themselves as champions of grace. How can a community distinguish between a genuine emphasis on grace and the "perverting" of grace that Jude describes?
4. Jude says the intruders denied Jesus as Master and Lord, probably by their conduct rather than their words. In what ways can people confess Jesus with their lips while denying him with their lives?
5. If contending for the faith is defined by vv. 20–23, what would it look like for you or your community to "fight for the faith" this week?
