# Research Method and Interpretive Principles {.unnumbered}

A commentary is only as trustworthy as the way it was made. This chapter sets out how this book was researched, what kinds of sources stand behind its claims, and the principles that guided interpretation. Readers who want to get straight to the text can skip ahead and come back later. Readers who want to know how far to trust what follows should read on.

## Sources and how they were used

**The biblical text.** The English text is the Literal Standard Version (LSV), taken from the eBible.org edition and checked word for word against a second rendering of the same edition. The Greek text is the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes, with its apparatus of variant readings. An updated form of that apparatus records where the Nestle–Aland 28th edition (NA28), the most widely used critical edition today, differs. For comparison the book uses the Robinson–Pierpont edition of the Byzantine text (the form of the Greek found in the majority of later manuscripts), the Textus Receptus (the printed Greek text behind the King James Version), and four public-domain English translations: the WEB, ASV, BSB, and KJV.

**Ancient sources.** When Jude alludes to a Jewish or early Christian tradition, the book goes back to the ancient text itself wherever an openly available edition exists: 1 Enoch in R. H. Charles's translation, the Targums in J. W. Etheridge's translation, Josephus in William Whiston's, the Septuagint in Brenton's edition, and the church fathers in the standard nineteenth-century translations. Each passage relied on was read directly. The notes give book, chapter, and section so that readers can check them.

**Lexical sources.** Greek word meanings are drawn mainly from G. Abbott-Smith's *Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament* (1922). It is a public-domain lexicon that still repays use, and it summarizes the classical, Septuagint, and papyrus evidence for each New Testament word. Word counts and grammatical data were computed directly from the MorphGNT edition of the SBLGNT, so a statement such as "this word occurs nowhere else in the New Testament" rests on a count of the actual text.

**Modern scholarship.** The book draws on the main modern commentaries on Jude, above all Richard Bauckham's, which shaped the modern study of the letter, together with studies of its text, rhetoric, and background. These works could not all be re-consulted page by page during the research for this edition. The book therefore attributes to them only positions they are well known for, cites them by the verse under discussion rather than by page, and identifies them in the Bibliography as works to check before any claim is built on them. This is a deliberate choice: it is better to say less, and say it accurately, than to decorate a sentence with a citation that cannot be traced.

The full **source ledger**, which records what was verified, how, and what remains uncertain, is part of the project files that accompany the manuscript.

## Principles of interpretation

**1. The text has the first word.** Fidelity to Scripture comes before defending any preferred interpretation. Where a popular reading does not fit Jude's words, the book says so, even when that reading is cherished.

**2. Meaning before application.** Each chapter separates what the text meant in its first setting from how it may be applied now. The two are related, but they are not the same thing, and confusing them is the source of much misuse of Jude.

**3. Kinds of claims are labeled.** The book tries to make clear whether a statement is a textual fact ("the Greek word occurs only here"), historical evidence ("Pliny reports…"), scholarly consensus, a contested interpretation, a theological tradition, a plausible inference, or a suggested application. Historical reconstructions, however likely, are presented as reconstructions.

**4. Words mean what they mean in use.** A Greek word's meaning is determined by how it was actually used in its period, not by its etymology or by adding up its parts. The book points out etymologies when they are interesting and warns when they have been overworked. For example, ἐκκλησία does not mean "the called-out ones" just because it contains the parts "out" and "call." Jude's word σπιλάδες, discussed in Chapter 6, is a good example of a word whose sense must be settled by usage.

**5. Traditions outside the Bible are taken seriously and handled carefully.** Jude quotes 1 Enoch and alludes to a tradition about Michael and Moses that appears nowhere in the Old Testament. The book does not explain these away. It shows where they come from, how Jude uses them, and how the church has argued about what that use implies. Quoting a text is not the same as endorsing everything in it, and the book is careful not to claim more than Jude does.

**6. Disagreements are presented fairly.** When responsible scholars disagree, the book sets out the main options, the evidence for each, and their strengths and weaknesses. It sometimes states a preference and gives reasons, but it tries to give readers what they need to decide differently.

**7. No denominational polemics.** Christians read Jude within many traditions. Where those traditions differ in ways that affect the meaning, for example on the "brothers" of Jesus or on how believers are "kept," the book describes the differences without taking sides in old quarrels.

**8. The letter stays whole.** Word studies serve the reading of the letter and do not replace it. Each chapter ends by placing its passage in the flow of Jude's argument, and the overview chapter maps the whole.

## About the Greek

Greek words appear in Greek script with a transliteration in italics, for example ἀγάπη (*agapē*). The transliteration follows a simple scholarly system: η = *ē*, ω = *ō*, υ = *y* (*u* in diphthongs), χ = *ch*, θ = *th*, φ = *ph*, ψ = *ps*, and rough breathing = *h*. Readers who do not read Greek can ignore the Greek script and follow the transliteration and explanation. Grammatical terms are explained when they first appear and collected in the Glossary.

A few grammatical ideas recur often enough to introduce here:

- **Tense and aspect.** Greek verb forms say less about *when* an action happens than English tenses do and more about *how* the action is viewed. The **perfect** presents a completed action with continuing results ("having been loved," and still loved). The **aorist** presents an action as a whole, without comment on its duration. The **present** presents an action as ongoing or in progress.
- **Participles** are verbal adjectives ("having been called," "praying"). Jude uses them heavily, and much of his meaning rides on them.
- **The optative** is a verb form used to express wishes ("may mercy … be multiplied"). It is rare in the New Testament, and Jude uses it twice.
- **Cases** (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative) mark a noun's role in the sentence. The **dative** in particular can mean "to," "for," "by," or "in," and Jude's first verse depends on which is chosen.

## About the variant readings

The New Testament survives in thousands of handwritten manuscripts, and they differ in small and sometimes significant ways. Jude has more than its share of interesting variants. One of them changes who is said to have led Israel out of Egypt, and another changes the shape of the letter's final instructions. The book explains the significant variants where they arise and gathers them in Appendix A. Readers should know that none of these variants touches the substance of the Christian faith. Some do, however, affect how particular verses are read, and they deserve honest explanation.

## About application

The "Contemporary Understanding" sections suggest ways Jude's message may inform belief, worship, fellowship, ethics, and spiritual practice today. They are offered as possibilities to weigh, not as divine instructions. Jude was written against particular people doing particular things, and it is easy to aim its sharpest words at whoever we happen to dislike. The book tries to resist that temptation and to let Jude question its readers, this author included, before it is turned on anyone else.
