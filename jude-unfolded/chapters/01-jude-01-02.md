# Chapter 1. Called, Loved, and Kept: Identity and Greeting (Jude 1–2)

## A. The Text

> **1** Judas, a servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James, to those called, having been loved in God [the] Father, and having been kept in Jesus Christ, **2** mercy and peace and love be multiplied to you!
>
> — Jude 1–2, LSV

## B. Plain-English Orientation

Ancient letters opened with a standard formula: *From A, to B, greetings.* Jude follows the pattern, but he fills each part with meaning. He introduces himself by two relationships, servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James. He describes his readers by three things God has done for them: God has called them, loved them, and kept them. Then he wishes them three gifts in abundance: mercy, peace, and love.

These two verses are easy to skim on the way to the "real" content of the letter. They are, however, the foundation on which everything else stands. Before Jude says a word about danger, he tells his readers who they are and whose they are. The letter will speak of people who "did not keep" their place and are "kept" for judgment, and it will urge the readers to "keep" themselves in the love of God. All of that is anchored here, in a description of believers as people who have *already* been loved and kept.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### "Judas"

The author's name is the Greek form of *Judah*, the patriarch whose name became the name of a tribe, a kingdom, and finally the Jewish people as a whole. For a Jewish family in Galilee, naming a son Judah was a patriotic and pious act. The name also recalled Judas Maccabeus, the hero of the revolt against Antiochus IV in the second century BC. Among Jews of the first century it was one of the most common names.

English Bibles traditionally write "Jude" for the author of this letter and "Judas" for others, partly so that readers do not confuse him with Judas Iscariot. The LSV, true to its literal approach, keeps "Judas." The Greek makes no distinction. As the Historical Introduction explains, the author is almost certainly the Judas named among the brothers of Jesus in Mark 6:3.

### "a servant of Jesus Christ"

The Greek word is δοῦλος (*doulos*), the ordinary word for a slave. The LSV's "servant" is a long-standing English convention, but it softens the word. In the Roman world a *doulos* was not an employee who could resign. A *doulos* was a person owned by another, whose time, labor, and body belonged to a master.

Two backgrounds give the phrase its force.

The first is the Old Testament phrase "servant of the LORD" (in Hebrew *ʿebed YHWH*). It was a title of honor, given to Moses ("My servant Moses is dead," Josh 1:2, LSV), to David ("Go, and you have said to My servant, to David," 2 Sam 7:5, LSV), and to the prophets. To be someone's servant was to be identified with that person's authority and to act on his behalf. The Septuagint uses δοῦλος almost always to translate the Hebrew *ʿebed*.[^c1-1]

The second is Christian usage. Paul begins Romans with "Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ" (Rom 1:1, LSV), and James begins his letter "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ" (Jas 1:1, LSV). Paul could also describe Christ himself as one who "emptied Himself, having taken the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7, LSV), using the same word, *doulos*.

For Jude the title says something personal. He grew up in the same household as Jesus. He might have presented himself as an equal, or at least as a relative with special standing. Instead he calls himself Jesus' slave. The family tie remains, but it has been taken up into a new relationship of total belonging, the same belonging that will mark all his readers. A few lines later he will call Jesus "our only Master" (v. 4), and the Greek word there, δεσπότης (*despotēs*), is the normal word for a slave's owner. Jude is consistent: if Jesus is the *despotēs*, Jude is his *doulos*.

### "and brother of James"

The second relationship identifies Jude for his readers by naming his famous brother, James, the leader of the Jerusalem church (Gal 1:19; 2:9; Acts 15:13). As the Historical Introduction explains, early readers like Clement of Alexandria saw humility in Jude's choice to name James rather than Jesus.[^c1-2] The phrase also establishes credentials. Jude speaks as a member of a family whose authority the churches recognized, without claiming to be an apostle.

### "to those called"

In Greek the description of the readers is built so that the key word comes last: *to-the-in-God-the-Father-loved-and-for-Jesus-Christ-kept called-ones*. The final word, κλητοῖς (*klētois*), "called," is the noun the other phrases describe. It is the readers' basic identity: they are people God has called.

"Calling" in the Bible is not a general invitation that people may take or leave. It is God's effective summons that brings a people into being. God called Abraham out of Ur, and he called Israel out of Egypt. In Isaiah, God says to his servant, "I, YHWH, called you in righteousness, / And I lay hold on your hand, and keep you" (Isa 42:6, LSV). There *calling* and *keeping* stand side by side, as they do in Jude.

The New Testament applies this language to the church. Paul writes to "the called of Jesus Christ … called holy ones" (Rom 1:6–7, LSV), and to "those called—both Jews and Greeks" (1 Cor 1:24, LSV). Revelation pictures the Lamb's companions as those who "are called, and chosen, and steadfast" (Rev 17:14, LSV). By placing "called" in the climactic position, Jude says that his readers' existence as a community begins with God's initiative, not their own.

### "having been loved in God [the] Father"

The Greek participle ἠγαπημένοις (*ēgapēmenois*) is in the **perfect** tense, which presents a past action with continuing results. Its sense is "having been loved and remaining loved." The love Jude has in mind is not a passing feeling but a settled state.

The phrase "in God [the] Father" is unusual. Most often the New Testament speaks of being loved *by* God. Some interpreters take the Greek preposition ἐν (*en*) here in an instrumental sense, "loved *by* God the Father." Others keep the local sense, "loved *in* God," meaning that believers are held within the sphere of God's own love. The LSV keeps the literal "in" and lets the reader feel its strangeness. Either way, the source of the love is God the Father, and it is the same love in which the readers are later told to "keep" themselves (v. 21).

The Old Testament roots of this idea are deep. Moses told Israel that God did not choose them for their size or merit, "but because of YHWH's loving you, and because of His keeping the oath" (Deut 7:8, LSV). Love and keeping stand together there too.

**A variant reading.** Many later Greek manuscripts read ἡγιασμένοις (*hēgiasmenois*), "sanctified," in place of ἠγαπημένοις, "loved." Webster's 1833 revision of the traditional English Bible follows this reading ("to them that are sanctified by God the Father"), as does the WEB, which translates the Majority Text ("sanctified by God the Father"). The critical editions (Westcott–Hort, Tregelles, NA28, and SBLGNT) all read "loved," and the LSV, though it generally follows the Majority Text, does so here too. The likely explanation is that a scribe found "loved in God" awkward and replaced it with the more familiar "sanctified in," which echoes 1 Cor 1:2 ("sanctified in Christ Jesus"). This variant also illustrates a basic principle of textual criticism. According to the manuscript tallies in one modern edition, "sanctified" appears in about 90 percent of surviving manuscripts and "loved" in about 9 percent.[^c1-3] Most of those manuscripts, however, are late copies of a single textual tradition. The editors of the critical texts judged that the reading of the earliest witnesses, and the harder reading that best explains its rival, is more likely original. Counting manuscripts is not the same as weighing them.

### "and having been kept in Jesus Christ"

Again the participle is perfect: τετηρημένοις (*tetērēmenois*), "having been kept and remaining kept." The verb τηρέω (*tēreō*) means "to watch over, guard, keep, preserve."[^c1-4] It will become the central word of the letter.

The Greek has no preposition here. It reads simply "to-Jesus-Christ kept," with "Jesus Christ" in the dative case. Because the Greek dative can express several relationships, three readings are possible:

1. **"kept *for* Jesus Christ"** (WEB, ASV, and most modern translations). Believers are kept safe *for* Christ, held in trust until his return. This fits Paul's prayer that believers' "whole spirit and soul and body be preserved, unblameably at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thess 5:23, LSV), and the letter's own climax, where God sets believers before his glory (v. 24).
2. **"kept *by* Jesus Christ."** Christ himself is the keeper. Compare Jesus' prayer, "when I was with them in the world, I was keeping them in Your Name" (John 17:12, LSV).
3. **"kept *in* Jesus Christ"** (LSV). The preposition "in" from the previous phrase is understood again, so that believers are kept within their union with Christ.

The first reading is the most natural sense of a dative of this kind, and it fits the forward-looking shape of the letter, which ends with believers presented before God's glory. The other readings are possible, and all three are true to the New Testament's wider teaching. Jude's emphasis is clear in any case: the readers' safety is something God has done and is doing.

The word will return with sharp irony. In v. 6, angels who "did not keep" (μὴ τηρήσαντας, *mē tērēsantas*) their position are "kept" (τετήρηκεν, *tetērēken*) in bonds for judgment. In v. 13, the gloom of darkness "has been kept" (τετήρηται, *tetērētai*) for the wandering stars. In v. 21, the readers are told, "keep yourselves" (τηρήσατε, *tērēsate*). From the opening line, Jude is preparing a contrast between two kinds of keeping: keeping for glory and keeping for judgment.

### "mercy and peace and love"

Most New Testament letters greet their readers with "grace and peace." The pastoral letters to Timothy and 2 John have "grace, mercy, peace." Only Jude has **mercy, peace, and love**.

- **Mercy** (ἔλεος, *eleos*) is God's compassion toward the needy and undeserving. In the Septuagint it most often translates the Hebrew *ḥesed*, God's steadfast covenant love and loyalty.[^c1-5] Jude will mention mercy three more times: the readers are to wait for "the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v. 21), and they are to "show mercy" to others (vv. 22–23). The letter begins with mercy given and ends with mercy passed on.
- **Peace** (εἰρήνη, *eirēnē*) carries the full sense of the Hebrew *shalom*: wholeness, safety, right relationship with God and others. In the Septuagint, *eirēnē* chiefly translates *shalom*.[^c1-6]
- **Love** (ἀγάπη, *agapē*) picks up "having been loved" from v. 1. It will appear again in a surprising place, the community's "love-feasts" (v. 12), and once more in the command "keep yourselves in the love of God" (v. 21).

The three gifts are not three separate packages. Together they describe the life of the called community: God's mercy received, God's peace enjoyed, God's love shared.

### "be multiplied to you!"

The verb πληθυνθείη (*plēthyntheiē*) is an **optative**, the Greek mood used for wishes and prayers. It is rare in the New Testament, which has only about sixty-eight optative forms in all.[^c1-7] Jude uses it twice: here and in Michael's "The LORD rebuke you!" (v. 9).

The wish formula has a history. In the Greek translation of Daniel attributed to Theodotion, the royal letters of Nebuchadnezzar and Darius both open with εἰρήνη ὑμῖν πληθυνθείη, ‘peace be multiplied to you’ (Dan 4:1; 6:26).[^c1-8] First and Second Peter use the same formula: "Grace and peace be multiplied to you!" (1 Pet 1:2, LSV; cf. 2 Pet 1:2). Jude uses a familiar form of greeting, apparently at home in Jewish letters, and fills it with his own triad.

The point of "multiplied" is abundance. Jude does not wish his readers a modest share of mercy, peace, and love; he prays that these will grow and overflow. Given the troubles the letter describes, that prayer is not decoration but provision. A community facing division needs more love, not less, and mercy that grows enough to reach those who are wavering (vv. 22–23).

**A caution about *agapē*.** Popular teaching often claims that *agapē* and the verb *agapaō* name a uniquely divine, self-giving love, distinct from human affection. The New Testament's own usage does not support so sharp a distinction. The same verb describes Demas, who deserted Paul, "having loved the present age" (2 Tim 4:10, LSV), and those of whom John says "men loved the darkness rather than the light" (John 3:19, LSV). Abbott-Smith's lexicon notes that the traditional contrast between *agapaō* and *phileō* rests partly on a disputed etymology.[^c1-9] What gives Christian *agapē* its distinctive character is not the word itself but its object and source: the love of God shown in Christ. Jude's usage fits this. The readers are loved *by God*, and they are to keep themselves in *God's* love.

**A grammatical note on word order.** Greek allows an author to wrap a noun in its descriptions. Jude's "to the … called" encloses "in God the Father loved and for Jesus Christ kept." The arrangement places God the Father and Jesus Christ in parallel as the sources of the readers' security, and it saves the noun of identity for last.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Letter-writing conventions.** Thousands of letters survive from the Greco-Roman world, many of them on papyrus from Egypt. The typical opening was brief: "A to B, greetings (χαίρειν, *chairein*)." Jewish letters often used "peace" instead. Christian letter-writers took over the basic form and transformed it, replacing *chairein* with *charis* ("grace") or with theological triads like Jude's, and expanding the descriptions of sender and recipients to make theological claims. Jude's opening is shorter than Paul's, but it is just as deliberate.

**Slavery in the Roman world.** Slavery was pervasive in the first-century Mediterranean world. Slaves worked in households, farms, mines, workshops, and government offices. Some held positions of considerable responsibility as stewards and agents of their masters, and a slave of a powerful master could share some of that master's status. Calling oneself "a slave of Jesus Christ" therefore said two things at once: complete submission, and representation of a master whose authority exceeded every other. Modern readers should hold both senses without romanticizing an institution that was, for most of those caught in it, brutal.

**The family of Jesus.** The household of Joseph and Mary in Nazareth was a family of craftsmen (Mark 6:3 calls Jesus "the carpenter"). Hegesippus's account of Jude's grandsons farming a small plot of land suggests a family of modest means in the following generations.[^c1-10] By the time Jude wrote, members of this family were known throughout the churches. James led the church in Jerusalem, and "the brothers of the LORD" traveled as missionaries (1 Cor 9:5, LSV). Jude writes from inside this network of relationships, but he presents himself first of all as Christ's servant.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Called and kept (Isa 41–42; Rom 8).** The pairing of God's call with God's keeping goes back to Isaiah's portrait of the servant Israel: "I have called you, / And I say to you, You [are] My servant, / I have chosen you, and not rejected you" (Isa 41:9, LSV); "I, YHWH, called you in righteousness, / And I lay hold on your hand, and keep you" (Isa 42:6, LSV). Paul's chain of God's actions in Rom 8:30, "whom He called, these also He declared righteous; and whom He declared righteous, these also He glorified" (LSV), expresses the same confidence that God finishes what he begins. Jude belongs to this stream: those God calls, he holds. This is a shared theme rather than a direct allusion.

**Loved by God (Deut 7:7–8; Rom 1:7; 2 Thess 2:13).** Israel's election rested on God's love, not on its merit (Deut 7:7–8). Paul addresses Roman believers as "beloved of God, called holy ones" (Rom 1:7, LSV), joining "loved" and "called" as Jude does. Second Thessalonians gives thanks for "brothers, beloved by the LORD, that God chose you from the beginning to salvation" (2 Thess 2:13, LSV).

**Kept (John 17; 1 Pet 1:4–5; Rev 3:10).** Jesus' prayer, "Holy Father, keep them in Your Name" (John 17:11, LSV), uses the same verb as Jude, τηρέω. So does Jesus' further petition that the Father "keep them out of the evil" (John 17:15, LSV), and his promise in Revelation: "I also will keep you from the hour of the trial" (Rev 3:10, LSV). Peter describes believers as people "who, in the power of God are being guarded, through faith, to salvation" (1 Pet 1:5, LSV). The Aaronic blessing, "YHWH bless you and keep you" (Num 6:24, LSV), stands behind all of this language. These passages share Jude's conviction, though Jude's wording is not drawn directly from any of them.

**The greeting formula (Dan 4:1; 6:26; 1 Pet 1:2; 2 Pet 1:2).** As noted above, "be multiplied" appears in royal letters in Greek Daniel and in the openings of 1 and 2 Peter. This is a shared epistolary convention rather than a literary dependence.

**Mercy in benedictions (Gal 6:16; 1 Tim 1:2; 2 Tim 1:2; 2 John 3).** "Mercy" appears in several New Testament blessings, but only Jude combines it with peace *and love*.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Who is Jude?** This question is treated in the Historical Introduction. For the opening verses, it is enough to note that the self-description "servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James" is most naturally read as coming from the brother of Jesus and of James of Jerusalem. Scholars who regard the letter as pseudonymous read it as a later writer's appeal to that family's authority.

**2. "Loved" or "sanctified"?** The critical editions and the LSV read "loved." The Byzantine tradition reads "sanctified." The meaning differs only slightly, since both describe God's action on behalf of believers. The choice affects how strongly the opening anticipates the letter's theme of love (vv. 2, 12, 21).

**3. "Kept for," "by," or "in" Jesus Christ?** All three are grammatically possible. The choice affects the emphasis: Christ's return, Christ's agency, or union with Christ.

**4. How does God's keeping relate to human responsibility?** Jude says that believers *have been kept* (v. 1). He urges them to *keep themselves* (v. 21). He closes by praising the God who *is able to guard* them (v. 24). Christian traditions have related these statements in different ways:

- The **Reformed** tradition reads v. 1 and v. 24 as teaching the perseverance of the saints: those truly called and loved by God will be preserved to the end. On this view the command of v. 21 is the means by which God's keeping is worked out.
- **Wesleyan and Arminian** traditions emphasize that v. 21 is a real command whose neglect has real consequences, and they read the warnings of the letter (especially v. 5) as showing that those once delivered can fall away.
- **Catholic and Orthodox** traditions speak of grace and human cooperation working together, with God's keeping as the ground and enabling power of the believer's perseverance.

Jude himself does not resolve the theological puzzle. He states both sides with equal force and lets them stand together. This book follows his lead. The keeping of God comes first and frames everything, and the call to keep oneself is meant seriously. Readers will weigh the systematic question according to their traditions, but they should notice that Jude saw no contradiction in saying both.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

The greeting is the letter's foundation stone. It introduces three threads the rest of the letter will pull tight:

- **Keeping.** The readers are "kept" (v. 1). The rebels are "kept" for judgment (vv. 6, 13). The readers must "keep" themselves (v. 21). God is able to "guard" them (v. 24).
- **Love.** The readers are "loved" (v. 1). Love is wished upon them (v. 2). Their meals are "love-feasts" that intruders spoil (v. 12). They must stay in "the love of God" (v. 21).
- **Mercy.** Mercy is wished upon them (v. 2). They wait for Christ's mercy (v. 21). They are to show mercy to others (vv. 22–23).

These threads form a frame. The opening greeting (vv. 1–2) and the closing exhortation and doxology (vv. 20–25) share most of their key words: love, keep, mercy, Jesus Christ, God. Whatever darkness comes between, the readers are meant to read it from inside this frame.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*The following reflections suggest ways the passage may inform Christian life today. They are applications, not claims about what Jude's first readers were thinking.*

**Identity before instruction.** Jude tells his readers who they are before he tells them what to do. Modern Christian teaching often reverses the order and leads with demands. Jude's order suggests that believers may face hard truths and difficult tasks better when they begin from the security of being called, loved, and kept. For churches facing conflict, this ordering may itself be part of faithful leadership.

**Authority without self-promotion.** Jude had a family connection to Jesus that few could claim, yet he introduced himself as a servant and named his brother James instead of the Lord. Leaders in churches and Christian organizations today might ask how they present their credentials, and whether their self-descriptions point to themselves or to the one they serve.

**Being kept in a world of loss.** Many people experience faith as fragile, something they might lose through doubt, failure, or suffering. Jude's perfect participles ("having been loved," "having been kept") describe a settled state grounded in God's action. They do not deny the reality of struggle; the rest of the letter shows that Jude took struggle seriously. They do offer a place to stand.

**Blessing as a practice.** Jude's greeting is a prayer: "mercy and peace and love be multiplied to you!" Christians often greet one another casually. Jude's opening might encourage the practice of speaking blessing deliberately, in letters, messages, and gatherings, wishing others an abundance of God's gifts rather than mere pleasantries.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Jude describes his readers by what God has done for them (called, loved, kept) before he describes anything they must do. How might this order change the way you approach the challenges described later in the letter?
2. Why do you think Jude called himself a "servant of Jesus Christ" rather than "brother of the Lord"? What would each description have communicated to his readers?
3. The phrase "kept in [or for, or by] Jesus Christ" can be read three ways. Which reading seems most natural to you, and what difference does the choice make?
4. Jude says believers *have been kept* (v. 1) and must *keep themselves* (v. 21). How do you hold these two statements together? Where have you seen people emphasize one at the expense of the other?
5. Of the three gifts Jude wishes his readers (mercy, peace, love), which do you think your own community most needs "multiplied" right now, and why?
