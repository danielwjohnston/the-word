## B. Plain-English Orientation

Jude ends his letter not with greetings or travel plans but with praise. Having warned his readers of danger, urged them to keep themselves in God's love, and told them to rescue others, he turns their eyes to God. God is able to keep them from stumbling. God will bring them into his glorious presence, blameless and full of joy. To this God, the only God and Savior, belong glory, majesty, power, and authority, through Jesus Christ, before all time, now, and forever.

This doxology is one of the most beloved in Scripture and is often used to close Christian worship. In the context of Jude it is also the letter's final word on its central theme. The readers have been kept (v. 1) and must keep themselves (v. 21), and now they are assured that God is able to guard them. The danger of stumbling is real, but the one who holds them is stronger.

**A note on the text.** The LSV's wording of v. 25 follows the Byzantine tradition and the Textus Receptus: "to the only wise God our Savior … both now and forever." The SBLGNT and NA28 have a longer text: "to the only God our Savior, **through Jesus Christ our Lord**, [be] glory, majesty, power, and authority **before all time** and now and forever." This chapter discusses both, since the differences are theologically interesting.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### Τῷ δὲ δυναμένῳ (*Tō de dynamenō*) — ‘now to the one who is able’

The doxology begins with a dative, Τῷ δὲ δυναμένῳ (*Tō de dynamenō*), "Now to the one who is able." Its main clause comes only at the end: "to Him … [is] glory." Paul's letters close in a similar way: "And to Him who is able to establish you" (Rom 16:25, LSV); "and to Him who is able to do exceedingly [and] abundantly above all things that we ask or think" (Eph 3:20, LSV).

The emphasis falls on God's **ability**. Throughout the letter Jude has described powers that threaten the readers: intruders who slip in unnoticed, persuasive dreamers, slanderers, reefs, wild waves. Now he names the power that is greater. Everything depends not on the readers' strength but on God's.

### φυλάξαι ὑμᾶς ἀπταίστους (*phylaxai hymas aptaistous*) — ‘to guard you from stumbling’

The infinitive φυλάξαι (*phylaxai*), "to guard," comes from φυλάσσω (*phylassō*), a near synonym of τηρέω (*tēreō*), "keep," the word that has run through the whole letter. φυλάσσω carries the idea of a guard posted to protect, "implying custody and protection."[^c13-1] Jesus used it in his prayer: "I guarded those whom You have given to Me, and none of them were destroyed" (John 17:12, LSV).

**A textual note.** The SBLGNT, NA28, and TR read ὑμᾶς (*hymas*), "you," and the LSV follows them. The Robinson–Pierpont text, following most Byzantine manuscripts, reads αὐτούς (*autous*), "them," which shifts the focus slightly.[^c13-2]

**"Without stumbling"** translates ἀπταίστους (*aptaistous*), an adjective found nowhere else in the New Testament. It is formed from πταίω (*ptaiō*), "to stumble, trip," with the negative prefix ἀ-, and means "not stumbling, sure-footed."[^c13-3] It is used in the Greek Bible only once elsewhere, in 3 Maccabees, where God rescues the Jews of Egypt "unharmed" from destruction (ἀπταίστους, 3 Macc 6:39).[^c13-4] See the deep dive below.

### καὶ στῆσαι κατενώπιον τῆς δόξης αὐτοῦ ἀμώμους ἐν ἀγαλλιάσει (*kai stēsai katenōpion tēs doxēs autou amōmous en agalliasei*) — ‘and to make you stand before his glory without blemish, with great joy’

God's ability extends beyond protection along the way to the destination. He is able στῆσαι κατενώπιον τῆς δόξης αὐτοῦ (*stēsai katenōpion tēs doxēs autou*), "to make [you] stand before his glory." The verb ἵστημι (*histēmi*), "to set, make stand," suggests standing upright in the presence of a king or judge. The adverb κατενώπιον (*katenōpion*) means "before, in the presence of."[^c13-5] "His glory" is the radiant presence of God, the glory that Moses longed to see and that the prophets described. The scene is the final judgment, the same judgment described in vv. 14–15. For the ungodly it means conviction. For those kept by God it means standing before his glory.

They will stand **"unblemished"** (ἀμώμους, *amōmous*). The word was originally used of sacrificial animals "without blemish," and then morally of people who are "faultless."[^c13-6] Paul uses it of the goal of God's work: "to present you holy, and unblemished, and unblameable before Himself" (Col 1:22, LSV); "that He might present the Assembly to Himself in glory, having no spot or wrinkle … but that it may be holy and unblemished" (Eph 5:27, LSV). In Jude the word stands in deliberate contrast to the intruders, who were "stains" (v. 12) and whose garments were "stained" by the flesh (v. 23). The readers will be presented without stain.

And they will stand **"in gladness"** (ἐν ἀγαλλιάσει, *en agalliasei*). The noun means "exultation, exuberant joy."[^c13-7] It is the joy of celebration, of a festival or a wedding. Peter uses the related verb: "rejoice, that you may also rejoice in the revelation of His glory—exulting" (1 Pet 4:13, LSV). After so much darkness, Jude's letter ends with the picture of believers standing before God's glory, faultless and overflowing with joy.

### μόνῳ θεῷ σωτῆρι ἡμῶν (*monō theō sōtēri hēmōn*) — ‘to the only God our Savior’

The God who can do all this is "the only God our Savior." The LSV, following the TR and Byzantine text, reads "only **wise** God" (μόνῳ σοφῷ θεῷ, *monō sophō theō*). The critical texts lack "wise." Most likely a scribe added it under the influence of Paul's doxology, "to the only wise God, through Jesus Christ, to Him [be] glory for all ages" (Rom 16:27, LSV; cf. 1 Tim 1:17).[^c13-8] On either reading, the emphasis falls on "only." There is one God. Earlier, Jude called Jesus Christ "our only Master and Lord" (v. 4). The two "only"s stand side by side without contradiction. For Jude, confessing Jesus as the only Master does not compromise the confession of one God.

**"Our Savior"** (σωτῆρι ἡμῶν, *sōtēri hēmōn*) gives God the title σωτήρ, "Savior, deliverer." In the Greco-Roman world the title was commonly given to kings and emperors.[^c13-9] In the Bible it belongs to God, who saved Israel from Egypt (v. 5) and who saves now. The letter that began with "our common salvation" (v. 3) ends by naming God "our Savior."

### διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν (*dia Iēsou Christou tou kyriou hēmōn*) — ‘through Jesus Christ our Lord’

The critical texts include the phrase διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν (*dia Iēsou Christou tou kyriou hēmōn*), "through Jesus Christ our Lord." The phrase is absent from the Byzantine text, the TR, and therefore the LSV. It is well attested in early manuscripts, and most scholars regard it as original.[^c13-10]

If original, it says something important. Glory is given to God *through* Jesus Christ. That may mean that God's glory is revealed and exercised through Christ, or that believers give glory to God through Christ, or both. Paul's doxology in Romans has the same pattern ("to the only wise God, through Jesus Christ"). So does Peter's: "so that in all things God may be glorified through Jesus Christ, to whom is the glory and the power through the ages of the ages" (1 Pet 4:11, LSV). The doxology, like the whole letter, holds God and Jesus Christ together.

### δόξα μεγαλωσύνη κράτος καὶ ἐξουσία (*doxa megalōsynē kratos kai exousia*) — ‘glory, majesty, power, and authority’

Four attributes are ascribed to God:

- **Glory** (δόξα, *doxa*): God's radiant splendor and honor. This is the "glory" before which believers will stand (v. 24).
- **Greatness** or **majesty** (μεγαλωσύνη, *megalōsynē*): a word of the Greek Bible, used in Hebrews of God as "the Greatness in the highest" (Heb 1:3, LSV).[^c13-11]
- **Power** or **dominion** (κράτος, *kratos*): might and sovereign rule, common in New Testament doxologies (1 Tim 6:16; 1 Pet 4:11; 5:11; Rev 1:6).[^c13-12]
- **Authority** (ἐξουσία, *exousia*): the right to rule.

The list recalls David's prayer: "To You, O YHWH, [is] the greatness, and the might, and the beauty, and the victory, and the splendor … to You, O YHWH, [is] the kingdom" (1 Chr 29:11, LSV). The intruders "disregard lordship" (v. 8) and slander glorious beings (v. 8). The doxology declares where true glory, majesty, and authority lie.

**"Is" or "be"?** The Greek has no verb. Translators must supply one. The LSV supplies "[is]," making the doxology a declaration: glory *is* God's. Most translations supply "be," making it a wish or prayer: glory *be* to God. Both are grammatically possible. In 1 Pet 4:11 a verb is expressed ("to whom *is* the glory," ᾧ ἐστιν ἡ δόξα), which shows that the declarative sense was natural to early Christians. Perhaps the best understanding holds both: glory belongs to God, and the worshiping community gladly acknowledges it.

### πρὸ παντὸς τοῦ αἰῶνος καὶ νῦν καὶ εἰς πάντας τοὺς αἰῶνας (*pro pantos tou aiōnos kai nyn kai eis pantas tous aiōnas*) — ‘before all time, and now, and to all the ages’

The critical text has a magnificent triple expression of time: πρὸ παντὸς τοῦ αἰῶνος καὶ νῦν καὶ εἰς πάντας τοὺς αἰῶνας (*pro pantos tou aiōnos kai nyn kai eis pantas tous aiōnas*), "before all the age, and now, and into all the ages." God's glory reaches back before creation, fills the present, and extends without end into the future. The phrase "before all time" is absent from the Byzantine text and the LSV, which reads "both now and forever."[^c13-13]

The longer form is unusual in New Testament doxologies. It is the only one that explicitly includes the eternity *before* time. It fits the letter's sweep, which ranges from the ancient past (Enoch, the angels, the exodus), through the present (the intruders and the readers), to the future (the coming of the Lord, eternal life). The God who was glorious before all ages is the God who keeps his people now and will present them in glory forever.

### ἀμήν (*amēn*) — ‘amen’

The final word, ἀμήν (*amēn*), is a Hebrew word meaning "truly, so be it." It was the congregation's response to prayer and praise in the synagogue. Early Christians kept it in its Hebrew form. When Jude's letter was read aloud in the community, the people would likely have answered "Amen," making his doxology their own.

### Deep dive: Being kept from stumbling

Jude's last promise, that God is able to guard his readers "without stumbling," gathers up the letter's central theme.

**Stumbling in the Bible.** The image of the foot that slips is common in Scripture, especially in the Psalms. "He does not permit your foot to be moved, / He who is preserving you does not slumber" (Ps 121:3, LSV). "You have delivered my soul from death, / Do You not [keep] my feet from falling?" (Ps 56:13, LSV). "You have delivered my soul from death, / My eyes from tears, my feet from overthrowing" (Ps 116:8, LSV). In Psalm 73 the writer confesses, "As nothing, my steps have slipped, / For I have been envious of the boastful" (Ps 73:2, LSV). His stumbling began with envy of the arrogant, the kind of people Jude has described.

**What stumbling means in Jude.** In the context of the letter, to stumble is to fall away from "the faith once delivered" (v. 3). It means following the intruders into their perversion of grace, being swept along by wild waves, leaving the place God has given as the angels did (v. 6), or failing to believe as the wilderness generation did (v. 5). The letter has shown how real that danger is.

**The guard who does not sleep.** Against this danger stands the God who is "able." The verb φυλάσσω, "guard," suggests a sentry who keeps watch through the night. The Psalmist's "He who is preserving you does not slumber" captures the image (Ps 121:3, LSV). God's guarding does not depend on the believer's alertness. It is God's own vigilance.

**The relationship with v. 21.** How does God's guarding relate to the command "keep yourselves in the love of God" (v. 21)? Jude places both side by side without tension. God's ability to guard is the ground of the believer's confidence. The believer's keeping is the way that confidence is lived. The doxology does not cancel the command. It makes it possible. A believer who builds up, prays, and waits (vv. 20–21) does so in the assurance that God is able to finish what he began.

**Not perfection, but preservation.** "Without stumbling" does not mean that believers will never sin or struggle. The New Testament is honest about believers' failures ("we all make many stumbles," Jas 3:2, LSV). The promise is that God will keep them from the stumbling that leads to ruin, from falling away from him. The goal is to "set [you] in the presence of His glory unblemished," and that blamelessness is God's gift, not the reader's achievement.

**A word for the wavering.** Jude has just told his readers to show mercy to "those who are doubting" (v. 22). The doxology is good news for them too. The strength that keeps believers from stumbling is not their own certainty but God's ability. Those who waver may find their footing not by trying harder to be sure but by trusting the one who is able.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Doxologies in Jewish and Christian worship.** Jewish prayer was rich in blessings and doxologies ascribing glory to God, and the five books of the Psalter each close with one (Pss 41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52; 106:48; 150). Early Christians continued the practice, and doxologies often closed letters that would be read in worship (Rom 16:25–27; Phil 4:20; 1 Tim 1:17; 1 Pet 4:11; 5:11; 2 Pet 3:18). Jude's letter, read aloud in the community, would end in worship.

**"Savior" in the Roman world.** Hellenistic kings and Roman emperors were often hailed as "savior" (σωτήρ) and "benefactor" in inscriptions and official language.[^c13-14] When Christians called God "our Savior" and gave him "glory," "power," and "authority," they were making a claim that implicitly relativized every human ruler. Jude's doxology is not a political manifesto, but its language would not have been neutral in the first-century world.

**Standing in the presence of a king.** In the ancient Near East, to stand in the presence of a king was an honor reserved for those admitted to court. To be "set" before God's glory "unblemished" and "in gladness" is the image of acceptance at the royal court of heaven.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Romans 16:25–27; Ephesians 3:20–21 (form).** The closest parallels in form: "to Him who is able … to the only wise God, through Jesus Christ, to Him [be] glory" (Rom 16:25, 27, LSV). The parallel probably explains the addition of "wise" in some manuscripts of Jude.

**1 Timothy 1:17; 1 Peter 4:11; 2 Peter 3:18 (doxologies).** Other New Testament doxologies use similar vocabulary. Second Peter ends: "to Him [is] the glory both now, and to the day of the age! Amen" (2 Pet 3:18, LSV), a doxology to Christ that resembles Jude's.

**Psalms 121; 56:13; 116:8 (being kept from stumbling).** The Old Testament roots of Jude's promise.

**Colossians 1:22; Ephesians 1:4; 5:27; 1 Thessalonians 3:13 (presentation unblemished).** The goal of God's work is a people presented holy and blameless before him.

**1 Chronicles 29:10–13 (attributes).** David's blessing of God, ascribing greatness, power, glory, and kingdom to him.

**John 17:5; 2 Timothy 1:9 (before time).** "The glory that I had with You before the world was" (John 17:5, LSV); grace "given to us in Christ Jesus, before the times of the ages" (2 Tim 1:9, LSV). These texts share the critical text's vision of glory before time.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Which text of v. 25 is original?** Most scholars accept the longer text of the critical editions, with "through Jesus Christ our Lord" and "before all time," and without "wise." The Byzantine form is shorter in two places and adds "wise," probably from Rom 16:27. Readers of the LSV should know that their translation reflects the Byzantine form here.

**2. Is the doxology addressed to God or to Christ?** It is addressed to "the only God our Savior," with glory ascribed "through Jesus Christ" (critical text). Some later doxologies are addressed directly to Christ (2 Pet 3:18; Rev 5:13). Jude's is addressed to God through Christ, and that reflects the pattern of early Christian worship.

**3. Does "able to guard you without stumbling" guarantee perseverance?** As in Chapters 1 and 11, Christian traditions answer differently. All agree that the doxology expresses confidence in God's power and faithfulness. Some read it as a guarantee that true believers will persevere. Others read it as a statement of God's ability that believers must still respond to. Jude's placement of the doxology after v. 21 suggests that he did not see the two as competing.

**4. "Is" or "be"?** The verbless Greek allows both. The LSV's "[is]" emphasizes that glory already belongs to God. The traditional "be" emphasizes the worshipers' acknowledgment.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

The doxology completes the letter's frame. The opening described the readers as "called, … loved … and … kept" (v. 1). The ending praises God who is "able to guard" them (v. 24). Between these two statements of God's keeping lies everything else: the intruders, the examples of judgment, the woes, the images of disorder, the call to build, pray, keep, wait, and show mercy.

Every major theme of the letter is gathered in these two verses:

- **Keeping and guarding:** "able to guard you" (v. 24), completing the τηρέω thread (vv. 1, 6, 13, 21).
- **Stumbling and falling:** the danger shown by the wilderness generation, the angels, and the intruders, and averted by God's power.
- **Blemish and purity:** "unblemished" (v. 24), in contrast to the stains (v. 12) and the stained garment (v. 23).
- **Glory:** the "glorious [ones]" slandered by the intruders (v. 8), and the true glory of God before which the readers will stand (vv. 24–25).
- **Joy:** "in gladness" (v. 24), the final mood of a letter that began with mercy, peace, and love.
- **Salvation:** "our common salvation" (v. 3) and "God our Savior" (v. 25).
- **Lordship:** "our only Master and Lord—Jesus Christ" (v. 4) and ‘through Jesus Christ our Lord’ (v. 25, critical text).
- **Time:** ancient examples, present dangers, and future judgment, all embraced by the eternal glory of God, ‘before all time, and now, and forever.’

Jude wanted to write about "our common salvation" (v. 3) and was forced to write a warning instead. In the doxology he finally gets to write about salvation, and he does it in praise.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Ending in worship.** Jude ends a hard letter with praise. Christians facing conflict, confusion, or danger might learn from his example to return, finally, to the worship of God, not as an escape from difficulty but as the truest response to it.

**Confidence that is not self-confidence.** The doxology's emphasis on God's ability frees believers from depending on their own steadiness. That is good news for the wavering, the weary, and those who have stumbled before.

**Joy as the end.** The final picture is of believers standing before God's glory "in gladness." Christian faith, for all its seriousness about sin and judgment, aims at joy. Communities that have forgotten that may need Jude's ending as much as his warnings.

**Using the doxology.** Jude 24–25 has long been used as a benediction at the close of Christian services. Its use may be enriched by remembering its context. It is spoken to people who face real dangers, by someone who knows those dangers well, in confidence that God is greater.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. How does reading the doxology after the rest of Jude change its meaning for you, compared with hearing it as a stand-alone benediction?
2. What does "stumbling" look like in your life or community? How does the promise that God "is able to guard you" speak to that?
3. Jude pictures believers standing before God's glory "unblemished, in gladness." What does that picture suggest about the goal of the Christian life?
4. The critical text includes "before all time, and now, and forever." What difference does it make to think of God's glory as stretching back before creation?
5. Jude both commands believers to keep themselves (v. 21) and praises God for keeping them (v. 24). How do you hold these together in your own life?
