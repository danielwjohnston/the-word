## B. Plain-English Orientation

Jude completes his series of images with two more, drawn from the sea and the sky. The intruders are like **wild waves** that throw up foam and scum on the shore, except that what they throw up is their own shameful conduct. They are also like **wandering stars**, heavenly bodies that have left their proper courses and for whom the deepest darkness has been reserved forever.

Together with the clouds and trees of v. 12, these images complete a tour of the created world: the air, the earth, the sea, and the heavens. In each region Jude finds a picture of disorder: clouds that do not rain, trees that do not bear, waves that cannot rest, stars that do not keep their paths. Jewish readers familiar with 1 Enoch would have recognized the pattern. In the natural world, everything obeys God's order. Only sinners do not.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### κύματα ἄγρια θαλάσσης (*kymata agria thalassēs*) — ‘wild waves of the sea’

The Greek κύματα ἄγρια θαλάσσης (*kymata agria thalassēs*) means "wild waves of the sea." The adjective ἄγριος (*agrios*) is used of wild animals and uncultivated land. Here it describes the untamed, restless violence of a stormy sea.

For the people of the Bible, the sea was not a place of recreation. It was the great image of chaos and danger. In the beginning "darkness [was] on the face of the deep" (Gen 1:2, LSV), and God's power is shown in setting limits to the sea: "Who has made sand the border of the sea, / A perpetual limit, and it does not pass over it, / They shake themselves, and they are not able" (Jer 5:22, LSV). In Job, God says to the sea, "To here you come, and no more, / And a command is placed / On the pride of your billows" (Job 38:11, LSV). The Psalms praise God as "ruler over the pride of the sea" (Ps 89:9, LSV). The sea is powerful, but in Scripture it is held in check by God's command.

### ἐπαφρίζοντα τὰς ἑαυτῶν αἰσχύνας (*epaphrizonta tas heautōn aischynas*) — ‘foaming up their own shameful deeds’

The participle ἐπαφρίζοντα (*epaphrizonta*), "foaming up," another word found only here in the New Testament, comes from ἀφρός (*aphros*), "foam."[^c7-1] The image is of breakers that heave up foam, weed, and debris and leave them on the shore.

What the waves throw up is τὰς ἑαυτῶν αἰσχύνας (*tas heautōn aischynas*), "their own shames," a plural meaning "shameful deeds" or "disgraceful things." The LSV supplies "[deeds]" to make the sense clear.

The image almost certainly draws on Isaiah: "And the wicked [are] as the driven out sea, / For it is not able to rest, / And its waters cast out filth and mire. 'There is no peace,' said my God, 'for the wicked!'" (Isa 57:20–21, LSV). As noted in the Introduction, the Greek Septuagint version of this verse lacks the "filth and mire," while the Hebrew includes it. Jude's image matches the Hebrew.[^c7-2]

The point is twofold. First, the intruders are restless and agitated, never at peace, always churning, like the sea of Isaiah that "is not able to rest." Second, their activity produces only filth. As a storm leaves a beach strewn with foam and debris, their teaching and conduct leave behind visible shame. Paul's description of those "whose glory [is] in their shame" (Phil 3:19, LSV) is a close parallel. The intruders may have boasted of their freedom, but what they produced was disgraceful.

### ἀστέρες πλανῆται (*asteres planētai*) — ‘wandering stars’

The final image is ἀστέρες πλανῆται (*asteres planētai*), "wandering stars." The LSV's "stars going astray" brings out the moral sense of πλανάω (*planaō*), "to lead astray, wander." The noun πλανήτης (*planētēs*), "wanderer," unique in the New Testament to this verse, is the source of the English word "planet."[^c7-3] Greek astronomers called the planets "wandering stars" because, unlike the fixed stars, they moved across the sky along irregular-seeming paths. The word also links back to "the deceit (πλάνη, *planē*) of Balaam" in v. 11. The intruders wander, and they lead others to wander.

What did Jude's readers picture? Several possibilities have been suggested, and they are not mutually exclusive:

1. **Planets.** The ordinary meaning of the phrase. To an observer, the planets seemed to break the regular order of the heavens.
2. **Shooting stars or meteors.** Stars that appear to fall from their places and vanish into darkness. This fits "for whom the gloom of the darkness … has been kept," but the word πλανῆται does not usually mean meteors.
3. **Stars as angels.** In Jewish and early Christian thought, stars were often associated with angels (Rev 1:20; 9:1; Job 38:7). In 1 Enoch, as the deep dive shows, disobedient stars are punished in a place of darkness and fire. On this reading, the intruders are compared to the rebellious heavenly beings of v. 6.

The third possibility is the strongest, because of Jude's evident use of 1 Enoch. It combines naturally with the first. To an ancient Jewish observer, a "wandering" star might be precisely a star that had transgressed its appointed course.

There may also be a practical image here. Ancient sailors navigated by the stars. A star that did not keep its place would lead travelers astray, just as the intruders' teaching misled the community. That fits the nautical imagery of the reefs (v. 12) and the sea (v. 13).

### οἷς ὁ ζόφος τοῦ σκότους εἰς αἰῶνα τετήρηται (*hois ho zophos tou skotous eis aiōna tetērētai*) — ‘for whom the gloom of darkness has been kept forever’

The final clause, οἷς ὁ ζόφος τοῦ σκότους εἰς αἰῶνα τετήρηται (*hois ho zophos tou skotous eis aiōna tetērētai*), "for whom the gloom of darkness has been reserved forever," announces the destiny of the wandering stars. It repeats two key terms:

- **ζόφος** (*zophos*), "gloom," used in v. 6 for the darkness in which the rebellious angels are held. The repetition binds the intruders' fate to the angels'.
- **τετήρηται** (*tetērētai*), "has been kept," the perfect passive of τηρέω (*tēreō*). This is the fourth occurrence of "keep" in the letter. The readers have been kept for Jesus Christ (v. 1). The angels did not keep their position and are kept in bonds (v. 6). Now darkness is kept for the intruders.

The phrase "the gloom of the darkness" (ὁ ζόφος τοῦ σκότους) doubles the idea of darkness, which is something like "the blackness of darkness" (WBS) or "blackest darkness" (BSB). The LSV's "throughout the age" renders εἰς αἰῶνα (*eis aiōna*), literally "into the age," a common biblical idiom for "forever." Some manuscripts read εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα with the article. The meaning is unchanged.[^c7-4]

For a star, whose nature is to shine, the ultimate judgment is darkness. The image recalls Jesus' sayings about "the outer darkness—there will be the weeping and the gnashing of the teeth" (Matt 8:12, LSV). Second Peter uses Jude's phrase almost word for word: "to whom the deepest gloom of darkness has been kept throughout the age" (2 Pet 2:17, LSV).

**A caution on "planet."** It is tempting to read modern astronomy into Jude's "wandering stars," as though Jude were making a scientific observation about planetary orbits. Jude uses the ancient observational category, lights in the sky that do not keep the regular pattern of the fixed stars, and gives it a moral and theological meaning. The image works because, to his readers, the heavens normally displayed perfect order.

### Deep dive: Wandering stars and ancient cosmological imagery

To understand Jude's last image, it helps to step into the ancient view of the heavens.

**Order in the heavens.** For ancient peoples, the night sky was the supreme picture of order. The fixed stars wheeled in the same patterns year after year, and the calendar, agriculture, and navigation depended on them. Scripture celebrates this order: "Praise Him, sun and moon, / Praise Him, all stars of light. … He gave a statute, and they do not pass over" (Ps 148:3, 6, LSV). Jeremiah speaks of God as the one "giving the sun for a light by day, / The statutes of moon and stars for a light by night" (Jer 31:35, LSV). The Jewish *Testament of Naphtali* draws the moral lesson: "Sun and moon and stars change not their order; so also you shall not change the law of God in the disorderliness of your doings."[^c7-5]

**Disorder as rebellion.** Against this background, a heavenly body that did not keep its course was a picture of rebellion. 1 Enoch develops this idea vividly. Enoch, on his heavenly journey, sees "seven stars like great burning mountains," and the angel explains: "This place is the end of heaven and earth: this has become a prison for the stars and the host of heaven. And the stars which roll over the fire are they which have transgressed the commandment of the Lord in the beginning of their rising, because they did not come forth at their appointed times. And He was wroth with them, and bound them" (1 Enoch 18:13–16, trans. Charles).[^c7-6] Later he sees "seven stars of the heaven bound together," and is told: "These are of the number of the stars ⌈of heaven⌉, which have transgressed the commandment of the Lord, and are bound here" (21:3, 6).

In 1 Enoch these stars are closely linked with the fallen angels. Their "prison" is described in the same terms as the angels' prison (21:10: "This place is the prison of the angels"). Clement of Alexandria read Jude 13 in this way: "Wandering stars,—that is, he means those who err and are apostates are of that kind of stars which fell from the seats of the angels—to whom, for their apostasy, the blackness of darkness is reserved forever."[^c7-7]

**The four regions of nature.** The pattern goes further. 1 Enoch opens, immediately after the prophecy Jude quotes in vv. 14–15, with a call to observe the obedience of nature:

> Observe ye everything that takes place in the heaven, how they do not change their orbits, ⌈and⌉ the luminaries which are in the heaven, how they all rise and set in order each in its season, and transgress not against their appointed order. … Behold the summer and the winter, how the whole earth is filled with water, and clouds and dew and rain lie upon it.
>
> — 1 Enoch 2:1, 3 (trans. Charles)

It goes on to the trees ("how the trees cover themselves with green leaves and bear fruit," 5:1) and the sea ("how the sea and the rivers in like manner accomplish and change not their tasks," 5:3). Then it turns to the sinners: "But ye—ye have not been steadfast, nor done the commandments of the Lord, But ye have turned away and spoken proud and hard words With your impure mouths against His greatness" (5:4).[^c7-8]

Later, 1 Enoch describes how, "in the days of the sinners," nature itself will fall into disorder: "the rain shall be kept back," "the fruits of the trees shall be withheld in their time," and "many chiefs of the stars shall transgress the order (prescribed)" (1 Enoch 80:2–3, 6).[^c7-9]

Compare Jude's four nature images:

| Jude 12–13 | Region | 1 Enoch 2–5: obedient nature | 1 Enoch 80: disorder in the days of sinners |
|---|---|---|---|
| "waterless clouds" | air/sky | "clouds and dew and rain lie upon it" (2:3) | "the rain shall be kept back" (80:2) |
| "autumnal trees without fruit" | earth | "the trees … bear fruit" (5:1) | "the fruits of the trees shall be withheld" (80:3) |
| "wild waves of a sea" | sea | "the sea and the rivers … change not their tasks" (5:3) | — |
| "stars going astray" | heavens | luminaries "transgress not against their appointed order" (2:1) | "many chiefs of the stars shall transgress the order" (80:6) |

The correspondence is striking enough to suggest that Jude had these passages of 1 Enoch in mind. J. B. Mayor drew the same connection for the last image: "ἀστέρες πλανῆται. This is borrowed from Enoch," citing 1 Enoch 80, "In the days of the sinners, many chiefs of the stars will err, and will alter their orbits and tasks."[^c7-10] Each part of creation has its God-given task: clouds give rain, trees bear fruit, the sea keeps its bounds, and stars keep their courses. The intruders are like nature gone wrong. They fail their purpose as surely as a rainless cloud, a fruitless tree, a sea that will not rest, or a star that leaves its path.

The parallel also explains why Jude's images move straight into the quotation from Enoch (vv. 14–15). 1 Enoch 1:9, which Jude quotes, stands just before the call to "observe" nature in chapters 2–5. Jude may be moving through a passage he knew well: the nature contrast first, then the prophecy that precedes it in the book.

**What Jude adds.** Jude does not simply repeat 1 Enoch. He applies its cosmic images to people *within* the Christian community, which turns a description of the wicked in general into a warning about leaders the readers know. He also connects the stars' fate with the language of "keeping" that runs through his letter.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**The sea in the ancient Near East.** In the myths of Israel's neighbors, the sea was a chaotic divine power that the storm god had to defeat. Israel's scriptures echo this imagery but transform it. The sea is a creature, powerful but subject to the LORD (Ps 93:3–4). For people who lived mostly inland and knew the sea from the coastline or from dangerous voyages, its restless waves and debris-strewn beaches were familiar images of turmoil.

**Astronomy and astrology.** Educated people in the Greco-Roman world knew the five visible planets (Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn) as "wanderers." Many also believed that the stars and planets influenced human affairs, and astrology was widespread. Jewish and Christian writers generally rejected astrological fatalism. 1 Enoch even warns that sinners will "err and take them to be gods" (80:7). Jude uses the image of wandering stars to describe people who have left their place, not to say anything about the influence of the heavens.

**Darkness and judgment.** In both Jewish and Greek thought, the realm of the dead and the place of punishment were associated with darkness, ζόφος in Homer, "outer darkness" in Jesus' sayings, and the dark prison of the angels in 1 Enoch. Jude draws on this shared imagery.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Isaiah 57:20–21 (probable allusion).** "The wicked [are] as the driven out sea, / For it is not able to rest, / And its waters cast out filth and mire" (LSV). Jude's image of waves foaming up shame matches the Hebrew of this verse closely.

**Job 38:8–11; Psalm 89:9; Jeremiah 5:22 (shared theme).** God's restraint of the sea's "pride" provides the background against which "wild waves" appear as rebellion.

**James 1:6 (verbal and thematic link).** James compares "he who is doubting" to "a wave of the sea, driven by wind and tossed" (LSV). The verb translated "doubting" (διακρίνομαι, *diakrinomai*) is the same verb Jude uses of those who are "doubting" in v. 22. The restless sea is an image of instability both in James and in Jude.

**Revelation 1:20; 8:10; 9:1; Isaiah 14:12 (shared imagery).** Stars as angels, and fallen stars as images of judgment or of rebellious powers, appear across Scripture. Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon, "How you have fallen from the heavens, / O shining one, son of the dawn!" (Isa 14:12, LSV), was later read by many Christian interpreters as a description of Satan's fall.

**Daniel 12:3; Philippians 2:15 (contrast).** The faithful are pictured as stars that shine: "those justifying the multitude as stars for all time and forever" (Dan 12:3, LSV); believers "appear as luminaries in the world" (Phil 2:15, LSV). The wandering stars of Jude 13 are their dark opposite.

**2 Peter 2:17 (literary parallel).** Peter keeps the "gloom of darkness" but applies it to "wells without water, and clouds driven by a storm" (LSV), and drops the waves and stars.

**1 Enoch 2–5; 18; 21; 80 (source tradition).** See the deep dive.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Do the images reflect 1 Enoch?** Many scholars think so, given the correspondence of the four regions of nature and Jude's explicit use of 1 Enoch in the next verses. Others regard the images as drawn more generally from common Jewish and biblical imagery. The parallels with 1 Enoch 2–5 and 80 are strong enough to make the connection probable, though not certain.

**2. What are the "wandering stars"?** Planets, meteors, or stars identified with fallen angels. The Enochic background favors the last, without excluding the ordinary meaning "planets."

**3. Does "forever" imply eternal punishment?** The phrase "has been kept … throughout the age" (εἰς αἰῶνα) uses standard biblical language for permanence. As with "continuous fire" in v. 7, questions about the nature and duration of final judgment belong to the wider biblical and theological discussion. Jude's own concern is to stress the seriousness and finality of the intruders' destiny, in contrast with the readers, who await "continuous life" (v. 21).

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verse 13 completes the application begun in v. 12. The five images (reefs, clouds, trees, waves, stars) have moved from the community's table outward through creation, and they end in darkness. The repetition of "gloom" (ζόφος) from v. 6 and of "kept" (τηρέω) from vv. 1 and 6 ties this conclusion to the beginning of Jude's argument. The intruders' fate is the fate of the rebel angels.

The verse also sets up the next section. The nature imagery of 1 Enoch 2–5 leads naturally to the prophecy of 1 Enoch 1:9, quoted in vv. 14–15, which announces the coming of the Lord to judge "all the impious." Jude moves from images to prophecy, from picture to proclamation.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Restlessness and peace.** Isaiah's sea "is not able to rest," and "There is no peace … for the wicked." Jude's wild waves may prompt reflection on how a life without God's order can be restless, churning, and productive mainly of debris. In contrast, Jude wishes his readers peace (v. 2).

**Order as gift.** The ancient view of the heavens saw order as beautiful and good. Modern readers who value freedom from constraint may find it fruitful to consider how faithfulness to God's order can be compared to a star keeping its course: not a cage, but the condition of shining.

**Guides who lead astray.** Wandering stars were useless, and dangerous, to navigators. Believers might consider whom they look to for guidance, and how to tell a reliable guide from one who has left the path. They might also consider whether their own lives are guides others can safely follow.

**The beauty of creation's obedience.** 1 Enoch, and probably Jude, invited readers to observe nature's faithfulness: rain in its season, fruit in its time, the sea within its bounds, the stars on their courses. In an age of ecological disruption, that invitation may take on new resonance. Though ancient writers did not imagine modern environmental science, the conviction that human sin and creation's disorder are connected runs through Scripture (Gen 3:17–18; Rom 8:19–22). The application must be made carefully, but it is not foreign to the biblical vision.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. Why do you think Jude used images from nature (clouds, trees, sea, stars) to describe people? What do these images communicate that plain description could not?
2. Read 1 Enoch 2:1–5:4 and 80:2–8 (or the excerpts in this chapter). Do you find the connection with Jude 12–13 convincing? What difference does it make to your reading?
3. Isaiah says the wicked are like a sea that "is not able to rest." What forms of restlessness do you observe in contemporary life, and what might Jude's image suggest about their source?
4. Daniel pictures the faithful as stars that shine forever, and Jude pictures the intruders as stars headed for darkness. What do you think it means for a believer to "keep course" like a star?
5. How might the ancient conviction that creation displays God's order shape the way Christians relate to the natural world today?

### 1 Enoch 21 {#hl-c07-1en-21}

*R. H. Charles, The Book of Enoch (1917), public domain*

**1** And I proceeded to where things were chaotic. **2** And I saw there something horrible: I saw neither a heaven above nor a firmly founded earth, but a place chaotic and horrible. **3** And there I saw seven stars of the heaven bound together in it, like great mountains and burning with fire. **4** Then I said: ‘For what sin are they bound, and on what account have they been cast in hither?’ **5** Then said Uriel, one of the holy angels, who was with me, and was chief over them, and said: ‘Enoch, why dost thou ask, and why art thou eager for the truth? **6** These are of the number of the stars of heaven, which have transgressed the commandment of the Lord, and are bound here till ten thousand years, the time entailed by their sins, are consummated.’ **7** And from thence I went to another place, which was still more horrible than the former, and I saw a horrible thing: a great fire there which burnt and blazed, and the place was cleft as far as the abyss, being full of great descending columns of fire: neither its extent or magnitude could I see, nor could I conjecture. **8** Then I said: ‘How fearful is the place and how terrible to look upon!’ **9** Then Uriel answered me, one of the holy angels who was with me, and said unto me: ‘Enoch, why hast thou such fear and affright?’ And I answered: ‘Because of this fearful place, and because of the spectacle of the pain.’ **10** And he said unto me: ‘This place is the prison of the angels, and here they will be imprisoned for ever.’

### 1 Enoch 80 {#hl-c07-1en-80}

*R. H. Charles, The Book of Enoch (1917), public domain*

**1** And in those days the angel Uriel answered and said to me: ‘Behold, I have shown thee everything, Enoch, and I have revealed everything to thee that thou shouldst see this sun and this moon, and the leaders of the stars of the heaven and all those who turn them, their tasks and times and departures. **2** And in the days of the sinners the years shall be shortened, And their seed shall be tardy on their lands and fields, And all things on the earth shall alter, And shall not appear in their time: And the rain shall be kept back And the heaven shall withhold (it). **3** And in those times the fruits of the earth shall be backward, And shall not grow in their time, And the fruits of the trees shall be withheld in their time. **4** And the moon shall alter her order, And not appear at her time. **5** [And in those days the sun shall be seen and he shall journey in the evening on the extremity of the great chariot in the west] And shall shine more brightly than accords with the order of light. **6** And many chiefs of the stars shall transgress the order (prescribed). And these shall alter their orbits and tasks, And not appear at the seasons prescribed to them. **7** And the whole order of the stars shall be concealed from the sinners, And the thoughts of those on the earth shall err concerning them, [And they shall be altered from all their ways], Yea, they shall err and take them to be gods. **8** And evil shall be multiplied upon them, And punishment shall come upon them So as to destroy all.’
