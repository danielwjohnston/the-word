# Chapter 8. Enoch, the Seventh from Adam (Jude 14–15)

## A. The Text

> **14** And Enoch, the seventh from Adam, also prophesied to these, saying, “Behold, the LORD has come with myriads of His holy ones, **15** to do judgment against all, and to convict all their impious ones, concerning all their works of impiety that they did impiously, and concerning all the harsh [words] that impious sinners spoke against Him.”
>
> — Jude 14–15, LSV

## B. Plain-English Orientation

Jude now quotes a prophecy attributed to Enoch, the ancient patriarch who "habitually walks with God, and he is not, for God has taken him" (Gen 5:24, LSV). The prophecy announces that the Lord comes with countless holy angels to judge everyone and to convict the ungodly of all their ungodly deeds and of all the harsh words they have spoken against him. Jude says that Enoch prophesied "to," or about, "these," the intruders. He sees the ancient prophecy as describing people in his own community.

The quotation comes from the opening chapter of the book known today as **1 Enoch**, a Jewish writing composed in stages over the last centuries before Christ. This is the only explicit quotation in Jude, and the clearest case in the New Testament of a Jewish writing from outside the Hebrew Bible being quoted and introduced as prophecy. It has raised questions from the second century to the present: Did Jude regard 1 Enoch as Scripture? What does his quotation imply about the book's authority? This chapter looks first at what the quotation says and how Jude uses it, and then turns to the questions of canon.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### "And Enoch, the seventh from Adam"

Genesis 5 lists the generations from Adam to Noah: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch (cf. 1 Chr 1:1–3). Counting Adam as the first, as ancient counting usually did, Enoch is the seventh: ἕβδομος ἀπὸ Ἀδάμ (*hebdomos apo Adam*), "seventh from Adam." The phrase appears in 1 Enoch itself, where Noah refers to his great-grandfather as "taken up, the seventh from Adam, the first man whom the Lord of Spirits created" (1 Enoch 60:8), and where Enoch says, "I was born the seventh in the first week" (1 Enoch 93:3).[^c8-1]

The description does three things:

1. **It identifies him.** There was another Enoch, Cain's son, after whom Cain named a city (Gen 4:17). Jude means the righteous Enoch of Seth's line.
2. **It stresses antiquity.** Enoch lived before the flood, in the seventh generation of humanity. His prophecy is among the oldest words of God imaginable. Calvin saw this as Jude's purpose: "he says expressly that he was the seventh from Adam, in order to commend the antiquity of the prophecy, because it existed in the world before the flood."[^c8-2]
3. **It may suggest completeness.** Seven was a number of fullness and perfection in Jewish thought. The "seventh" from Adam was a person of special standing, the one who "walks with God" and was taken by God. Jude does not draw attention to the symbolism, but his readers may have felt it.

Genesis says that Enoch "habitually walks with God" and that "God has taken him" (Gen 5:24, LSV). Hebrews adds: "By faith Enoch was translated—not to see death … for before his translation he had been testified to—that he had pleased God well" (Heb 11:5, LSV). A man so close to God and taken from the earth without dying was an ideal recipient of revelation, and Jewish tradition attributed to him visions of heaven, of the secrets of the cosmos, and of the final judgment.

### "also prophesied to these"

The verb προεφήτευσεν (*proephēteusen*), "prophesied," is significant. Jude does not say that Enoch "wrote" or that "it is written." He says that Enoch prophesied. The word "also" (καί, *kai*) links this prophecy to the other evidence Jude has presented: Israel's history, the angels, Sodom, Michael, Cain, Balaam, and Korah all testify against the intruders, and so does Enoch.

The dative τούτοις (*toutois*), "to these," is best understood as "about these" or "with reference to these." Jude does not claim that Enoch foresaw these particular individuals by name. He claims that the people Enoch described, the "impious" whom the Lord will judge, are people like these. This is the same way of reading prophecy found in the Dead Sea Scrolls and elsewhere in the New Testament: ancient words are understood as speaking to the reader's own time.

### "saying, 'Behold, the LORD has come'"

The quotation opens with Ἰδού (*Idou*), "Behold!" or "Look!", a prophetic call to attention.

"The LORD" translates κύριος (*kyrios*). The LSV prints it in capitals because the reference is to the God of Israel. In 1 Enoch, the subject is God: "And behold! He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones" (1 Enoch 1:9, trans. Charles). In Jude, however, "Lord" most often refers to Jesus (vv. 4, 17, 21; also, on some readings, v. 5). Many interpreters think Jude understands the coming Lord of Enoch's prophecy to be Jesus Christ at his return. That would be a further instance of the pattern seen in v. 5 and v. 4: language about the God of Israel applied to Jesus. The New Testament repeatedly pictures Jesus coming "with all His holy ones" (1 Thess 3:13, LSV) and "with messengers of His power" (2 Thess 1:7, LSV).

The verb ἦλθεν (*ēlthen*) is in the aorist, "came" (LSV: "has come"). In the context of a prophecy of future judgment, the past tense expresses certainty. Hebrew prophets often spoke of future events in the perfect tense, as though already accomplished, and Jude's Greek reproduces the effect. Some interpreters suggest that the past tense reflects the Aramaic or Greek form of Enoch that Jude knew.

### "with myriads of His holy ones"

The phrase ἐν ἁγίαις μυριάσιν αὐτοῦ (*en hagiais myriasin autou*), "with his holy myriads," means "with tens of thousands of his holy ones." A μυριάς (*myrias*) is ten thousand, and the plural means "countless thousands." The "holy ones" are angels, the heavenly army that accompanies God in his appearing.

The image goes back to the theophany at Sinai, as described in Moses' blessing: "YHWH has come from Sinai, / And has risen from Seir for them; He has shone from Mount Paran, / And has come [with] myriads of holy ones" (Deut 33:2, LSV). 1 Enoch's opening deliberately echoes this. It describes God coming to "tread upon the earth, (even) on Mount Sinai" (1 Enoch 1:4).[^c8-3] Zechariah also foresees the day when "my God YHWH has come in, / All holy ones [are] with You" (Zech 14:5, LSV). Jesus speaks of the Son of Man coming "in His glory, and all the holy messengers with Him" (Matt 25:31, LSV).

### "to do judgment against all"

The purpose of the coming is ποιῆσαι κρίσιν κατὰ πάντων (*poiēsai krisin kata pantōn*), "to execute judgment against all." The word "all" (πᾶς, *pas*) is repeated four times in the quotation: judgment on all, convicting all, concerning all their works, concerning all the harsh words. The judgment is universal and complete. Nothing escapes.

### "and to convict all their impious ones"

The verb ἐλέγξαι (*elenxai*) means "to expose, convict, prove guilty." It is the language of a courtroom in which guilt is demonstrated. Jesus says that the Spirit "will convict the world concerning sin, and concerning righteousness, and concerning judgment" (John 16:8, LSV).

**A textual note.** The object of "convict" varies in the manuscripts:

| Edition | Reading | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| SBLGNT, WH, Tregelles | πάντας τοὺς ἀσεβεῖς | "all the impious" |
| Robinson–Pierpont (Byzantine), TR | πάντας τοὺς ἀσεβεῖς αὐτῶν | "all their impious ones" (LSV) |
| NA28 | πᾶσαν ψυχήν | "every soul" |

The NA28 reading, "every soul," corresponds more closely to the wording of 1 Enoch 1:9, "to convict all flesh" in Charles's translation, and the NA28 editors judged it original. The SBLGNT editors preferred "all the impious," which fits Jude's emphasis on the word "impious." The LSV follows the Byzantine reading with "their." The difference does not affect the sense of the passage: on any reading, the ungodly are convicted.[^c8-4] (The TR also reads ἐξελέγξαι, a strengthened form of the verb.)

### "concerning all their works of impiety that they did impiously"

Here the quotation reaches its most emphatic point. The Greek περὶ πάντων τῶν ἔργων ἀσεβείας αὐτῶν ὧν ἠσέβησαν (*peri pantōn tōn ergōn asebeias autōn hōn ēsebēsan*) uses the root ἀσεβ- (*aseb-*), "ungodly," twice in quick succession: "their ungodly works which they ungodly did." With "impious" earlier and "impious sinners" at the end, the word family appears four times in a single sentence. The verb ἠσέβησαν (*ēsebēsan*), "they acted impiously," occurs nowhere else in the New Testament.

The repetition is deliberate. Jude introduced the intruders as ἀσεβεῖς, "impious," in v. 4. Now, in the words of the ancient prophecy, the same word rings out four times. Jude's earlier description of them was not merely his own opinion. It was the language of prophecy.

### "and concerning all the harsh [words] that impious sinners spoke against Him"

The last clause shifts from deeds to words: περὶ πάντων τῶν σκληρῶν ὧν ἐλάλησαν κατ' αὐτοῦ ἁμαρτωλοὶ ἀσεβεῖς (*peri pantōn tōn sklērōn hōn elalēsan kat' autou hamartōloi asebeis*), "concerning all the hard things that ungodly sinners spoke against him." The adjective σκληρός (*sklēros*) means "hard, harsh, rough." In context it means defiant, insolent speech against God.

1 Enoch returns to this theme repeatedly. Immediately after the prophecy, it accuses sinners: "ye have turned away and spoken proud and hard words / With your impure mouths against His greatness" (1 Enoch 5:4). It speaks of a valley of judgment for those "who utter with their lips against the Lord unseemly words and of His glory speak hard things" (27:2), and it charges, "ye spake proud and insolent words against His righteousness" (101:3).[^c8-5]

The emphasis on speech connects the quotation with Jude's charges. The intruders "speak evil of glorious [ones]" (v. 8) and of "whatever [things] they have not known" (v. 10). In the next verse Jude will add that "their mouth speaks swollen words" (v. 16), and the apostles foretold "scoffers" (v. 18). Sins of speech run through the letter.

### Comparing Jude 14–15 with 1 Enoch 1:9

Placing Jude's quotation beside R. H. Charles's translation of 1 Enoch 1:9 (which Charles made from the Ethiopic, compared with the Greek) shows both close agreement and some differences:

| Jude 14–15 (LSV) | 1 Enoch 1:9 (trans. Charles) |
|---|---|
| "Behold, the LORD has come" | "And behold! He cometh" |
| "with myriads of His holy ones," | "with ten thousands of ⌈His⌉ holy ones" |
| "to do judgment against all," | "To execute judgement upon all," |
| — | "And to destroy ⌈all⌉ the ungodly:" |
| "and to convict all their impious ones," | "And to convict all flesh" |
| "concerning all their works of impiety that they did impiously," | "Of all the works ⌈of their ungodliness⌉ which they have ungodly committed," |
| "and concerning all the harsh [words] that impious sinners spoke against Him." | "⌈And of all the hard things which⌉ ungodly sinners ⌈have spoken⌉ against Him." |

The differences are of three kinds:

1. **Jude names "the Lord"** where 1 Enoch has "He." This may be Jude's clarification, and it opens the way to applying the prophecy to Christ.
2. **Jude lacks the line "And to destroy all the ungodly,"** found in the Ethiopic and Greek of 1 Enoch.
3. **"All flesh" vs. "the impious."** The NA28 reading, "every soul," is close to 1 Enoch's "all flesh," which is one reason the NA28 editors adopted it.

The Aramaic original of 1 Enoch survives only in fragments from Qumran Cave 4. The complete book survives only in Ge'ez, and a Greek translation of chapters 1–32 survives in a manuscript found at Akhmim in Egypt.[^c8-6] How exactly Jude's wording relates to these versions is debated, and this book does not try to settle it. Earlier commentators already weighed the evidence. Alfred Plummer noted that the Book of Enoch has a counterpart to "the hard things which sinners have spoken against God," and judged this "almost conclusive" against the theory that a later writer copied the prophecy from Jude into Enoch: "If so, why did he not copy it exactly?" The Cambridge commentary judged it "more natural to suppose that he quoted here, as in previous instances, what he thought edifying" than to adopt theories of an independent tradition or a special inspiration.[^c8-7] What is clear is that Jude quotes a form of the text very close to the one we know, adapting it to his purpose.

### Deep dive: The Book of Enoch and the biblical canon

Jude's quotation of Enoch raises one of the most interesting questions in the history of the Bible: what does it mean when a biblical writer quotes a book that is not itself in most Bibles?

#### 1. What is 1 Enoch?

"1 Enoch" (also called "Ethiopic Enoch") is a collection of five main sections, written by Jewish authors at different times between roughly the third century BC and the turn of the era:

| Section | Chapters | Content | Approximate date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Book of the Watchers | 1–36 | Coming judgment; fall of the Watchers; Enoch's journeys | 3rd century BC or earlier |
| Book of Parables (Similitudes) | 37–71 | Visions of the "Son of Man" / "Elect One" and judgment | 1st century BC–1st century AD (debated) |
| Astronomical Book | 72–82 | Movements of sun, moon, and stars; calendar | 3rd century BC or earlier |
| Book of Dreams | 83–90 | Visions of the flood and of Israel's history (the "Animal Apocalypse") | 2nd century BC |
| Epistle of Enoch | 91–108 | Exhortations, woes, the "Apocalypse of Weeks" | 2nd century BC |

The quotation in Jude comes from the opening of the Book of the Watchers, one of the oldest parts.[^c8-8]

**How the text survives.** Aramaic fragments of most parts of 1 Enoch were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls in Qumran Cave 4 (manuscripts known as 4Q201–4Q212). The oldest are dated by their handwriting to the early second century BC. The Book of Parables is not represented at Qumran, which is one reason it is often dated later. A Greek manuscript of chapters 1–32, found at Akhmim (ancient Panopolis) in Egypt and published in 1892, preserves the Greek translation, and other Greek fragments survive. The complete book survives only in **Ge'ez**, the classical language of Ethiopia, in which it has been transmitted as part of the Bible.[^c8-9]

**Canonical status today.** 1 Enoch is part of the Old Testament in the **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** and the **Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church**. It is used by the Beta Israel (Ethiopian Jewish) community. It is not part of the canon of Rabbinic Judaism or of other Christian churches, whether Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox outside Ethiopia and Eritrea, or Protestant.[^c8-10]

#### 2. How did early Christians respond to Jude's quotation?

The ancient church took up the question directly, and its answers diverged:

- **Tertullian** (c. 200) argued that the Book of Enoch should be accepted. He acknowledged that "the Scripture of Enoch … is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either," but he insisted that "Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude."[^c8-11] For Tertullian, Jude's quotation proved Enoch's authority.
- **Jerome** (late fourth century) reported the opposite effect: "because in it he quotes from the apocryphal Book of Enoch it is rejected by many. Nevertheless by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures."[^c8-12] For some, Jude's quotation of Enoch counted against Jude.
- **Augustine** (early fifth century) took a middle position: "We cannot deny that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, left some divine writings, for this is asserted by the Apostle Jude in his canonical epistle. But it is not without reason that these writings have no place in that canon of Scripture which was preserved in the temple of the Hebrew people." He explained that such books are excluded because of their "obscure origin" and because "though there is some truth in these apocryphal writings, yet they contain so many false statements."[^c8-13]
- **John Calvin** (sixteenth century) suggested that Jude might not have taken the prophecy from a book at all: "I rather think that this prophecy was unwritten, than that it was taken from an apocryphal book; for it may have been delivered down by memory to posterity by the ancients."[^c8-14]

#### 3. How can Jude's use be understood?

Modern interpreters generally take one of the following views. They are presented here with their strengths and weaknesses.

**(a) Jude regarded 1 Enoch as Scripture.** On this view, Jude, like some Jews of his time (the Qumran community preserved many copies of Enoch), treated 1 Enoch as authoritative revelation.

- *Strength:* Jude calls the passage prophecy and uses it as decisive evidence.
- *Weakness:* Jude does not use the formulas by which New Testament writers usually introduce Scripture ("it is written," "the Scripture says"). And the book was not, as far as we know, part of the scriptures generally recognized in the synagogues.

**(b) Jude regarded this prophecy as a genuine word of Enoch, without endorsing the whole book.** On this view, Jude believed that the historical Enoch spoke these words, which had been preserved in tradition and recorded in the Enochic literature, without implying that everything in 1 Enoch was inspired.

- *Strength:* This view takes Jude's "Enoch … prophesied" seriously and matches Augustine's position.
- *Weakness:* Modern historical study regards 1 Enoch as written many centuries after any historical Enoch. Those who hold this view must either dispute that conclusion or argue that the book preserves an ancient tradition. Both are possible but hard to demonstrate.

**(c) Jude quoted a respected text that his readers knew, as true in what it says, without making a claim about its authorship or canonical status.** On this view, "prophesied" means that the passage speaks truly about the future judgment, as indeed the Old Testament does in many places (Deut 33:2; Zech 14:5; Dan 7:10). Jude quotes it because it was familiar and authoritative *to his readers*, and possibly to the intruders themselves, if they appealed to visionary literature.

- *Strength:* The New Testament elsewhere quotes non-biblical sources for true statements without implying their inspiration. Paul quotes a Greek poet at Athens: "as certain of your poets have also said: For we are also His offspring" (Acts 17:28, LSV). Writing to Titus, he even calls the Cretan poet Epimenides "a prophet of their own" before quoting him and adding, "This testimony is true" (Titus 1:12–13, LSV). He quotes a proverb found in the Greek playwright Menander: "evil communications corrupt good manners" (1 Cor 15:33, LSV). Second Timothy names Moses' opponents "Jannes and Jambres" (2 Tim 3:8, LSV), names known from Jewish tradition and not from the Old Testament.
- *Weakness:* Jude's language is stronger than these parallels. He says Enoch "prophesied," and he treats the prophecy as applying directly to the intruders.

#### 4. Principles worth holding

Whatever view one takes, several principles help readers think clearly:

1. **Quotation is not canonization.** That a biblical author quotes a book does not make that book Scripture. Paul's quotations of pagan poets do not make those poets canonical. The question of Jude's view of Enoch must be decided on its own evidence.
2. **The quoted statement can be true without endorsing everything in the source.** Jude affirms that the Lord will come with his holy ones in judgment, a truth taught throughout Scripture. Nothing in his quotation requires readers to accept the astronomical calendar of 1 Enoch 72–82 or every detail of its angelology.
3. **Jude's own authority does not depend on the answer.** The church accepted Jude into its canon in full knowledge that Jude quoted Enoch, as Tertullian, Jerome, and Augustine show. Christians who accept Jude as Scripture can hold different views of 1 Enoch.
4. **The study of 1 Enoch illuminates the New Testament.** Whatever its canonical status, 1 Enoch helps modern readers understand ideas that were current in Jude's world: the Watchers, the judgment of fallen angels, the "Son of Man," and the coming of God with holy myriads. Reading it is no threat to faith. It is a way of hearing the New Testament in its own historical accent.
5. **Respect for other traditions.** For Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Christians, 1 Enoch is Scripture. Other Christians can respect that tradition while explaining their own canon, and can recognize that the boundaries of the canon were not settled in the same way or at the same time everywhere.

**On "prophesied."** The verb προφητεύω has a range of uses in the New Testament: foretelling (Matt 15:7), speaking God's message (1 Cor 14:1–5), and even mocking demands to "prophesy" (Mark 14:65). In Jude, it clearly means that Enoch's words convey a true message about the future judgment of the ungodly. Whether it also implies a judgment about the book's authorship and authority is the question discussed in the deep dive.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Enoch traditions in Judaism.** Enoch fascinated Jewish writers of the Second Temple period. Apart from 1 Enoch, he appears in *Jubilees*, in the Dead Sea Scrolls, and in later works such as *2 Enoch* (preserved in Slavonic) and *3 Enoch* (Hebrew). He was celebrated as a scribe, a sage, a recipient of heavenly secrets, and a witness to the coming judgment. Jude's readers, who knew the Watcher story (v. 6) and probably the nature images of 1 Enoch (vv. 12–13), evidently regarded Enoch as a significant voice.

**Fluid boundaries.** In Jude's day, the boundaries of the Jewish scriptures were not settled in every detail, especially at the margins of the "Writings." The Qumran community preserved many copies of 1 Enoch and Jubilees alongside biblical books. Other Jewish groups, and later the rabbinic tradition, did not treat these books as Scripture. Early Christians inherited this situation, and their own canonical lists took several centuries to stabilize. Jude's quotation should be read against that background, not anachronistically against later definitions.

**Prophecy as present-tense interpretation.** Jewish interpreters of the period frequently read ancient prophecies as referring to their own time. The Dead Sea Scrolls' commentaries (the *pesharim*) quote a prophetic text and then explain its "interpretation" in terms of the community's experience. Jude's "Enoch … prophesied to these" is the same kind of reading.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Genesis 5:21–24; Hebrews 11:5 (Enoch).** The biblical basis for Enoch's reputation. Hebrews celebrates his faith and translation.

**Deuteronomy 33:2 (source of the imagery).** The coming of the LORD "[with] myriads of holy ones" at Sinai is the ultimate source of the picture in 1 Enoch 1:9 and Jude 14.

**Zechariah 14:5; Daniel 7:10 (shared theme).** The coming of God with his holy ones to judge.

**Matthew 25:31; Mark 8:38; 1 Thessalonians 3:13; 2 Thessalonians 1:7 (shared theme).** The return of Jesus with his angels. If Jude understands "the Lord" in the quotation to be Jesus, these texts are his closest New Testament parallels.

**Acts 17:28; Titus 1:12–13; 1 Corinthians 15:33; 2 Timothy 3:8 (method).** New Testament quotations of, and allusions to, non-biblical sources. These help frame the question of what Jude's quotation implies.

**John 16:8 (vocabulary).** The Spirit "will convict" (ἐλέγξει, *elenxei*) the world, the same verb as Jude's "to convict."

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. Did Jude regard 1 Enoch as Scripture?** Discussed in the deep dive. Interpreters remain divided. The most cautious conclusion is that Jude regarded this prophecy as a true word of God about judgment, and that his readers recognized it as authoritative. The question of what Jude thought about the book as a whole cannot be answered from his single quotation.

**2. Who is "the Lord" in v. 14?** God in 1 Enoch. Most likely Jesus in Jude's application, given Jude's usage elsewhere and the New Testament's expectation of Christ's coming with his angels.

**3. Which text did Jude use?** Probably a Greek or Aramaic form of 1 Enoch close to the ones we know, perhaps with his own adaptations. The exact relationship is debated.

**4. Which reading is original in v. 15?** "All the impious" (SBLGNT), "all their impious ones" (Byzantine), or "every soul" (NA28). The meaning is the same in substance.

**5. Does Jude's use of Enoch undermine his authority?** Some early Christians thought so. The church as a whole concluded otherwise. Modern readers who share the church's acceptance of Jude can read his quotation as an example of how the Spirit used the literature and traditions of the time to speak a true word, without thereby endorsing everything in that literature.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

The Enoch quotation is the centerpiece of Jude's case. It supplies the clearest example of the judgment "written beforehand" (v. 4), and its fourfold "impious" confirms that Jude's description of the intruders was the language of prophecy. It is followed by another application ("These are…," v. 16), which applies the prophecy's charge of "harsh words" to the intruders' grumbling and arrogant speech.

The quotation also provides the letter's main picture of the future: the Lord coming with his holy ones. The readers, by contrast, are told to wait for "the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v. 21) and are promised that God will set them "in the presence of His glory unblemished" (v. 24). The same coming that brings judgment on the impious brings mercy and joy to those who have been kept.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**Reading Scripture with its world.** Jude's quotation of Enoch shows that the New Testament was written in conversation with the literature and traditions of its time. Modern readers may find their understanding enriched, not threatened, by learning about that world: the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Jewish writings of the Second Temple period, and the history of the canon.

**Humility about the canon.** Christians today hold different views about which books belong in the Old Testament (Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, and Ethiopian canons differ). Jude's quotation of Enoch, and the varied responses of Tertullian, Jerome, Augustine, and Calvin, may encourage both conviction about one's own tradition and charity toward others.

**Words matter to God.** Enoch's prophecy convicts sinners not only of their deeds but of "the harsh [words]" they have spoken against God. Jude's letter is full of concern about speech: slander, harsh words, grumbling, boasting, scoffing. In an age of easy and often harsh public speech, the passage may prompt reflection on accountability for words.

**The coming of the Lord.** The picture of the Lord coming with countless holy ones is both a warning and a comfort. It warns that injustice and ungodliness will not have the last word. It comforts those who wait for "the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v. 21).

1. How did you react to learning that Jude quotes a book not found in most Bibles? Did it raise questions for you, and how would you begin to answer them?

3. Tertullian, Jerome, Augustine, and Calvin each responded differently to Jude's quotation. Which response do you find most persuasive, and why?

5. How does the picture of the Lord coming "with myriads of His holy ones" shape your hope and your sense of responsibility?
