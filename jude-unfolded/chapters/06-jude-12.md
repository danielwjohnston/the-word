## B. Plain-English Orientation

After the woe of v. 11, Jude turns to the intruders again with "These are…" and describes them in a cascade of images. This verse holds the first three; v. 13 adds two more.

1. At the community's **love feasts**, its shared meals, the intruders are hidden dangers, like reefs beneath the surface (or, on another reading, stains on a garment). They eat with the community without reverence, and they care only for themselves, like shepherds who feed themselves instead of the flock.
2. They are **clouds without water**, promising rain that never comes, blown along by the wind.
3. They are **trees in late autumn**, the season of harvest, yet without fruit. They are dead twice over, pulled up by the roots.

Each image describes a gap between appearance and reality. The meal looks like fellowship but hides danger. The cloud looks like rain but delivers nothing. The tree looks like a fruit tree but bears no fruit and has no life. The intruders look like members, leaders, and teachers of the community, but they are empty and dangerous.

This chapter spends extra time on the first phrase, because the "love feast" opens a window onto the inner life of the earliest Christian communities.

## C. Phrase-by-Phrase Exploration

### οὗτοί εἰσιν (*houtoi eisin*) — ‘these are’

Οὗτοί εἰσιν (*Houtoi eisin*), "These are," introduces the second application in Jude's pattern of text and interpretation. The woe of v. 11, with its three biblical types, is now applied to the intruders' present behavior. The same formula opens vv. 16 and 19.

The Greek reads οὗτοί εἰσιν οἱ ἐν ταῖς ἀγάπαις ὑμῶν σπιλάδες συνευωχούμενοι (*houtoi eisin hoi en tais agapais hymōn spilades syneuōchoumenoi*). The article οἱ (*hoi*) is masculine, while σπιλάδες (*spilades*) is feminine. The article therefore goes with the masculine participle "feasting together," giving the sense "These are the ones who feast with you, [like] reefs at your love feasts." The Byzantine text omits the article.[^c6-1]

### οἱ ἐν ταῖς ἀγάπαις ὑμῶν σπιλάδες (*hoi en tais agapais hymōn spilades*) — ‘the hidden reefs (or stains) at your love feasts’

The two key words here, "stains" and "love-feasts," are each explored in a deep dive below.

**σπιλάδες (*spilades*).** The LSV's "stains" follows one ancient understanding of the word. The more common meaning in Greek literature is "reefs" or "rocks in the sea," and most modern translations adopt that sense (BSB: "hidden reefs"; WEB: "hidden rocky reefs"; ASV: "hidden rocks"). Webster, following the older English Bibles, has "spots" (WBS). See the deep dive "Hidden reefs versus stains."

**ἀγάπαις (*agapais*).** The plural of ἀγάπη (*agapē*), "love," here means "love feasts," the community's shared meals. This is the earliest surviving use of the word in this sense.[^c6-2] See the deep dive "Agape and the ancient Christian love feast."

The pronoun "your" (ὑμῶν, *hymōn*) matters. The love feasts belong to the readers. The intruders are guests at a table that is not theirs, though they behave as if it were.

### συνευωχούμενοι ἀφόβως (*syneuōchoumenoi aphobōs*) — ‘feasting with you without reverence’

The participle συνευωχούμενοι (*syneuōchoumenoi*), "feasting together," comes from a verb meaning "to entertain sumptuously, to feast," with a prefix meaning "with." It occurs in the New Testament only here and in the parallel at 2 Pet 2:13.[^c6-3] The word does not in itself imply excess, but it suggests a festive, abundant meal.

The adverb ἀφόβως (*aphobōs*), "without fear," can be read with what precedes ("feasting with you without fear") or with what follows ("without fear shepherding themselves"). Either way, it describes an absence of reverence. Elsewhere in the New Testament the word can be positive, meaning "without fear" of human threats (Luke 1:74; Phil 1:14). Here it is negative. The intruders have no fear of God. They eat at the Lord's table as if nothing sacred were happening and no judgment were possible. Paul had warned the Corinthians that eating the Lord's meal in an unworthy manner brings judgment (1 Cor 11:27–34).

### ἑαυτοὺς ποιμαίνοντες (*heautous poimainontes*) — ‘shepherding themselves’

The phrase ἑαυτοὺς ποιμαίνοντες (*heautous poimainontes*), "shepherding themselves" or "feeding themselves," alludes to Ezekiel's indictment of Israel's leaders: "Woe [to] the shepherds of Israel, / Who have been feeding themselves! Do the shepherds not feed the flock? You eat the fat, and you put on the wool, / You slaughter the fed one, / You do not feed the flock" (Ezek 34:2–3, LSV). The Septuagint's wording is close to Jude's: "the shepherds fed themselves" (ἐβόσκησαν οἱ ποιμένες ἑαυτούς, *eboskēsan hoi poimenes heautous*, Ezek 34:8).[^c6-4]

The image implies that the intruders held, or claimed, positions of leadership, as teachers or as hosts at the meals. Instead of using their position to nourish others, they used it to nourish themselves. At a meal where food is literally shared, the image is also concrete. See the deep dive "Shepherding as a biblical metaphor."

### νεφέλαι ἄνυδροι ὑπὸ ἀνέμων παραφερόμεναι (*nephelai anydroi hypo anemōn parapheromenai*) — ‘waterless clouds carried along by winds’

The second image moves from the community's table to the sky. νεφέλαι ἄνυδροι (*nephelai anydroi*), "clouds without water," and ὑπὸ ἀνέμων παραφερόμεναι (*hypo anemōn parapheromenai*), "carried along by winds." The verb παραφέρω (*parapherō*) means "to carry aside, carry away." Hebrews uses it in a warning: "Do not be carried away with strange and manifold teachings" (Heb 13:9, LSV).[^c6-5]

The image recalls Proverbs: "Clouds and wind without rain, / [Is] a man boasting himself in a false gift" (Prov 25:14, LSV). It describes people who promise much and deliver nothing. See the deep dive "Clouds without water."

### δένδρα φθινοπωρινὰ ἄκαρπα (*dendra phthinopōrina akarpa*) — ‘fruitless trees in late autumn’

The third image is δένδρα φθινοπωρινὰ ἄκαρπα (*dendra phthinopōrina akarpa*), "late-autumn trees without fruit." The adjective φθινοπωρινός (*phthinopōrinos*), unique to Jude in the New Testament, derives from φθινόπωρον (*phthinopōron*), "late autumn," the end of the harvest season.[^c6-6] A tree that has no fruit at harvest time is a failure. Jesus' cursing of the fig tree comes to mind: "He came to it, and found nothing in it except leaves only" (Matt 21:19, LSV). See the deep dive "Trees twice dead and uprooted."

### δὶς ἀποθανόντα ἐκριζωθέντα (*dis apothanonta ekrizōthenta*) — ‘twice dead, uprooted’

The trees are δὶς ἀποθανόντα (*dis apothanonta*), "twice dead," and ἐκριζωθέντα (*ekrizōthenta*), "uprooted." A fruitless tree might be thought merely dormant. These trees are dead, and dead beyond any hope of revival, because their roots have been torn out of the ground. "Twice dead" may mean (a) dead in two senses, fruitless and uprooted; (b) dead now and destined for the "second death" of final judgment (Rev 2:11; 20:14); or (c) as Clement of Alexandria suggested, "once … when they sinned by transgressing, and a second time when delivered up to punishment."[^c6-7] Jesus' saying lies close by: "Every plant that My heavenly Father did not plant will be rooted up" (Matt 15:13, LSV), using the same verb.

### Deep dive: Agape and the ancient Christian love feast

What exactly was a love feast? This is one of the questions Jude raises most sharply for modern readers, and the evidence allows a fairly full answer, provided we distinguish carefully between what is documented, what is probable, and what belongs to later centuries.

#### 1. Meals at the heart of early Christian life

From the beginning, Christian communities gathered around tables. Luke describes the Jerusalem believers "continuing steadfastly in the teaching of the apostles, and the fellowship, and the breaking of the bread, and the prayers" and "breaking bread at every house, they were partaking of food in gladness and simplicity of heart" (Acts 2:42, 46, LSV). At Troas, "on the first [day] of the weeks, the disciples having been gathered together to break bread," Paul taught until midnight, and later, "having broken bread, and having tasted," he continued talking "until daylight" (Acts 20:7, 11, LSV).

Christians met in homes, and the main gathering included a real meal. That was natural in the ancient world. Jewish households marked Sabbaths and festivals with meals, Jesus' ministry was remembered as a ministry of meals (Mark 2:15–17; Luke 15:1–2), and his last supper with his disciples took place at a meal. Greek and Roman social life also revolved around the communal dinner (*deipnon* in Greek, *cena* in Latin), whether in a household, a trade guild, a religious society, or a funeral association.

#### 2. The Lord's Supper within a meal (1 Corinthians 11)

Our earliest detailed evidence comes from Paul's first letter to Corinth, written in the mid-50s. The Corinthian Christians gathered for what Paul calls "the LORD's Dinner" (κυριακὸν δεῖπνον, *kyriakon deipnon*, 1 Cor 11:20, LSV). It was clearly a full meal, because Paul complains that "each takes his own dinner before in the eating, and one is hungry, and another is drunk" (11:21, LSV). Wealthier members, apparently arriving early or eating separately, consumed their own food while poorer members, perhaps slaves or laborers who arrived late, went hungry. Paul is indignant: "do you despise the Assembly of God, and shame those not having?" (11:22, LSV).

Within this meal, the bread and cup were shared in remembrance of Jesus, and Paul recalls the words of institution (11:23–26). His remedy is not to abolish the meal but to reform it: "so then, my brothers, coming together to eat, wait for one another; and if anyone is hungry, let him eat at home, that you may not come together to judgment" (11:33–34, LSV).

Paul does not call the Corinthian meal an "agape." The situation he describes is the kind of setting in which the word arose: a shared community meal, including the Lord's Supper, meant to express the unity of the body ("we all partake of the one bread," 1 Cor 10:17, LSV), and liable to be spoiled by selfishness and division.

The Corinthian problem has a well-documented cultural background. Roman dinners often reflected social hierarchy, with better food and seating for the honored and worse for the rest. Pliny the Younger describes with disgust a dinner at which the host served "choice dishes to himself and a few, cheap and scraps to the rest," and divided the wine into three grades, one for himself and his guests of rank, one for "lesser friends," and one for freedmen. Pliny himself says he serves everyone alike, "for I invite them to dinner, not to be graded."[^c6-8] The Corinthian church seems to have absorbed such customs, and Paul insisted that the Lord's table must not mirror the world's rankings.

#### 3. "Your love-feasts" (Jude 12)

Jude is the first writer known to call these community meals ἀγάπαι (*agapai*), "loves," meaning "love feasts." He does so casually, as if the term were already familiar to his readers. The name expressed the meal's meaning: it was a practical enactment of the love that bound believers together, rich and poor, Jew and Gentile, at one table.

The parallel in 2 Pet 2:13 shows how the term was understood and transmitted. The best-attested text of 2 Peter reads ἐν ταῖς ἀπάταις αὐτῶν (*en tais apatais autōn*), "in their deceits," and the LSV translates "reveling in their deceits, feasting with you." Some manuscripts read ἀγάπαις, "love feasts," as in Jude.[^c6-9] The two Greek words differ by only two letters (ΑΓΑΠΑΙΣ, ΑΠΑΤΑΙΣ), and the variant shows that early scribes connected the passages.

What did a love feast include? Jude does not describe one, but from the New Testament evidence and the sources that follow we can infer that it probably included a shared meal, prayers of thanksgiving, the Lord's Supper (at least in Jude's period), teaching and exhortation, and perhaps singing. It was the occasion at which traveling teachers would be welcomed and heard. That explains why the presence of the intruders at the love feasts was so dangerous: the meal was the setting for teaching as well as fellowship.

#### 4. Second-century evidence

Several sources from the generations after Jude fill out the picture. They come from different regions and should not be blended into a single description, but together they show the love feast as an established Christian institution.

- **Ignatius of Antioch** (c. 110), writing to the church at Smyrna, says: "It is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast" (ἀγάπην ποιεῖν, *agapēn poiein*).[^c6-10] This is the earliest use of the term after Jude, and it shows the agape as a recognized church gathering under the oversight of leaders. In the same passage, Ignatius speaks of the Eucharist separately, so it is unclear whether he regarded the two as one event or as distinct.
- **The *Didache*** (late first or early second century) gives eucharistic prayers for the cup and the bread and then a thanksgiving to be said "after you are filled."[^c6-11] That suggests a real meal surrounding the prayers. It also warns that "every prophet who orders a meal in the Spirit eats not from it, except indeed he be a false prophet,"[^c6-12] a striking parallel to Jude's intruders feasting at the community's table.
- **Pliny the Younger** (c. 112), Roman governor of Bithynia, reported to the emperor Trajan what he had learned from former Christians under interrogation. They said they used to meet on a fixed day before dawn, sing a hymn "to Christ as to a god," and bind themselves by oath not to commit crimes. "When this was done, it was their custom to depart and to assemble again to take food—ordinary, however, and harmless food." They had stopped doing even this after Pliny's edict banning associations (*hetaeriae*).[^c6-13] Pliny's "ordinary and harmless" answers popular rumors that Christian meals involved cannibalism or immorality. His account is the earliest non-Christian description of a Christian meal, and it shows two separate gatherings: worship at dawn and a meal later in the day.
- **Tertullian** (c. 197), defending Christianity to Roman officials, describes the love feast in detail: "Our feast explains itself by its name. The Greeks call it agapè, i.e., affection." Its cost is gain, "since with the good things of the feast we benefit the needy." "The participants, before reclining, taste first of prayer to God. As much is eaten as satisfies the cravings of hunger; as much is drunk as befits the chaste." After washing and lighting the lamps, "each is asked to stand forth and sing, as he can, a hymn to God." "As the feast commenced with prayer, so with prayer it is closed."[^c6-14] Tertullian presents the agape as a charitable, orderly meal in which the poor were fed.
- **Clement of Alexandria** (c. 200) complains about people who "dare to apply the name agape, to pitiful suppers, redolent of savour and sauces," and insists that "the supper is made for love, but the supper is not love (agape); only a proof of mutual and reciprocal kindly feeling."[^c6-15] The problem Jude addressed, the love feast corrupted by self-indulgence, was still alive more than a century later.

#### 5. What we can and cannot say

**Documented:** Early Christians gathered for shared meals. In Corinth in the 50s, the Lord's Supper was celebrated within such a meal. By the time of Jude, at least some communities called these meals "love feasts." In the second century, the love feast was an established practice, associated with prayer, charity to the poor, and the oversight of church leaders.

**Probable:** In Jude's time, the love feast and the Lord's Supper were not yet clearly separated, and the bread and cup were shared in the context of the meal. The love feast was an occasion for teaching and for receiving visitors.

**Later development:** Over the following centuries, the Eucharist and the agape gradually separated in most churches. The Eucharist became a liturgical rite, often celebrated in the morning, and the agape survived for a time as a charitable meal before largely disappearing or being restricted. Some modern Christian groups have revived love feasts in various forms, among them Moravians, Methodists, Church of the Brethren and related Brethren communities, and others. Those revivals are modern practices and should not be read back into Jude.

**Speculative and to be avoided:** Detailed reconstructions of a fixed "order of service" for first-century love feasts, or claims that every church observed them in the same way, go beyond the evidence.

#### 6. Why the love feast mattered to Jude

The love feast was where the community's life was most visible: rich and poor eating together, believers giving thanks together, the body of Christ made concrete around a table. For intruders to "feast" there "without fear" while "shepherding themselves" was a profound violation. They turned the table of love into an occasion for self-indulgence. Because the meal was also the setting for teaching, they turned it into a platform for their influence. That is why Jude's image for them at the love feast is so stark. They are not merely blemishes but hidden reefs on which the community might founder.

### Deep dive: Hidden reefs versus stains

The word σπιλάδες (*spilades*) has two possible senses, and the choice affects the image.

**"Reefs" or "hidden rocks."** In Greek literature, σπιλάς (*spilas*) normally means "a rock or reef over which the sea dashes." It is used in this sense by the poets, by the historian Polybius, and by Josephus.[^c6-16] Sailors dreaded such rocks because they lay just below the surface, invisible in calm water and fatal to an unwary ship. Most modern translations adopt this sense (BSB: "hidden reefs"; WEB and ASV: "hidden rocks").

**"Stains" or "spots."** In later Greek, σπιλάς was sometimes used as an equivalent of σπίλος (*spilos*), "a spot, stain."[^c6-17] The parallel in 2 Pet 2:13 has σπίλοι καὶ μῶμοι (*spiloi kai mōmoi*), "spots and blemishes" (LSV), and the same word family appears in Jude 23 ("the coat having been stained," ἐσπιλωμένον, *espilōmenon*). Webster ("spots," WBS) and the LSV ("stains") follow this sense.

**Weighing the options.**

- In favor of "reefs": it is the established meaning of σπιλάς. It fits the danger Jude describes, a hidden hazard that can wreck the community. It also prepares for the sea imagery of v. 13 ("wild waves of a sea"). Paul uses a related image when he speaks of those who "made shipwreck concerning the faith" (1 Tim 1:19, LSV).
- In favor of "stains": it matches 2 Peter's interpretation, which may be the earliest commentary on Jude, and the stained garment of v. 23. Abbott-Smith's lexicon notes that the stain sense is attested in late writers and cites J. B. Mayor's discussion.
- A third possibility: Jude may intend a deliberate double meaning, playing on the sound of σπιλάς and σπίλος. In that case the intruders are both hidden reefs and stains.

On balance, "hidden reefs" is the more probable primary sense, and this book follows it. The LSV's "stains" is a defensible rendering with ancient support, and readers of the LSV should be aware of the alternative. Either way, the point is that the intruders' presence at the love feasts is not harmless. Whether as unseen hazards or as defiling blemishes, they endanger the community's life.

### Deep dive: Shepherding as a biblical metaphor

In the ancient Near East, kings and gods were often called shepherds of their people. Israel's scriptures take up the image. The LORD is the shepherd of Israel (Ps 23; 80:1), and its leaders are under-shepherds, accountable to him. Moses and David were shepherds before they were leaders.

Ezekiel 34 is the classic indictment of bad shepherds. They feed themselves rather than the flock, take the wool and the fat, fail to strengthen the weak, heal the sick, or seek the lost, and have "ruled them with might and with rigor" (Ezek 34:4, LSV). The result is a scattered flock, prey to wild beasts. God's response is to promise that he himself will shepherd his sheep, and that he will set over them "one shepherd," "My servant David" (Ezek 34:23).

The New Testament sees that promise fulfilled in Jesus: "I AM the good shepherd; the good shepherd lays His life down for the sheep" (John 10:11, LSV). Jesus contrasts himself with "the hired worker," who flees when the wolf comes "because he is a hired worker, and is not caring for the sheep" (John 10:12–13, LSV). The apostles applied the image to church leaders. Peter says, "feed the flock of God that [is] among you, overseeing not by compulsion, but willingly, neither for shameful gain, but eagerly, neither as exercising lordship over the heritages, but becoming patterns for the flock" (1 Pet 5:2–3, LSV). Paul tells the Ephesian elders to "feed the Assembly of God" and warns that "there will enter in, after my departing, grievous wolves to you, not sparing the flock" (Acts 20:28–29, LSV).

Jude's phrase "shepherding themselves" places the intruders in the line of Ezekiel's false shepherds and Jesus' hired hands. They exercise leadership, but for their own benefit. At the love feast, where the shepherd's role was literally to see that all were fed, their self-feeding was both a spiritual failure and a visible one.

### Deep dive: Clouds without water

In the land of Israel, life depended on the rains. The rainy season ran from about October to April, with the "early rain" in autumn softening the ground for plowing and sowing and the "latter rain" in spring bringing the crops to maturity. Joel promised: "He … causes a shower to come down to you, / Early rain and spring rain [as] in the beginning" (Joel 2:23, LSV). Rain clouds came from the west, off the Mediterranean. Jesus remarked, "When you may see the cloud rising from the west, immediately you say, A shower comes, and it is so" (Luke 12:54, LSV). Elijah's servant saw "a little thickness as the palm of a man … coming up out of the sea," and soon "the heavens have become black—thick clouds and wind—and the shower is great" (1 Kgs 18:44–45, LSV).

A cloud that rolled in from the sea, darkened the sky, raised the farmer's hopes, and then drifted past without dropping rain was a bitter disappointment. Proverbs uses exactly this image for the empty promiser: "Clouds and wind without rain, / [Is] a man boasting himself in a false gift" (Prov 25:14, LSV). Hosea uses a related image for Israel's fleeting loyalty: "Your goodness [is] as a cloud of the morning, / And as dew rising early—going" (Hos 6:4, LSV).

Jude's "waterless clouds, being carried away by winds" combines the two ideas of empty promise and instability. The intruders promise spiritual refreshment, perhaps through their claimed revelations, but provide none. They are also driven by every wind. Ephesians speaks of believers who are "carried around by every wind of the teaching" (Eph 4:14, LSV). The intruders are not merely victims of such winds. They are clouds moved by them, with nothing of their own to give.

There may also be an echo of 1 Enoch, where the obedience of nature contrasts with the disobedience of sinners, and where in the days of sinners "the rain shall be kept back / And the heaven shall withhold (it)" (1 Enoch 80:2, trans. Charles).[^c6-18] That connection is explored in Chapter 7.

### Deep dive: Trees twice dead and uprooted

The fruit tree is one of Scripture's favorite images for a life before God. The righteous person "has been as a tree, / Planted by streams of water, / That gives its fruit in its season" (Ps 1:3, LSV). John the Baptist warned that "every tree not bearing good fruit is cut down, and is cast into fire" (Matt 3:10, LSV), and Jesus taught that "every good tree yields good fruits, but the bad tree yields evil fruits" (Matt 7:17, LSV). In his parable of the fig tree, the owner says, "three years I come seeking fruit in this fig tree, and do not find [it], cut it off" (Luke 13:7, LSV).

Jude's description is cumulative, and each term deepens the judgment:

1. **"Autumnal"** (φθινοπωρινά). The trees are seen in late autumn, at the end of the harvest season, when fruit is expected. Abbott-Smith explains the phrase as "autumn trees (said to be without fruit therefore at a time when fruit might be expected)," and refers to J. B. Mayor's "elaborate note" on the word.[^c6-19] Some interpreters think instead of trees in late autumn after the fruit is gone and the leaves have fallen, bare and lifeless-looking. The first sense fits the next word better.
2. **"Without fruit"** (ἄκαρπα). At the very season of fruit, they have none. The intruders bear no fruit of the Spirit (cf. v. 19, "not having the Spirit").
3. **"Having died twice"** (δὶς ἀποθανόντα). A fruitless tree may still be alive. These are dead, and "twice" dead. The phrase may mean dead in two ways, barren and rootless; dead now and destined for the "second death" (Rev 20:14); or, as Clement read it, dead first in sin and then in judgment. The language of a "second death" was current in Jewish as well as Christian circles, and the allusion may be intended.
4. **"Having been uprooted"** (ἐκριζωθέντα). The final image is of trees torn up by the roots, beyond any hope of recovery. "Every plant that My heavenly Father did not plant will be rooted up" (Matt 15:13, LSV); "God breaks you down forever, / … And He has uprooted you / Out of the land of the living" (Ps 52:5, LSV).

The sequence moves from disappointment to death to destruction. Like the cloud, the tree holds out a promise that it cannot keep. Unlike the cloud, which drifts on, the tree's end is fixed: uprooted.

**A caution on "agape."** Because the meal was called "agape," some teachers have treated the love feast as proof that the early church understood *agapē* as a technical term for a uniquely Christian kind of love. The word's use for the meal shows how central love was to Christian identity. It does not show that the word *agapē* by itself carried a special meaning. Its significance came from the love of God in Christ that the meal celebrated.

## E. The Historical and Cultural World

**Banquets and associations.** In the Greco-Roman world, voluntary associations (trade guilds, religious societies, burial clubs) met regularly for common meals, often with rules about seating, contributions, and behavior. Roman authorities were sometimes suspicious of such associations as potential centers of political unrest. Pliny's ban on *hetaeriae* in Bithynia, which led Christians there to stop their common meal, shows that Christian meals could be seen as one more association's dinner.[^c6-20] Within this culture, Christian love feasts stood out because they brought together people of different classes and backgrounds and because they fed the poor.

**Hospitality to traveling teachers.** As noted in Chapter 2, early Christian communities welcomed traveling apostles and prophets, who would naturally be invited to the community's meals. The *Didache*'s warning about prophets who order meals "in the Spirit" and eat from them suggests that some visitors exploited this hospitality.[^c6-21]

**The agricultural year.** Jude's readers lived close to the land. Hegesippus's account of Jude's grandsons as farmers suggests that Jude's own family did too. Images of clouds and rain, fruit trees and harvest, were part of daily experience, and the failure of either meant hardship.

## F. Connections Across Scripture

**Ezekiel 34 (allusion).** "Shepherding themselves" closely echoes Ezek 34:2, 8. This is probably a deliberate allusion.

**Proverbs 25:14 (probable allusion).** "Clouds and wind without rain" is the nearest Old Testament parallel to Jude's waterless clouds, and the shared context of empty boasting strengthens the connection. As noted in the Introduction, the Septuagint version of this proverb differs from the Hebrew, so Jude's image matches the Hebrew more closely.

**Matthew 15:13; Luke 13:6–9; Matthew 21:19 (shared imagery).** Jesus' sayings about uprooted plants and fruitless trees share Jude's imagery and moral logic.

**1 Corinthians 11:17–34 (background).** Paul's correction of the Corinthian meal is the best evidence for the setting in which love feasts were abused.

**2 Peter 2:13, 17 (literary parallel).** Peter has "spots and blemishes, reveling in their deceits, feasting with you" and "wells without water, and clouds driven by a storm" (LSV). His version supports the "stains" interpretation of σπιλάδες and shows that the cloud image was a fixed part of the tradition.

**John 10:11–13; 1 Peter 5:2–3; Acts 20:28–29 (shared theme).** These texts give the positive model of shepherding against which Jude's intruders are measured.

## G. Scholarly and Theological Questions

**1. "Reefs" or "stains"?** Discussed in the deep dive. Most modern scholars favor "reefs," while noting the support for "stains" in 2 Peter and in later Greek usage.

**2. Were the love feast and the Lord's Supper the same event in Jude's day?** Probably they were not yet separated, but the evidence is indirect. In 1 Corinthians the Lord's Supper occurs within a meal. In Ignatius and the *Didache*, the relationship is ambiguous. In Pliny, the meal appears to be separate from the dawn worship. Practices likely varied between regions and changed over time. Christian traditions today differ in how they understand the relationship of meal, fellowship, and sacrament, and readers may bring those differences to this verse. Jude's own concern is the conduct of people at the community's table.

**3. Were the intruders leaders?** "Shepherding themselves" suggests that they held or claimed some leadership role. They may have been traveling teachers, local leaders who had adopted their teaching, or hosts in whose homes the meals took place. Jude does not specify.

**4. What does "twice dead" mean?** See the deep dive. The options are not mutually exclusive.

## H. The Passage Within Jude

Verse 12 begins the longest application in the letter (vv. 12–13), which interprets the woe of v. 11 in five images. The images move through the natural world: the sea (reefs), the sky (clouds), the earth (trees), and in v. 13 the sea again (waves) and the heavens (stars). As Chapter 7 will show, this sweep through creation probably reflects 1 Enoch's contrast between the obedience of nature and the disobedience of sinners.

The love feast is the most concrete detail in Jude's description of the intruders. It shows that they were not distant heretics but people sitting at the same table as the readers, which makes Jude's later call to mercy and rescue (vv. 22–23) all the more pointed. The danger is not "out there," and neither are the people who need rescue.

## I. Contemporary Understanding

*Suggested applications, distinct from the original meaning.*

**The table as a test of community.** For the early Christians, the shared meal was where love was enacted or betrayed. Communities today might ask what their tables, whether in the Lord's Supper, fellowship meals, or hospitality, reveal about their love. Do they mirror the world's rankings, as at Corinth, or overcome them? Who is fed, and who goes hungry?

**Leadership that feeds others.** Jude's charge, "shepherding themselves," remains a searching test for anyone with influence. Leaders can ask whether their position mainly serves others or serves themselves, and communities can ask whether their structures reward self-serving leadership.

**Promises and fruit.** The waterless clouds and fruitless trees invite reflection on the difference between appearance and substance, between impressive promises and actual fruit. Jesus' teaching that a tree is known by its fruit (Matt 7:16–20) gives believers a way to evaluate teachers and movements, and themselves.

**Hidden dangers in familiar places.** Hidden reefs are dangerous because they are unseen in calm water. Communities may need to recognize that the gravest dangers often arise within, at the very places where trust is greatest. This awareness should lead to wisdom and care, not paranoia.

## J. Reflection and Discussion

1. What did you know about "love feasts" before reading this chapter? What surprised you most about the early Christian practice of shared meals?
2. Paul, Jude, and Clement of Alexandria all address abuses of the community meal. Why do you think shared meals are so vulnerable to selfishness and division? Where do you see similar dynamics today?
3. Does the image of "hidden reefs" or of "stains" seem more fitting to you? How does each image shape your understanding of the danger Jude describes?
4. Jude's images of clouds without rain and trees without fruit describe a gap between promise and delivery. Where do you see this gap in religious life today, and how can it be recognized?
5. If the love feast was where Christian love became visible, what practices in your community serve a similar purpose? How could they be strengthened?
